#3153. How tech became the villain of the US midterms

Datacenters, AI and distaste for the billionaires developing it has soured Americans on big tech

How will Trump’s mass deportation agenda shape the US midterm elections? Our reporters answer your questions

The campaign ad begins with a clear, idyllic stream that suddenly turns sludge-brown as two concrete datacenters slam on to its banks. Another facility thuds down on a peaceful suburban park, and two more crush-green pastures as an ominous soundtrack to a disaster movie blares.

“They’re coming for our rivers. Our neighborhoods. Our farmland,” a deep-voiced narrator says. “Because Brent Taylor wrote the law allowing datacenters to take over Tennessee.”

The attack ad, paid for by the Democrat-aligned Three Star Pac and targeting a Tennessee Republican congressional candidate, is one of more than 150 midterm election campaign commercials across the US focused on datacenters or the artificial intelligence industry. At debates and at rallies, in mailers and online ads, the burgeoning AI industry has taken center stage, as both Republican and Democratic candidates try to convince voters that their opponents will put the unpopular facilities in voters’ backyards.

And it’s not only datacenters – the public anxiety over AI and distaste for the billionaires developing it, as well as backlash over encroaching tech such as surveillance cameras, has turned the tech industry into a ubiquitous villain this election cycle. As with big pharma before it, candidates are straining to distance themselves from big tech.

The stigma of the tech industry has even forced prominent Republicans to break with Donald Trump’s pro-AI party line, with candidates like Ken Paxton, campaigning for US Senate in Texas, calling for a slowdown. In Tennessee, Taylor’s campaign ran its own television spot that claimed he was tough on tech and datacenters.

The campaign ads focused on tech have sprung up alongside an ecosystem of pro- and anti-AI political action committees, which have collectively spent hundreds of millions of dollars to influence elections and back candidates. The breathtaking amount of spending has pushed tech into the forefront of the midterms, even as polls show issues like the economy, democracy and healthcare still rate as more important among voters.

Super Pacs funded by tech billionaires are among the top spenders in this election cycle. Leading the Future, formed by the OpenAI president, Greg Brockman, and venture capitalist Marc Andreessen, has raised more than $75m to back tech-friendly candidates. Elon Musk has sunk $366m into Republican causes, more than he spent on the 2024 presidential election. AI money has also been funneled into pro-regulation Pacs seeking to counteract the influence of deregulation groups.

Including Musk’s contribution to various Republican-aligned Pacs, the tech industry and its leaders have spent more than half a billion dollars to influence elections and ballot proposals in 2026.

The cumulative effect of such vast spending, lobbying and advertising is that tech issues and the tech industry have become far more pervasive in the midterms than previously seen in US elections, marking the first major test of how American voters will react to the AI boom.

Concern over AI’s potential effect on society has grown over the last year, and the backlash has been most intense where there is a physical target for voters’ ire. None of those targets have loomed larger than the sprawling datacenter facilities that are part of the tech industry’s multitrillion-dollar infrastructure build-out. Although campaigns have nodded to AI regulation and fears over the technology’s risks, it is datacenters that candidates have repeatedly and specifically called out as a real, tangible threat to constituents.

“Datacenters are now a campaign question in nearly every race in the nation,” said Brad Carson, the head of a pro-AI-regulation group Public First Action and a former congressman.

The growing animosity toward datacenters has also been reflected in nationwide protests and polling. A Pew Research poll from last month showed that the percentage of US adults who believe that datacenters are bad for the environment, energy costs and quality of life has risen across demographics since January. Only 4% of respondents believe the facilities will be good for the environment or home energy costs.

Datacenters have become toxic enough that some candidates have even begun using the facilities as an epithet to tar their rivals.

“Data Center Derek gave the billionaires and greedy corporations a $2bn tax break. Driving up our electric bills, destroying Ohio farms,” declared a Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee attack ad against the Ohio Republican candidate Derek Merrin.

At least 20 candidates for governor across the US are calling for moratoriums on datacenters, according to the policy tracking firm MultiState, while fewer than 10 candidates across 36 gubernatorial races are backing the projects without any calls for increased regulations.

Along with datacenters, the public backlash against AI-enabled Flock surveillance cameras that automatically scan license plates has also bled into campaigns, including in key battleground races. In Michigan, the Democratic US Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed ran a Flock-focused ad that accused his Republican opponent Mike Rogers of pushing a surveillance state by backing the cameras. In Texas, Democrat James Talarico has similarly spoken out against the company and its data collection.

“Flock cameras have no place in Texas,” Talarico said last month at a rally. The state’s Republican governor, Greg Abbott, surprisingly agrees with him, blocking state agencies from spending on Flock cameras in August.

Elsewhere, legislation has targeted not just tech infrastructure but the billionaires behind it. In California, voters are considering a ballot measure that would levy a one-time 5% wealth tax on any resident of the state worth more than $1bn. The state’s ultra-rich have feverishly mobilized against it, raising more than $340m and spending more than $1m a day to fight the measure. Google co-founder Sergey Brin alone has contributed at least $102m to oppose the tax.

The collective backlash against the tech industry has put its leaders in a bind. Companies are facing internal and external criticism of their products and politics, but accepting more intense government regulation or halting datacenter construction would threaten their growth.

The pushback has caused a shift in at least one company’s calculus – OpenAI has distanced itself from Leading the Future in recent months, and Brockman backed out of a planned $25m donation to the Pac.

Musk, Brin and other tech moguls, however, have doubled down on their efforts to swing votes their way.

“They have never met a problem that they didn’t think they could solve by throwing more money at it,” said Charles Blaettler, political director of the pro-AI-regulation Pac Guardrails Alliance. “They’re realizing there’s an intense backlash to their ideas.”

The political landscape of the tech industry is vastly different from the last time the US held midterm elections. Anthropic, now potentially set to go public at a more than $2tn valuation, was just one year old in 2022. OpenAI had not publicly released ChatGPT. Musk had not yet allied himself with Donald Trump’s campaign.

The tech industry has become increasingly entrenched in government and determined to shape regulation in the period since. Tech leaders have simultaneously built up immense campaign finance and lobbying arms that have come into full force in this year’s midterms.

Meanwhile, Americans feel negatively about how AI will affect a range of issues from personal relationships to the economy to elections, multiple polls show, as well as broadly opposing datacenters in their communities. More Americans oppose a datacenter being built in their area than oppose a nuclear power plant, according to a Gallup poll from earlier this year.

The public distaste for the AI industry also extends to its leaders, with polls showing that the US generally views executives such as Musk, the OpenAI CEO, Sam Altman, and the Anthropic, CEO Dario Amodei, as untrustworthy and unlikable.

“There’s this very deep-seated sense of anxiety people have about the direction this technology is taking and who’s in control of it,” Blaettler said. “People just feel like they don’t have any agency in their lives any more.”

The enormous spending from the tech industry has largely flowed to Republicans, but it has also inspired a new category of pro-AI-regulation Pacs that have formed to counter groups like Leading the Future. Some of these pro-regulation Pacs have received money, counterintuitively, from AI companies and workers with different views on how government should oversee the technology. Anthropic contributed more than $40m to Carson’s Public First Action, while the Guardrails Alliance and its associated non-profit have raised more than $11.5m.

One of the early tests for this type of battle was in the Democratic primary for New York’s 12th congressional district in June, where pro- and anti-AI groups, including Carson’s, cumulatively spent $24m on the race – largely targeting candidate Alex Bores, who was running on a pro-regulation platform. The result of all the spending was hard to determine, with Bores coming second in a crowded field and losing to similarly pro-regulation candidate Micah Lasher. After the vote, Lasher sought to distance himself from both Anthropic and OpenAI’s spending.

“I have some news for the two big AI companies who’ve taken such an unusual interest in who won this congressional seat,” Lasher said in June. “I won’t be taking my cues from either of you when it comes to protecting our kids, our jobs, our environment.”

While the spending frenzy on pro- and anti-AI campaigns has helped fund some of the fights over tech in the midterms, leaders of pro-regulation Pacs say these are issues that voters already organically care about and are mobilizing around. The message from the AI industry has for years been that their products will usher in a utopian era of abundance and affluence, but this election cycle has shown there is deep skepticism from everyday voters that they will benefit from the boom.

“It’s now a world where most people on the street have a view about AI,” Carson said. “And it’s mostly negative.”

#3154. Tech firms should pay the creators whose work they use | Letter

It could raise up to £300m a year, providing UK creators with additional income, write Barbara Hayes, Tayyiba Nasser, Christian Zimmermann and Paul Seheult

Anthropic’s proposal (AI firm Anthropic to commit 5% of shares to fund for ‘public good’, 19 October) raises an important question: who should benefit from technological progress? Before pledging money for the public good, AI companies such as Anthropic should first pay the creators whose work they use.

Device manufacturers have a separate responsibility under the same principle: creators should share in the value their work generates. Smartphones, computers and other devices enable creative work to be copied and stored, yet UK creators receive no private-copying payments, unlike their counterparts in more than 40 other countries.

The proposed Smart Fund would require manufacturers and importers to make modest contributions on devices used to copy or store creative work. It could raise up to £300m a year, providing creators with additional income and supporting skills, cultural programmes and affordable workspaces.

For creators on persistently low and falling incomes, these payments would make a tangible difference. Visual artists’ median annual earnings are just £12,500 – 47% lower in real terms than in 2010. Authors earn a median of only £7,000 from writing, down from £11,300, according to surveys published in 2022 and 2018 respectively. Audiovisual performers report median annual earnings from performing of £17,500. Yet it is creators’ work that attracts people to technology, sustains their engagement and drives innovation. Those creators should share in the value their work helps generate, so they can continue creating and powering that cycle. The government should turn this principle into action by introducing the Smart Fund. The windfall would probably also add to the flurry of private investments and philanthropic donations from tech-savvy entrepreneurs who are eager to jump-start their favoured areas of biomedical research, such as cancer biology, longevity and reproductive technologies. “It’s a very exciting time,” says Andrew Lo, who studies entrepreneurship at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in Cambridge. “We’re going to see the creative energy of the tech sector unlocked.”

Wealthy technology entrepreneurs have long put their cash and creative energy into medical research. And as their fortunes have grown, so has the scale of their investments, and the variety of ways in which their funds are doled out. One major player is Altos Labs, a longevity-focused company in Redwood City, California, which launched in 2022 with $3 billion in funds raised from tech titans Yuri Milner and Jeff Bezos, among others — an unprecedented sum for a fledgling biotechnology company with no drug candidates. The Chan Zuckerberg Initiative (CZI) , founded by Facebook co-founder Mark Zuckerberg and his wife, physician Priscilla Chan, has spent about $4 billion on basic scientific research and other causes, and plans to spend up to $10 billion more over the next decade.

Ultra-wealthy research investors follow a different strategy than do government funding agencies such as the US National Institutes of Health (NIH). Organizations such as the CZI and Altos Labs boast a commitment to high-risk, long-term research. Tech entrepreneurs are also often keen to invest in new technology, and to harness the power of AI for biomedicine. In April, for example, the CZI announced that it would spend $500 million on funding predictive AI models of human cells. Last year, OpenAI Foundation, the non-profit arm of OpenAI in San Francisco, California, pledged to spend $25 billion on health, disease cures and AI resilience. The foundation has announced more than $100 million for Alzheimer’s disease research and another $125 million for life-sciences data projects this year.

But this investment comes at a time when the public is suspicious of the tech elite and its political influence. It is difficult to assess the success of philanthropic programmes, and many are geared towards encouraging the use of the same technology the philanthropists profited from developing. Meanwhile, high-profile private investment in firms that study longevity or controversial reproductive technologies, such as gene editing in human embryos, raises questions about whether tech entrepreneurs will skew research towards applications that benefit the wealthy, rather than helping a broader swathe of society. “In philanthropy, no one gets elected,” says Rob Reich, a political scientist at Stanford University in California. “There’s no way of holding them accountable.”

US philanthropists have a long history of influencing what research gets done, and how. In the early twentieth century, grants from industrialists such as Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller helped to transform higher education and solidify the role of US universities in scientific research. Their giving focused on ensuring that the institutions they supported succeeded; today’s philanthropists are more likely to seek a particular outcome rather than support the long-term health of a university, says Eric John Abrahamson, a historian at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, Maryland. In 2024, non-profit organizations, including philanthropies, supported an estimated $25 billion in research and development — about 11% of total research funding in the United States, according to the Science Philanthropy Alliance (see ‘Support sources’).

It is difficult to track all donations from technology magnates comprehensively, but individual examples show that their influence has been sizeable. In 2000, Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates established the Gates Foundation (formerly called the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation), which made massive investments in vaccine development and the study of infectious disease, among other fields. It remains an influential behemoth: last year, Gates announced that he would donate about US$200 billion over the next two decades.

Other large tech donors include Sergey Brin, a co-founder of Google, who has donated more than $1 billion to Parkinson’s disease research. Sean Parker, an early executive at Facebook, has given hundreds of millions to cancer-immunotherapy research. In 2021, several philanthropists, including tech entrepreneurs, donated $650 million to found the Arc Institute in Palo Alto, California, in the heart of Silicon Valley, to understand the root causes of complex diseases. And US software magnate Larry Ellison announced that he will spend billions of pounds over ten years on a new campus near the University of Oxford, UK. The campus will focus on health care, as well as agriculture, climate change and AI.

In addition to charitable donations, tech entrepreneurs have been fuelling biomedical innovation through private-sector investments, such as funding Altos Labs. Another company, Calico Life Sciences, was founded in 2013 with backing from Google. With a focus on combating ageing and curing disease, Calico, in South San Francisco, California, was conceived as a long-term ‘moonshot’ programme. Sam Altman, a co-founder and chief executive of OpenAI, has invested about $180 million in Retro Biosciences, another longevity-focused company, in Redwood City, California.

In some cases, the line between for-profit investments and philanthropic donations are becoming more blurred, says Louis Shekhtman, a network scientist at Bar-Ilan University in Ramat Gan, Israel. The CZI, for example, was set up as a limited liability company (LLC), rather than as a tax-exempt foundation. Doing so comes at a cost: depending on how they are constructed, LLCs might not be eligible for the tax breaks that more-conventional charities enjoy. But an LLC can be more flexible in how it spends its money, and has fewer requirements to publicly disclose its activities, says Joan Heminway, a business-law scholar at the University of Tennessee in Knoxville.

In a 2015 statement shortly after the launch of the CZI, Zuckerberg said the LLC structure would give the initiative “the flexibility to give to the organizations that do the best work — regardless of how they’re structured”. He also said that any net profits from CZI investments would be put towards furthering its philanthropic mission.

And US tech entrepreneurs have a penchant for experimentation in how they structure their investments, says Keith Yamamoto, vice-chancellor of science policy and strategy at the University of California, San Francisco. When it comes to philanthropy, private donors are more able to take risks with their investments than are government funding agencies, which are beholden to taxpayers to ensure that their money is spent wisely. “Public money limits your activities,” says Harold Varmus, a former director of the NIH, who has also been on advisory boards for the Gates Foundation and the CZI. “There is a freedom of motion that is associated with private money.”

#3160. The World Adds a Third Terawatt of Solar Power

In March, the world passed a major energy milestone, adding its third terawatt of solar power. Increasingly, poorer countries are leading the shift to solar, analysts say.

It took nearly seven decades, after the invention of the silicon solar cell in 1954, for the world to install 1 terawatt of solar capacity, conceivably enough to power the entire United States. After passing that milestone in 2022, the world added its second terawatt in 2024 and its third in 2026, Bloomberg reports.

BloombergNEF expects installations will continue apace, with the world hitting 9 terawatts of solar power by 2036. Analysts project that, by that date, poor countries will have installed more solar power than rich countries, China notwithstanding.

The first terawatt of solar was installed largely in wealthy nations, as governments subsidized renewable energy, Bloomberg reports. But since then, China has emerged as the global solar leader, installing huge amounts of solar while exporting inexpensive panels across the developing world.

The dynamic is most evident in countries hard hit by energy shocks, such as Pakistan, Nigeria, and Cuba. In the face of soaring fossil fuel prices, homeowners have begun snapping up imported solar panels.

The global solar boom is now spurring a push to build out transmission lines that can ferry energy from where it is produced to where it is needed, and fueling demand for batteries that can store up surplus power. Countries such as Pakistan, where energy shocks have spurred a solar buildout, have seen a surge in battery sales.

China, which is responsible for more than half of all solar installations globally, doubled its battery storage capacity last year, according to data from energy think tank Ember. And in its latest Five-Year Plan, it set a goal to ramp up the use of renewable energy by deploying yet more storage.

#3162. The Guardian view on Oxford Street’s pedestrianisation: cities should be designed around people, not cars | Editorial

The transformation of Europe’s busiest shopping street should be a case study in making cities greener, less congested and more inviting places to live

Sadiq Khan has been trying to pedestrianise Oxford Street since he first became the mayor of London in 2016. On Monday, after numerous setbacks, his plan will finally come to fruition. The busiest stretch of the nation’s famous shopping street will be closed to traffic in a scheme designed to improve the area and make it more attractive to pedestrians. It should be an emblematic case study in making cities greener, less congested, more inviting places to live and visit.

The Greater London Authority hopes it will rejuvenate a high street fallen on hard times. Since the pandemic, the number of vacant shops on Oxford Street has more than quadrupled. It estimates that the scheme will generate £82m of value a year in the area by increasing footfall and encouraging visitors to linger. This impact remains to be seen, but research from other cities suggests that shops experience higher sales once an area is pedestrianised. Given the prime minister’s focus on rejuvenating British high streets, whose dilapidation is correlated with support for Reform UK, he should be watching with interest.

The benefits of pedestrianisation are far greater than increased shopping. Domestic transport remains the largest contributor to greenhouse-gas emissions in Britain. This sector’s emissions have fallen more slowly than others largely because too many people still rely on cars. Mr Khan’s city is an outlier: 59% of all journeys in Britain were made by car in 2024, while in London cars accounted for 34%. This is hardly surprising when the Treasury spends twice as much on London’s transport system as it does on other regions in England, and the capital’s bus fares are cheaper. In Leeds, which has no mass-transit system, nearly half of people drive to work. Car use cannot be reduced without simultaneously improving public transport.

The critics of pedestrianisation – including Westminster council – suggest it merely displaces traffic elsewhere. But the evidence on this is more nuanced. One study from the University of the West of England suggests the closure of strategic roads causes some traffic to disappear permanently, as people switch to public transport or walk. Low-traffic neighbourhoods (LTNs), the pedestrianisation schemes that were rushed through in the capital during Covid-19 and have since been introduced in Manchester and Birmingham, were fiercely criticised for causing a buildup of road traffic elsewhere. Yet one study of 46 LTNs in London suggested their impact on traffic elsewhere was minimal.

Car-free areas should be accompanied by other policies that reduce pollution on surrounding roads, such as the low-emissions zones introduced in London and Oxford. When the catapult arm is pushed down, it stores potential energy, which is transferred to the ball as kinetic energy when the arm is released.

As the ball flies higher, it starts to slow down. The ball’s kinetic energy is temporarily converted to potential energy as it gains height. As the ball starts to fall, it speeds up again, and the stored potential energy is turned back into kinetic energy. Gravity acting on the ball gives it a curved path through the air.

The amount of stored energy – the further the catapult arm is pushed down, the more elastic potential energy is stored, so the ball flies further.

The angle of the launch – for maximum horizontal distance of flight, the launch angle should be about 45 degrees.

The weight of the ball—lighter projectiles usually fly further than heavier balls when the same amount of force is used, as less force is needed to accelerate them.

Flexibility of the sticks and elastic bands – more flexible materials can store more energy.

Many of the new philanthropists are adopting an engineering mindset, focusing their donations on technological approaches to solving societal problems. As Nature reports in a News feature, they are also bringing many of their own ideas about how this research should be prioritized, funded and executed.

These donors speak of high-risk, long-term and interdisciplinary research. Their goals include finding ways to make research faster and more efficient by encouraging data sharing and speeding up grant approvals, with solutions that include incorporating AI into the processes.

But there’s a disparity when you compare how these funders and those in the public sector operate. Like much of the research that takes place in companies, the results of projects funded by philanthropy won’t necessarily be published. Private organizations are not always obliged to collect and report data on how well their projects have worked — by publishing in peer-reviewed journals, for example — or to make other details about their grants public. At Nature, we think they should be more transparent. Transparency in science funding is a component of transparency in science itself: a non-negotiable principle. Research that is not transparent cannot be verified or built on by others, who might then improve it or find new applications.

An increasing number of examples of science philanthropy can be found around the world. In China, tech entrepreneurs have launched awards such as the Xplorer Prize for young researchers, which is funded mainly by the company Tencent in Shenzhen. New philanthropic foundations are emerging, including the Beijing-based Xiaomi Foundation, which supports universities and provides grants to teachers and researchers. And in Brazil, the Serrapilheira Institute in Rio de Janeiro was established in 2017 by private donors to fund fundamental research, which is usually supported by governments.

But it is in the United States that philanthropists have one of the longest histories of shaping the direction and structure of the scientific enterprise, from the industrialists of the early twentieth century to today’s tech entrepreneurs.

In 2015, Facebook founder Mark Zuckerberg and his wife, paediatrician Priscilla Chan, pledged to donate 99% of Facebook shares, then valued at US$45 billion, to the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative. One of the initiative’s ambitions is to build interdisciplinary research infrastructure to allow the study of complex biology, to take research beyond the study of individual diseases. By 2025, Bill Gates and Melinda French Gates had donated nearly $64 billion of their wealth to the Gates Foundation (formerly the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation) in Seattle, Washington, which is focused on projects to improve public health and alleviate poverty in some 140 countries.

These and newer donors are keen to fill some of the perceived gaps in publicly funded research. Some, such as those financing the Arc Institute in Palo Alto, California, have created funding schemes to allow investigators to rapidly change direction in response to research results — something that is difficult, if not impossible, to do with conventional grants. Others, including former Google chief executive Eric Schmidt, are investing in technologies to speed up research. The Astera Institute, co-founded by cryptocurrency entrepreneur Jed McCaleb and based in the San Francisco Bay Area, is focused on using AI and developing infrastructure to tackle what it describes as “neglected ideas that aren’t supported well through existing institutions”.

Philanthropic foundations do not have the same reporting requirements as government funding agencies, which must publicly document their investments and the outcomes of their spending choices. It can be argued that freedom from such requirements is liberating. Without the fear of being called before lawmakers to explain the value of specific research projects, philanthropists are free to be more adventurous with their money. They can invest in long-term and high-risk projects. And with fewer bureaucratic obligations, charities can be better positioned to pivot rapidly when new technologies emerge, and to bridge disciplinary divisions to assemble teams with diverse expertise.

That said, science supported by philanthropy still relies on a foundation of public funding. Privately funded science and technology builds on published findings, many of which have been achieved through research funded by the public purse (M. Mazzucato and G. Semieniuk Oxford Rev. Econ. Philanthropists should also bear in mind that in science, the line between an idea and a result is rarely straight or predictable. Some of the work, such as the use of gene editing in mosquitoes to control malaria, is paying off. But it has taken time. A decade after the launch of the challenges, Gates acknowledged that he had been “pretty naive” about how quickly the objectives could be achieved — a salutary lesson for his successors.

Without greater transparency about the results of philanthropic grant-making, however, there is no way of knowing what approaches have been successful or which might be instructive for guiding future science funding.

#3178. Abandoned Study of Australian Ash Trees Challenges Case for Clearcutting

A landmark study of the towering ash trees that grow in the mountains of Victoria, Australia, which was launched in the 1980s, boosted arguments for clearcutting the iconic trees. But when scientists returned to the site years later, they came to a much different conclusion.

Australian conservationists have long contended that clearcutting imperiled koalas and other native wildlife. Foresters, on the other hand, argued that mountain ash trees, which can grow taller than 300 feet and live for more than 400 years, were adapted to catastrophic bushfires that cleared the forest, leaving nothing behind. They pointed to stands of ash trees that were essentially uniform in age — evidence that the forest had been leveled and then regrew — and said that by clearcutting ash and burning any detritus that remained, they were recreating the effects of the most intense fires.

To settle the argument between conservationists and foresters, scientists launched a landmark experiment in Victoria’s Central Highlands in 1987. They compared large clearcuts, which could be more than 1,000 feet wide; small clearcuts, some less than 200 feet across; and plots that had been heavily thinned, with at least half of trees removed.

By the early 2000s, it was clear that mountain ash regrew best in the footprints of large clearcuts, giving credence to the foresters’ argument. In the smaller clearcuts and thinned plots, the study found, shorter acacia trees dominated. The results were published and the experiment abandoned.

However, when scientists returned to the site in 2014, they noticed something surprising: While mountain ash were abundant in the clearcuts, both large and small, in the thinned plots, ash trees had grown at a startling pace.

Monitoring showed that, with few competitors, the biggest trees in the thinned plots were 20 percent larger in diameter than trees in areas that had never been harvested. They also stored at least as much carbon, meaning that, in just a few decades, the surviving ash in the thinned plots had recovered all the carbon lost to logging, and then some. The findings were recently published in Forest Science.

The study authors say that bigger trees are more resistant to fire and more likely to form the hollows where greater gliders and Leadbeater’s possums make their homes. Thinning, they say, offers a way to supply timber while also preserving vital woodlands and their stores of carbon.

“Our findings show this for mountain ash, and other studies in North America have shown this for conifer forests,” they said, writing in The Conversation. “It is a win-win-win outcome that challenges the false dichotomy of conservation versus production that has dominated public debate for decades.”

#3186. ‘Wastelands’ No More: India Charts a Future for Its Grasslands

A wolf looks out over grasslands in the Indian state of Maharashtra. Mihir Godbole / The Grasslands Trust

Since the colonial era, India has regarded its native savannas as little more than degraded forests. Now, amid a growing recognition of rangelands globally, officials are working to map the nation’s ailing grasslands, with a view to restoring these vital ecosystems.

By Vaishnavi Chandrashekhar • July 30, 2026

It’s almost noon, and the sun is high over Gulunche, a village in the western Indian state of Maharashtra that is surrounded by sugarcane fields, grassy pastures, and short trees. The landscape appears verdant, but it belies an ecological precarity. The sugarcane guzzles water in the semi-arid terrain; a dominant tree species is a South American mesquite, introduced decades ago to fight desertification and provide firewood; and the grass itself has been overrun by a single species that provides poor fodder for the wild herbivores and sheep that depend on these pastures.

Standing on the edge of a small nursery off the highway, conservationist Mihir Godbole points to rows of tall green grasses growing at the edge of a trench. The grass is one of several indigenous species thought to have vanished from the land years ago and that his organization, the nonprofit Grasslands Trust, is trying to revive in one corner of this 50-acre site. The blades have survived two summers now without irrigation or fertilizer. “It can grow without a lot of external inputs,” says Godbole. “That tells us that this is the right grass for this region.”

Godbole’s experiment is a small step in an ambitious effort to restore the region’s native grasslands — part of a new conservation agenda in a country better known for tiger-filled forests than open savannas. Such efforts are showing early signs of success: In the arid northwestern state of Rajasthan, grassland restoration efforts in the Tal Chhapar Wildlife Sanctuary have brought a resurgence of wildlife and birds. In Gujarat’s Banni grasslands, pastoralists have helped remove invasives and restored around 7,000 acres of land.

And in an important policy move, India’s National Board for Wildlife in April directed government agencies to map, for the first time, the extent and biodiversity of these ecosystems nationwide. The findings will eventually be published in an “Atlas of Grasslands,” with a view to creating a national policy framework to protect and manage these lands. “It’s a long overdue recognition,” says Abi Vanak, an advisor to the Grasslands Trust and the director of the Centre for Policy Design at the Ashoka Trust for Research in Ecology and the Environment. “[The move] signals an important shift in government thinking about these ecosystems.”

The shift is not restricted to India. International attention on grasslands and other scrub landscapes has grown in recent years. A key policy moment, says Chetan Kumar, global head of forests and grasslands at the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), was the publication of the United Nations’ 2024 Global Land Outlook report, which highlighted the untapped potential of rangelands and pastoralism to bolster climate resilience, biodiversity, and rural development. This year is also the U.N. International Year of Rangelands and Pastoralists — an attempt, says Susan Gardner, director of the ecosystems division of the U.N. Environment Programme (UNEP), to “recognize rangelands as core infrastructure… and to recognize pastoralists as essential partners in protecting, managing, and restoring them.”

In the northern Great Plains of the United States, the National Audubon Society has restored more than 20,000 acres of grassland through a program that works with farmers and ranchers. In Kazakhstan, the Altyn Dala Conservation Initiative has helped protect around 12 million acres of grassland across the Central Asian steppe, increased the population of native saiga antelope, and reintroduced wild horses. Between 2021 and 2025, China restored an average of about 7.7 million acres of degraded grassland each year.

A herder grazes his sheep in the state of Rajasthan. Sandeep Bisht / Alamy

Savannas, steppes, grasslands, and scrub are open lands marked by grass or shrubby vegetation and sparse tree cover. These ecosystems may look desolate, but they contain extensive root systems that stabilize soil, increase rainwater infiltration, and store carbon long term. These habitats are also vital for migratory birds, native bees and butterflies, and diverse wildlife. Many of these landscapes also function as rangelands, supporting livestock and pastoralist communities. According to the UNEP, these lands cover more than half of Earth’s surface, support 2 billion people, provide 16 percent of global food production, and store up to 30 percent of terrestrial carbon.

Yet their importance has not, until recently, been reflected in policy or investment, says Gardner. Much of this landscape has already been lost to farming, industry, and invasive species. The U.S. has lost more than half its historic prairies, primarily to industrial agriculture. India has seen its grasslands shrink from roughly 110 million acres in 1880 to 60 million in 2010, according to one estimate.

In India, this loss has historical causes. Conservation policy has historically focused on forests, in part due to a legacy of colonialism. British officials in India viewed forests primarily in economic terms, as resources that could be converted to timber plantations and cropland or conserved for game animals. Vanak, of the Ashoka Trust, argues that the British recognized savannas as distinct ecosystems in Africa but not in India partly because India’s large game animals, especially its tigers, were concentrated in forests. Apart from the cheetah, which was hunted to extinction, grassland species like wolves weren’t as valued.

The British classified many grasslands as forests, where grazing was prohibited, while others were labeled as degraded forests or “wastelands,” making them easier to convert to other uses. More recently, says Gardner, farming has shaped assumptions about what “productive” or well-managed land should look like, privileging stationary agriculture over pastoralism.

The wasteland classification persisted in independent India. For a long time, scientists continued to believe India had no natural savannas and that grassy or shrubby areas were merely deforested land. But paleoclimate research has shown otherwise: A 2021 study of lake sediment in western India found evidence that a weakening monsoon 6,000 years ago drove the expansion of savannas in peninsular India.

Misperceptions about baseline conditions have made some areas vulnerable to change. In the 1980s, the government set up a National Wasteland Development Board with the aim of turning these lands to “productive use,” promoting their conversion to farming, plantations, and other uses.

A first among major nations, India is industrializing with solar. Read more.

A 2023 study led by Vanak found that less than 5 percent of India’s semi-arid open natural ecosystems are protected — and that nearly 70 percent of those open systems overlapped with officially designated wastelands. More recently, these seemingly empty landscapes have been targeted for solar and wind power projects — to the detriment of local wildlife and pastoralists — as well as for more tree plantations. In 2024, a new policy opened degraded land, including scrubland and pasture commons, to private plantations for the generation of carbon credits or to fulfill national afforestation goals.

Left to right: An Indian gazelle, Indian wolf, and Indian courser. All three species have been harmed by the loss of grasslands. The Grasslands Trust; Tisha Mukherjee via Wikipedia; Rushikesh Deshmukh via Wikipedia

Conservationists hope that the new effort to map grasslands will help avert further losses. The official Atlas project is still in early stages: Since the April directive, the government-funded Wildlife Institute of India has put together a comprehensive plan, in partnership with two other forest research agencies, to map existing grasslands, document biodiversity, and highlight areas under threat. Based on the findings, agencies will suggest restoration and management strategies, according to a government researcher with knowledge of the project.

State governments are also beginning to shift their approach to land use. In May, the state of Maharashtra issued guidelines for afforestation that recognized grasslands as distinct ecosystems that need locally tailored restoration, rather than blanket tree plantation. “Government attitudes are slowly changing,” says Godbole of the Grasslands Trust. “But things need to move faster on the ground.”

The challenges of conservation are apparent on the newly widened road to Gulunche. The restoration site lies less than 60 miles southeast of Pune, a once-small town that has tripled in size over the past three decades. The semi-arid landscape is a patchwork of privately owned farms, state-owned forest land, and commons, known as “gairans,” which are also state-owned. An irrigation scheme introduced 10 years ago helped expand the farming of sugarcane, a water-intensive cash crop. With an international airport planned in the area, land prices have risen and owners have begun to fence off their plots, blocking the passage of sheep and wild deer.

As Pune’s native habitat has shrunk, so has its wildlife. Some of India’s fastest-declining bird species, according to the IUCN Red List, are scrubland species that don’t perch on trees. Populations of the Indian courser, for example, have declined by more than 70 percent due to habitat loss. Gazelle numbers have also declined, and Indian gray wolves survive in these habitats partly by feeding on waste dumped by poultry farms, says Godbole. One major conservation challenge, notes his colleague and cofounder Vishwatej Pawar, is that “the species is protected but the habitat is not.”

Local and nomadic pastoralists, who graze their flocks of sheep on this open land, have also been affected by habitat loss. Sadashiv Mote, a local shepherd, says he’s reduced his flock because farms, buildings, and fences have taken over grazing land. “Water is also scarcer now,” he says as he herds his roughly 100 sheep toward a patch along the highway where he spotted new grass growing.

While pastoralists have traditional rights on grazing commons — according to one estimate, India has some 13 million practicing pastoralists — the practice has often been viewed by economists as inefficient and by conservationists as environmentally destructive, says the UNEP’s Gardner. But such attitudes are starting to change. Last year, for the very first time, India’s five-year livestock census counted the animals held by nomadic pastoralists, not just animals on farms — a sign of expanding recognition for pastoralists. “The shepherd is as much a part of the grassland landscape as the wolf,” says Godbole.

Locals plant native grasses at a restoration site in the village of Gulunche. The Grasslands Trust

That philosophy underpins the work of the Grasslands Trust, which partners with local communities as well as the forest department. The Gulunche restoration site sits on commons land where, with the consent of the village, grazing is temporarily banned. “We were not using the area much, and we wanted to support a project that could benefit us,” says Samradnee Nigade, the young head of the village council. Those benefits include employment for locals and the production of a high-quality fodder made of fermented native grasses, sugarcane, and corn, as well as biochar made from invasive trees removed from the site. The trust is also figuring out how to revive ancient water courses that may have shrunk due to human activities.

The challenges at Gulunche mirror those of larger restoration projects. Scientists know much less about restoring grasslands than conserving forests — one review found that 89 of the 206 endemic Indian savanna species had been described only in the last two decades. Traditional knowledge is critical for managing many of these landscapes, says the IUCN’s Kumar, but research is especially important in areas changing rapidly due to development and climate change. Unfortunately, conservationists say, funding to run restoration experiments is not nearly as abundant as funding for forests, in part because donors prefer to support projects with highly visible outcomes.

“We need to provide alternative models to afforestation for dryland areas,” Vanak says. It shouldn’t be that hard: As Godbole likes to point out, trees are difficult to grow in dry landscapes. Grassland restoration, he says, “is cheaper and more ecologically responsible.”

#3191. Is a job guarantee the solution to mass AI unemployment?

An old idea springs anew as a chorus of influential voices predicts that AI may trigger crises of vast magnitude

In a preface to his book Capitalism and Freedom, the American economist Milton Friedman famously observed that a crisis can propel once-fringe ideas into the political mainstream.

“Only a crisis – actual or perceived – produces real change. When that crisis occurs, the actions that are taken depend on the ideas that are lying around,” he wrote. The job of intellectuals, thinktanks and economists, he argued, is to refine the quality of these ambient ideas, so that good ones are ready when disaster strikes.

A chorus of influential voices is now predicting that AI may trigger crises of vast magnitude. Bill Gates has declared that AI will advance so rapidly that within a decade, humans will not be needed “for most things”. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has warned that the technology could cause widespread unemployment within one to five years, eliminating many positions in law, tech, finance and other white-collar professions. Even if these predictions are wrong, investors who bet heavily on the technology may conclude that AI is a bubble, and this in itself could cause a recession and widespread job loss.

Whether a crisis of mass AI-related job loss turns out to be actual or perceived, one policy idea that is attracting growing attention and support could directly mitigate this problem: a job guarantee.

In its most ambitious form, such a program would ensure that anyone who wants work is guaranteed a meaningful, decently paid job. By creating a public option for employment, its supporters argue, a job guarantee could protect millions of people from the financial and psychological damage of joblessness and mobilize a vast labor force to tackle urgent societal problems, from infrastructure and climate adaptation to elder care and childcare.

The concept of a job guarantee has a long history, both as an ideal and an actual policy. Article 23 of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights states: “Everyone has the right to work, to free choice of employment, to just and favourable conditions of work and to protection against unemployment.” During the Great Depression in the 1930s, Franklin D Roosevelt established the Works Progress Administration (WPA), which employed millions of Americans in ambitious public works projects that constructed roads, bridges, sewers, playing fields and much more. More recently, France, India and Argentina are among the countries that have launched versions of a job guarantee.

Though a job guarantee and universal basic income (UBI) are often grouped together, a recent global survey found greater support for job guarantees than UBIs. Polling of Americans by the Democratic strategist David Shor also shows the same pattern, with greater support for the creation of well-paid jobs than for direct income support. This makes sense, the venture capitalist and civic activist Nick Hanauer said, because “jobs represent so much more than income in human societies. They represent connection and dignity and enfranchisement and participation.”

Algernon Austin, director for race and economic justice at the Center for Economic and Policy Research, agreed: “A job guarantee is significantly preferable to UBI because work is more than just income. When you have a job, you gain work experience and you gain skills and you gain a network.”

Calculating the cost of a job guarantee depends on a holistic assessment of its impacts. During a US Senate hearing on affordability in June, John Kennedy, a Republican senator for Louisiana, suggested that a job guarantee would cost trillions of dollars and was impossible. When Dr Julie Margetta Morgan, president of the public policy thinktank the Century Foundation, told Kennedy that she supported a federal job guarantee, he replied: “Do you believe in the Easter bunny?”

Economists who study job guarantees see the calculation of costs and benefits differently. Max Kasy, a professor of economics at Oxford who has studied a recent job guarantee experiment in Austria, said that while the wages a job guarantee pays participants comprise a cost, such programs also save money in multiple ways.

“The cost saving comes from the fact that you don’t have to pay unemployment benefits, or maybe disability benefits or early retirement benefits. Then people who are working pay taxes and make social insurance contributions, so that’s additional revenue for the state. And then they hopefully do something that’s actually useful for society,” he said.

Participants in the job guarantee experiment in Austria also experienced social and psychological benefits. While the monetary value of greater wellbeing may be hard to quantify, such intangibles are crucial, according to Pavlina Tcherneva, a professor of economics at Bard College.

“Providing a good job to a person is absolutely transformative,” she said. Factoring in the costs of health problems that come from precarious unemployment and long-term unemployment, she added, makes the policy even more attractive, saying: “It’s not only affordable, it’s an investment.”

In September, the US representative Ayanna Pressley, a Democrat from Massachusetts, reintroduced a congressional resolution calling for the creation of a federal job guarantee, something she first proposed in 2021. Her proposal would create a federally funded but locally administered job guarantee that pays participants at least $25 an hour, with regular adjustments to reflect the rising cost of living. The jobs would include a range of sectors, such as emergency preparedness, relief and recovery after natural disasters, care for children and seniors, and public art projects modelled on the WPA’s popular Federal Art Project.

Another recent proposal would directly tax AI companies to fund a federal job guarantee. The plan, which is supported by Democratic US representatives Greg Casar of Texas, Valerie Foushee of North Carolina and Sara Jacobs of California, would tax large AI companies on whatever was higher: the value of their “tokens” – units of data used in AI models – or their revenue from AI products.

If the unemployment rate rose, the tax rate would increase. “AI CEOs are openly predicting their technology could put millions of Americans out of work, and our government is doing nothing to save jobs and protect working people,” Casar said in a written statement.

In Cleveland, Ohio, a non-profit called Universal Basic Employment (UBE) is launching a small pilot study with a cohort of five workers who drive vans at a transportation company. Rather than create new jobs from scratch, the program subsidizes the wages of existing private-sector jobs in a way that benefits both workers and employers, according to Devin Cotten, founder and CEO of UBE.

Cotten and his team wanted workers to make what they call an autonomous wage – the pay level at which people are no longer eligible for most social safety net benefits. In Cleveland, that was just over $27 per hour. At a cost of almost half a million dollars for one year, UBE’s pilot will split that $27 figure with employers. The drivers currently make about $15 an hour, so the employer will save money, and the workers will get a sizable raise.

The program is not meant to replace other forms of assistance such as housing vouchers, Cotten said. But workers receiving more money as wages, rather than as a siloed benefit, can build their credit, perhaps qualify for a mortgage and also keep dollars circulating in the local economy, helping to revitalize poor neighborhoods.

Such a model may even attract a broader base of political support. “Work is a shared value across political lines,” Cotten said. “I think crafting a solution based on work allows us to be a bipartisan solution.”

Griechenland beruft sich vor allem auf das UN-Seerechtsübereinkommen (UNCLOS). Dessen Artikel 121 besagt, dass bewohnbare Inseln, die "ein eigenes Wirtschaftsleben zulassen", die gleichen Rechte auf eine AWZ besitzen wie das Festland selbst. Dies gelte auch für Kreta, Rhodos oder Lesbos.

Dort wurde diese Auslegung des Seerechts im Prinzip bestätigt - auch wenn bei sich überlappenden Interessen eine gerechte Abgrenzung gefunden werden muss. Diese muss die Küstenverläufe, die geografischen Verhältnisse und die Auswirkungen einer möglichen Grenzziehung berücksichtigen.

Genau darauf wiederum beruft sich die Türkei. Ankara argumentiert, dass die Geografie des Mittelmeers eine Sonderrolle einnimmt: Würde man jeder kleinen griechischen Insel vor der türkischen Küste (und Zypern) eine vollständige AWZ zusprechen, wäre die Türkei größtenteils vom offenen Meereszugang abgeschnitten. Dies müsse bei der Grenzziehung stärker berücksichtigt werden.

Anders als Griechenland hat die Türkei UNCLOS nie unterzeichnet. Aus diesem Grund will sie den Streit auch nicht vor einem UN-Schiedsgericht verhandeln lassen. In der Frage, wie eine "gerechte Aufteilung" in diesem Fall konkret aussehen könnte, sind aber auch internationale Seerechtsexperten uneins.

Als die Türkei das Abkommen 2019 forcierte, herrschte in Libyen noch ein erbitterter Bürgerkrieg. Damals intervenierte die Türkei militärisch im Westen, um die Regierung in Tripolis zu retten, und schlug damit die Offensive des ostlibyschen Generals Khalifa Haftar zurück.

Inzwischen hat sich das Verhältnis jedoch grundlegend gewandelt: Ankara betreibt seit geraumer Zeit eine aktive Charmeoffensive gegenüber dem Haftar-Lager, was zu einer politischen Annäherung führte.

Mittlerweile besitzt Ankara ein gutes Verhältnis zu beiden Teilen Libyens - zum eigenen Vorteil: Neben dem Plan, gemeinsam mit dem nordafrikanischen Land die Energieressourcen im Mittelmeer zu fördern, haben türkische Baukonzerne in den vergangenen Jahren milliardenschwere Aufträge für den Wiederaufbau des kriegszerstörten Landes erhalten. Zudem arbeiten die staatliche türkische Erdölgesellschaft TPAO und die libysche National Oil Corporation bereits an gemeinsamen Plänen für seismische Untersuchungen vor der libyschen Küste.

Außerdem hat Ankara sich als neuer militärischer Partner für Haftars Streitkräfte etabliert, liefert Waffen und bietet Trainingsprogramme an. Damit drängt die Türkei den Einfluss Ägyptens und Russlands in Ostlibyen zurück und stärkt seine eigene Rolle als geopolitischer Player in der Region.

Durch die nun erfolgte Ratifizierung will sich auch der Osten Libyens seinen Anteil an den lukrativen Rohstoffen sichern. Und auch General Haftar erhofft sich dadurch in Zukunft mehr internationales Gewicht.

Die Spannungen im östlichen Mittelmeerraum dürften sich durch die Vereinbarung weiter erhöhen - insbesondere dann, wenn die Türkei und Libyen tatsächlich mit der Förderung der Öl- und Gasvorkommen im umstrittenen Seegebiet beginnen sollten.

Zudem gefährdet das Abkommen ein anderes Großprojekt, das sich derzeit noch in Planung befindet: die EastMed-Pipeline. Sie soll eigentlich in Zukunft Erdgas von Israel und Zypern aus über Griechenland nach Europa transportieren und so auch einen wichtigen Beitrag zur Energiesicherheit in der EU leisten. Der neue türkisch-libysche Korridor durchschneidet jedoch nicht nur den geplanten Verlauf der Pipeline, die Türkei beansprucht zudem das Recht für sich, Kabel- oder Pipeline-Projekte durch diese Zone kontrollieren oder blockieren zu können.

Langfristig könnte es so zu einer zunehmenden Spaltung des östlichen Mittelmeerraums in zwei konkurrierende Energie- und Verteidigungsblöcke kommen: Der Achse Türkei-Libyen stünde womöglich eine Allianz aus Griechenland, Zypern, Ägypten und Israel gegenüber, die auch von der EU unterstützt wird.

Recent breakthroughs in pig-to-human xenotransplantation have successfully prevented hyperacute rejection of kidney, heart, liver and lung xenografts, marking a milestone long considered a critical barrier. However, these major advances also brought new challenges into focus, as immune-mediated graft injuries including antibody-mediated rejection, T cell-mediated rejection and innate immune activation have been observed in most xenotransplantation procedures to date. Detection and management of these injuries remain a critical bottleneck, as conventional histopathology and standard clinical monitoring lack the sensitivity required to identify subclinical injury early enough to guide timely intervention. This diagnostic gap underscores an urgent need for the development of advanced, multimodal precision diagnostic frameworks to enhance immune surveillance, which will be essential for standardizing clinical protocols and enabling pathogenesis-driven therapeutic intervention. This approach will inform both immunosuppressive regimens and future gene editing strategies aimed at engineering rejection-resistant porcine organs, ultimately prolonging graft survival. Broad adoption of such an integrative strategy will be essential to transition xenotransplantation from an experimental intervention into a safe, scalable and patient-centred clinical therapy. In this Review, we synthesize recent progress in xenotransplantation and position multimodal precision diagnostics as a pivotal strategy to address the remaining immunological and clinical barriers to widespread clinical implementation.
