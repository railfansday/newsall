#2453. Eating 30% Less Food Has an Unexpected Effect on DNA, Study Finds

Eating fewer calories may do more than slow certain aspects of aging. Scientists have found that mice consuming 30 percent less food accumulated fewer DNA mutations across several tissues, suggesting that diet can influence how genetic damage builds up over time.

The study, published in Cell, examined mutations across a large portion of the genome rather than focusing on individual genes. The results showed that calorie restriction affected different organs and regions of DNA in surprisingly different ways.

DNA mutations develop throughout life as cells experience genetic damage or make mistakes while copying and repairing their DNA. Most mutations are harmless, but some interfere with normal gene function and can contribute to cancer and other diseases. Although animal studies have established that calorie restriction can extend lifespan and slow certain effects of aging, its broader influence on mutation accumulation has remained unclear.

To investigate, researchers led by NYU Langone Health and University Hospitals Cleveland worked with scientists at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center and the National Institutes of Health. Using highly accurate DNA sequencing technology, they examined genetic changes in mice fed 30 percent fewer calories than animals allowed to eat freely.

The restricted diet reduced several types of mutations, including substitutions, which occur when one DNA letter is replaced by another, and insertions and deletions, which add or remove DNA letters. However, the reductions differed across tissues, with liver cells showing a stronger response than kidney or brain cells.

“While links between diet and lifespan have been established in animals, our results show that diet and mutations across the genome are also linked,” said co-corresponding study author Gilad D. Evrony, MD, PhD, a researcher at NYU Langone Health and associate professor at NYU Grossman School of Medicine. “Mice on a calorie-restricted diet are healthier for longer, but this diet is too challenging for humans. Nevertheless, considering the role mutations play in cancer and many other diseases, it’s exciting to have found a way to decrease mutations, which we can now work to understand.”

The researchers also discovered that calorie restriction did not affect all regions of DNA equally. In liver and kidney cells, the greatest reductions in mutations occurred in the least active portions of the genome. These regions contain either no genes or genes that are not being used by the cells.

One possible explanation involves differences in DNA repair activity. Regions containing actively used genes already undergo frequent DNA repair, which may limit how much additional protection they receive if calorie restriction reduces DNA damage. Less active regions, where repair occurs less frequently, could experience a larger reduction in accumulated mutations. The researchers have not yet established whether this mechanism explains the pattern.

“We didn’t expect to find this asymmetry across the genome,” said co-corresponding author Jonathan Shoag, MD, an associate professor in the Department of Urology at University Hospitals Cleveland and Case Western Reserve University School of Medicine. “By profiling more dietary interventions and mutations in additional tissues and cell types, we hope to learn what drives the connection between diet and mutations.”

The findings establish a connection between calorie restriction and lower mutation levels in mice, but they do not demonstrate that people would experience the same benefits. Sustained, substantial calorie restriction is also difficult to maintain and can carry health risks.

Researchers now hope to identify the biological processes responsible for the reduction in mutations. Understanding those processes could help scientists investigate approaches that protect DNA without requiring people to follow severely restricted diets.

“Understanding these mechanisms could eventually reveal new ways to reduce mutations – without the challenges and risks of an extreme diet—to counter diseases of aging,” said first study author Marta Grońska-Pęski, PhD, a postdoctoral fellow in Evrony’s laboratory.

The research relied on a specially established group of mice used to study the biological effects of calorie restriction. “We were fortunate to have collaborated with Dr. Victoria Acosta-Rodríguez, Dr. Joseph Takahashi, and Dr. Carla Green, who established this mouse caloric restriction cohort,” said Dr. Evrony.

Reference: “Caloric restriction modulates genome-wide somatic mutation in mice” by Marta Grońska-Pęski, Victoria Acosta-Rodríguez, Amoolya Srinivasa, Amanda Garrido, Shany Picciotto, Carla B. Green, Joseph S. Takahashi, Jonathan E. Shoag and Gilad D. Evrony, 9 September 2026, Cell. DOI: 10.1016/j.cell.2026.08.013

This work was supported by National Institutes of Health grants T32AG052909, F32AG076287, and UH3NS132024, the Pew Charitable Trusts, the Jacob Goldfield Foundation, the Damon Runyon Cancer Research Foundation, the Smythe Family Impact Fund, the Vail family, and the Howard Hughes Medical Institute.

#2454. The World of CSS Transforms

Like so many things in CSS, the transform property is surprisingly remarkable.

At first glance, it may seem like a pretty niche thing. How often do we need to rotate or skew something, after all? And yet, the more I learn about transform, the more I find myself taking advantage of it. In my blog's codebase, I've used the transform property more than 800 times!

In this blog post, we're diving deep into the transform property. I'll show you some of the cool and unexpected things you can do with it!

This tutorial is intended to be beginner-friendly, though I do assume that you're already comfortable with the fundamentals of HTML/CSS.

Unless you're a seasoned CSS expert, I bet you'll learn something neat!

The transform property can do a whole bunch of different things, through the use of transform functions like translate and skew.

Translation allows us to move an item around:

We can use translate to shift an item along in either axis: x moves side to side, y moves up and down. Positive values move down and to the right. Negative values move up and to the left.

Critically, the item's in-flow position doesn't change. As far as our layout algorithms are concerned, from Flow to Flexbox to Grid, this property has no effect.

For example: in this visualization, we have 3 children aligned using Flexbox. When we apply a transform to the middle child, the Flexbox algorithm doesn't notice, and keeps the other children in the same place:

This is similar to how top / left / right / bottom work in positioned layout, with relatively-positioned elements.

When we want to move an element along a single axis, we can use translateX and translateY:

There's one thing that makes translate ridiculously powerful, though. Something totally unique in the CSS language.

When we use a percentage value in translate, that percentage refers to the element's own size, not the available space within the parent container.

Setting transform: translateY(-100%) moves the box up by its exact height, no matter what that height is, to the pixel.

This is incredibly handy when we want an element to sit just outside another one:

Alright, let's look at another transform function!

scale allows us to grow or shrink an element:

Scale uses a unitless value that represents a multiple, similar to line-height. scale(2) means that the element should be 2x as big as it would normally be.

We can also pass multiple values, to scale the x and y axis independently:

At first glance, this might seem equivalent to setting width and height, but there's one big difference.

Check out what happens when our element has some text in it:

The text scales up and down with the element. We aren't just transforming the size and shape of the box, we're transforming the entire element and all of its descendants.

This reveals an important truth about transforms: elements are flattened into a texture. All of these transforms essentially treat our element like a flat image, warping and contorting it as you might in Photoshop.

Incidentally, this is what makes transform such a good choice for animations!

Think about how much work is required when we change something like width. All of the layout algorithms need to re-run, figuring out exactly where this element and all of its siblings should be. If the element has text inside, the line-wrapping algorithm needs to figure out if this new width affects the line breaks. Then, the paint algorithms run, figuring out which color every pixel needs to be, and filling it in.

It's fine to do this once when the page loads, but when we animate something, we need to do all of those calculations many many times a second. With transforms, we can skip a bunch of steps. This means that the calculations run quicker, leading to smoother motion.

It may seem like a bummer that scale will stretch/squash the element's contents, but we can actually use this effect to our advantage. For example, check out this old-timey TV power animation:

For this animation, the squashing effect actually improves the effect!

And, if we really don't want our text to squash, we can apply an inverse transform to the child.

This is an advanced technique, far beyond the scope of this blog post, but know that it's possible to use scale to increase an element's size without distorting its children. Libraries like Framer Motion(opens in new tab) take advantage of this fact to build highly-performant animations without stretching or squashing.

You guessed it: rotate will rotate our elements:

We typically use the deg unit for rotation, short for degrees. But there's another handy unit we can use, one which might be easier to reason about:

The turn unit represents how many turns the element should make. 1 turn is equal to 360 degrees.

It's obscure, but well-supported; the turn unit goes all the way back to IE 9!

Finally, skew is a seldom-used but pretty-neat transformation:

As with translate, we can skew along either axis:

Skew can be useful for creating diagonal decorative elements (à la Stripe(opens in new tab)). With the help of calc and some trigonometry, it can also be used on elements without distorting the text! This technique is explored in depth in Nils Binder's awesome blog post, “Create Diagonal Layouts Like It's 2020(opens in new tab)”.

One common gotcha with transforms is that they don't work with inline elements in Flow layout.

Inline elements don't enjoy being jostled. Their goal is to wrap around some content with as little disruption as possible. Transforms aren't their cup of tea.

The easiest fix is to switch it to use display: inline-block, or to use a different layout mode (eg. Flexbox or Grid).

In addition to the 2D transforms we've covered in this tutorial, CSS can transform elements in a third dimension!

#2456. Low-dose aspirin linked to 70% lower dementia risk, but there's a catch

A new analysis from Monash University suggests that low-dose aspirin may be linked to a dramatically lower risk of dementia in older adults with certain genetic characteristics. Researchers found that people with a specific genetic profile who took aspirin had a 70 percent lower relative risk of developing dementia, raising the possibility that genetic testing could eventually help identify people who might benefit from preventive treatments.

The findings, published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association, were based on genetic data from more than 13,500 participants in the landmark ASPREE Trial (Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly). Scientists examined whether differences in participants' genes could help explain why aspirin might affect dementia risk differently from one person to another.

Aspirin Linked to 70% Lower Dementia Risk in Certain People

The most striking results involved genes associated with platelet count. Platelets are small components of the blood that help form clots, and researchers wanted to determine whether genetic variations related to platelet levels could influence aspirin's potential effects on dementia.

After assigning participants genetic scores associated with platelet count, the team compared dementia rates among those who received aspirin and those given a placebo.

Among people in the highest 20 percent of platelet count genetic scores, just 1 percent of aspirin users developed dementia, compared with 3.3 percent of participants taking a placebo. That difference translates to approximately a 70 percent lower relative risk of dementia among those receiving aspirin.

However, the apparent benefit was accompanied by a significant safety concern. In the same genetic group, 4.4 percent of aspirin users experienced major bleeding, compared with 2.1 percent of those receiving a placebo.

A Genetic Clue Could Change Dementia Prevention

Lead author Dr. Peter Fransquet, a Research Fellow at Monash's School of Public Health and Preventive Medicine, said the results suggest that a person's genetic makeup could eventually play an important role in determining which dementia prevention strategies are most appropriate.

"Previous trials found aspirin didn't prevent dementia when everyone was considered together," Dr. Fransquet said.

"Our findings suggest there may be more to the story.

"In people with this particular genetic profile, we saw substantially fewer cases of dementia among those taking aspirin.

"It raises the possibility that genetics could one day help us identify who may benefit from a preventive treatment, rather than taking a one-size-fits-all approach."

The possibility of more targeted prevention is especially important as dementia becomes increasingly common. Dementia Australia estimates that approximately 446,500 people in Australia are living with the condition in 2026. That number is expected to exceed one million by 2065.

Blood Platelets Reveal an Unexpected Dementia Connection

Previous research has connected dementia with platelet activation and aggregation, the processes through which platelets become active and stick together. However, scientists have had a less complete understanding of how a person's overall platelet count might relate to dementia.

The new findings point toward platelet biology as a potentially important area of investigation. Notably, the genetic association identified by researchers did not involve the well-established genetic risk factors typically linked to Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia.

"What's particularly interesting is that the signal wasn't linked to the established genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease and dementia," Dr. Fransquet said.

"Instead, it's pointing us towards platelet biology and gives us a new avenue to investigate."

Why More Research Is Needed Before Taking Aspirin

Although the findings suggest that aspirin could have different effects depending on a person's genes, researchers emphasize that the results must be confirmed before they can guide medical recommendations.

The next step is to investigate whether the association appears in other populations. Scientists would then need to conduct a clinical trial specifically involving people with the genetic profile identified in the analysis.

"We now need to confirm the finding in other studies and ultimately test it in a trial designed specifically for people with this genetic profile.

"People shouldn't start taking aspirin to prevent dementia on the basis of this study without consulting with their doctor, particularly given the increased risk of serious bleeding.

"If it holds up, the fact that aspirin is already cheap and widely available could make personalized dementia prevention a real possibility."

#2461. Tiny Atomic Defects Reveal a Quantum Secret Hidden Inside an Unusual Metal

Scientists at Columbia University have discovered that electron waves can become trapped inside a metal, even when pathways remain available for them to escape. The finding, published in Science, provides the first experimental evidence of a long-predicted quantum phenomenon in a metallic material.

The trapped waves were found near atomic defects in a metal called Pd5AlI2. Unlike electrons that move freely through conducting materials, these waves remain confined because of quantum interference.

“Bound electrons shouldn’t occur in a metal, but they do in Pd5AlI2. What we have observed is the first clear evidence of quantum interference trapping a state in a metal,” said Columbia theoretical physicist Raquel Queiroz.

Quantum mechanics describes electrons as waves that can travel along multiple possible paths. When these waves overlap, they can reinforce or cancel one another. Under certain conditions, destructive interference can prevent an electron wave from spreading, even when other electron states can move through the same material.

This phenomenon is called a bound state in the continuum (BIC). “Quantum mechanics predicts that an electron wave can, theoretically, remain trapped even when there are infinitely many paths for it to escape. This effect is known from light waves, but it has never been seen before for electrons,” said Queiroz.

The discovery began in Columbia chemist Xavier Roy’s laboratory, where researchers developed Pd5AlI2, a metal that can be separated into thin, two-dimensional layers while retaining its metallic properties. Measurements showed that its electrons moved more slowly than expected.

Working with Queiroz and experimental physicists Cory Dean and Aravind Devarakonda (a former Columbia postdoc who is now an assistant professor of applied physics at Columbia), the team traced this behavior to electron hopping frustration. The metal’s atomic structure creates competing paths for electrons to move between atoms, allowing destructive interference to suppress their movement. The findings were published in Nature Physics.

Researchers working with Columbia physicist Abhay Pasupathy later detected unexpected patterns while examining Pd5AlI2 using atomic-scale scanning and spectroscopy techniques. Further analysis revealed electron waves trapped near defects in the crystal lattice. Queiroz developed theoretical models explaining how quantum interference produced these localized states.

Bound states in the continuum have previously been demonstrated with light, where they have helped advance lasers, optical sensing, and nonlinear optics. The Columbia findings establish that a similar effect can occur with electrons in metals.

“Trapping a wave is an idea as old as quantum mechanics itself, but it took almost a century to figure out how to do it with light. We have now realized it with electrons in a material,” said Roy. “And if you trap a particle, you can manipulate it.”

Electronic bound states could eventually contribute to highly sensitive quantum sensors, quantum information processing, and quantum chemistry. However, controlling their formation remains a challenge.

In Pd5AlI2, the electron traps form around defects that arise randomly during crystal growth. Roy and Pasupathy are now investigating ways to deliberately position these defects and control the resulting interference patterns. The discovery also offers a way to identify other metals with frustrated crystal structures that could support trapped electron waves.

#2462. How To Learn Stuff Quickly

It's often said that the internet has democratized education: the sum of human knowledge is only a Google search away! And yet, having access to information is only half of the story; you also need to be able to convert raw information into usable skills.

For a lot of us, the gap between the two can lead to things like tutorial hell—getting stuck doing tutorial after tutorial without ever feeling like you're making substantive progress.

Learning how to learn effectively is super important, especially as a software developer; learning new things is practically the whole gig! If you can learn to quickly pick up new languages/frameworks/tools, you'll be able to be way more productive than the average developer. It's sort of a superpower.

In this blog post, I'll share what I've learned about learning, and show you how I pick up new skills lickety-split!

Broadly speaking, there are two categories of learning:

If you only follow guided resources, you'll wind up in tutorial hell. You won't develop the problem-solving skills needed to succeed as a developer. When you try to build your own project, you won't know where to start. It will feel like you've spent so much time practicing without developing any tangible, practical skills.

On the other hand, if you focus entirely on unguided learning, it'll take forever. Without an experienced guide, you'll need to reinvent every wheel, spending days or weeks solving already-solved problems. This is a long and frustrating road. In the worst case, you might wind up quitting altogether, convinced (incorrectly!) that you just aren't smart enough for this stuff.

Some courses are aware of this dichotomy, and will include opportunities for unguided learning. Things like stretch goals, thought experiments, and challenging exercises. I wish these types of resources were more common!

Let's look at some ideas for how to mix some unguided learning into guided resources.

If you're anything like me, you don't like making mistakes. You want everything to go perfectly, the very first time.

This mindset is generally helpful in life, and it's helpful in other fields. If you work as an auto mechanic, mistakes can cost hundreds of dollars in parts. If you work as a dentist, a mistake might ruin someone's smile.

With software development, though, mistakes are free! If we make a mistake, we can tab back to our editor, change the code, and try again. We even have helpful error messages that can (sometimes) point us in the right direction. This is an incredible luxury, and not one that we take advantage of enough.

When I follow a tutorial, I like to play with the code. Instead of copy/pasting the provided code verbatim, try experimenting with it: what happens if you omit one of the lines? Or if you change some of the values?

I try and act like a scientist. If I have a hypothesis about how this code is supposed to work, I test that hypothesis by changing the code, and seeing if it breaks in the way I expect. When I discover that my hypothesis is flawed, I might detour from the tutorial and do some research on Google. Or I might add it to a list of "things to explore later", if the rabbit hole seems to go too deep.

This process helps us avoid the sinister rhythm of following a tutorial on autopilot, copy/pasting code without really understanding what it does or why we're doing it.

Learning is an active process. Poking and prodding at the code will help us build a mental model for what's going on.

Years ago, when I was just starting out, I used a process I call "the tutorial fade".

Like the scientist mindset described above, this process is useful because it forces you to pay attention. The tutorial fades away, and you wind up learning how to build the thing without guidance.

This method is super effective, but not everyone enjoys building the same thing over and over. If you've struggled to escape from Tutorial Hell, though, it might be worth a shot!

This strategy is nice because you avoid the stress of a blank canvas. You already have a fully-functional, well-understood project. You're adding bricks to a solid foundation.

It also has a nice side-benefit: if you add significant extensions to a tutorial project, you can take credit for it in your portfolio! I cover this strategy in depth in my book, Building an Effective Dev Portfolio(opens in new tab).

Once you've finished the tic-tac-toe project, you might be a little unsure about what to do next.

Before hopping onto another tutorial, it might be a good idea to try building a similar project from scratch.

For example, maybe you can make a bingo game! You'll be able to leverage some of your new skills (state management, event listening), but in a slightly different context. You'll likely hit a point where you don't know how to do something, because it wasn't covered in the tutorial; you can do some sleuthing on Google to try and find a solution!

If you really can't crack it, you can set this project aside for now. Do a few more tutorials, and then check back later to see if you've learned enough to unblock yourself.

I've seen this strategy described as "one on, one off". Follow a guided resource like a tutorial, and then spend an equal amount of time creating a similar (but unguided) project. If the tutorial shows you how to build an Instagram clone, try building a Bluesky clone on your own!

When I'm at the very beginning of a learning journey, I tend to focus primarily on guided learning. It's difficult to build anything in an unguided way when I'm still grappling with the syntax and the fundamentals!

As I become more comfortable, though, the balance shifts. I spend more and more of my time on unguided learning, building things that seem interesting to me. I'll seek out tutorials when I encounter new and unfamiliar problems, but that becomes less and less common as I gain more experience.

Your graph might look a bit different; ultimately, it's up to you to find the right balance! The important thing is that we don't focus exclusively on guided or unguided learning.

#2467. Scientists overcome a hidden obstacle to more powerful quantum computers

Silicon spin qubits could play a major role in the future of quantum computing because they can be produced using the same semiconductor manufacturing methods that make today's computer chips. However, researchers have faced a significant challenge. When multiple silicon qubits are controlled at the same time, their operations tend to become less accurate. Building practical, large quantum computers will require scientists to control many qubits simultaneously while keeping errors to a minimum.

Researchers at the RIKEN Center for Emergent Matter Science and their collaborators have now made important progress toward solving this problem. In a study published in Nature Physics, the team demonstrated highly accurate control of several silicon spin qubits operating simultaneously. Their experiments used an array of five qubits connected to a shared microwave control line. By identifying a major cause of errors during simultaneous operation, the scientists developed a technique to reduce those errors and maintain precision.

Leon Camenzind, a RIKEN research scientist and co-first author who supervised the experiments, explains the significance of the results: "This shows that high-fidelity control can be maintained even when several qubits are operated at the same time and represents an important step toward scaling up silicon-based quantum processors."

Scientists Identify a Hidden Source of Quantum Computing Errors

Unlike conventional computers, which store information as bits representing either 0 or 1, quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits, that can exist in combinations of both states. Silicon spin qubits store this information using the spin of individual electrons confined within silicon. Performing reliable quantum calculations requires extremely precise control over these electron spins.

Microwave signals provide that control, but operating multiple qubits simultaneously can create unwanted effects that interfere with their accuracy. The researchers traced much of this problem to a phenomenon called the AC Stark shift.

This effect occurs when microwave control signals alter a qubit's effective energy levels, producing changes in the phase of its quantum state. Those phase shifts can disrupt the intended operation of individual qubits, particularly when several are being controlled at once.

To counteract the problem, the team created a calibration method that compensates for these unwanted shifts. Rather than separately calibrating every possible combination of qubits, the technique relies on calibrations performed between pairs of qubits. This makes the approach more practical for systems containing larger numbers of quantum bits.

"Rather than requiring an entirely separate calibration procedure for every possible combination of qubits, our method uses calibrations between pairs of qubits to compensate for the unwanted phase shifts," explains Yi-Hsien Wu, the first author who carried out the experiments as part of his PhD research.

Quantum Qubits Achieve Accuracy Above 99.99%

The researchers combined their calibration technique with an improved device design and carefully adjusted microwave control pulses. Their results showed that exceptionally high accuracy could be maintained even when multiple qubits were operating simultaneously.

When the five qubits were tested individually, each achieved single-qubit gate fidelity well above 99.99%, with the highest approaching 99.999%. Fidelity describes how closely a quantum operation matches its intended result.

The team then tested how well the system performed when several qubits were active together. With as many as three qubits operating simultaneously, fidelity remained above 99.99%. Even when all five qubits were controlled at once, accuracy stayed at approximately 99.9%, approaching the levels needed for practical fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Although the calibration method greatly reduced errors, it did not eliminate them entirely. The researchers found that the remaining loss of accuracy was linked to drive-induced decoherence, an effect caused by the greater microwave power required to operate multiple qubits simultaneously.

Unlike the unwanted phase shifts corrected by the new technique, decoherence involves the loss of the delicate quantum coherence needed for reliable operations.

According to Camenzind, "This is a limitation, but further improvements to device design could help mitigate this source of error."

A New Path Toward Larger Quantum Processors

The findings show that controlling several silicon spin qubits at the same time does not necessarily require a major sacrifice in accuracy. By correcting a key source of errors, the researchers have demonstrated a promising approach for operating more qubits through shared microwave control lines.

This is especially important for the development of large quantum processors. Giving every qubit its own independent control system would make increasingly complex devices difficult to manage. Shared control lines could offer a more practical way to expand these systems while maintaining the precision needed for quantum calculations.

"By addressing a key source of control errors through pairwise calibration," says Camenzind. "Our study supports the use of shared control lines as a practical approach to scaling up quantum processors. Combined with silicon's compatibility with established semiconductor manufacturing, we believe that this advance could help pave the way toward larger quantum processors capable of performing increasingly complex computations."

The broader objective is to develop quantum computers that can reliably perform calculations despite the errors and disturbances that affect quantum systems. Such machines, known as fault-tolerant quantum computers, will require precise, dependable control over large numbers of qubits.

"Our long-term goal is to build a fault-tolerant quantum computer based on silicon spin qubits, and reliable parallel control is an essential step toward that goal," adds Seigo Tarucha, project manager of a silicon quantum computing project under JST's Moonshot Goal 6.

#2469. Scientists Develop a Clever New Way To Kill Cancer Cells From the Inside

Scientists have developed an experimental strategy that exploits a chemical byproduct of cancer cells to produce anticancer drugs directly inside them.

Scientists in Japan have developed an experimental method that uses a chemical produced by cancer cells to manufacture anticancer drugs directly inside them. The approach relies on carbon monoxide, a toxic gas that certain tumors produce in unusually high amounts, to activate a chemical reaction designed to destroy cancer cells while limiting damage to healthy tissue.

In a proof-of-concept study published in Nature Communications, researchers at the RIKEN Pioneering Research Institute (PRI) demonstrated that their system could generate drug compounds selectively in cultured cancer cells. The findings suggest a possible alternative to conventional chemotherapy, although the technique has not yet been tested in animals or humans.

Carbon monoxide is best known as a poisonous gas released during incomplete combustion, including in vehicle exhaust. However, the human body also produces small amounts of it naturally. Research has shown that many types of cancer cells generate elevated levels, creating a chemical difference that could potentially be exploited for targeted treatment.

A team led by Katsunori Tanaka investigated whether this excess carbon monoxide could serve as a raw material for producing anticancer compounds inside tumors. Their strategy uses palladacycles, organometallic compounds containing palladium, to convert carbon monoxide into a molecule called phenanthridinone. Certain phenanthridinone derivatives are toxic to cells and have been used in anticancer drugs.

The researchers faced a significant obstacle: palladium catalysts are rapidly deactivated by substances naturally present inside cells, including glutathione. Without protection, the catalyst would lose its ability to carry out the drug-producing reaction.

To solve this problem, the team enclosed a palladacycle within albumin, an abundant protein found in blood. Albumin acts as a protective carrier, shielding the palladium catalyst from molecules that would otherwise disable it. The resulting albumin complex can circulate through the bloodstream and remain chemically active after entering cells.

After screening several palladacycles to identify the most effective candidate, the researchers tested the protected complex against human liver, cervical, breast, and lung cancer cell lines, along with a noncancerous cell line.

Following 48 hours of incubation, the researchers detected the intended phenanthridinone product in all four cancer cell lines but not in the noncancerous cells. The results indicated that the protected catalyst remained functional and that the reaction depended on chemical conditions associated with the cancer cells.

The team then refined the system to allow the newly synthesized compound to separate from its carrier and move freely within the cell. Further modifications enabled the production of a highly toxic phenanthridinone derivative used in anticancer drugs. When tested in liver cancer cells, the modified system generated the toxic compound internally, causing harmful effects in those cells.

Another concern was whether carbon monoxide already present in blood might activate drug production before the complex reached a tumor. Blood tests showed no detectable formation of the toxic compound. The researchers suggested that carbon monoxide bound to hemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells, was unavailable to participate in the reaction.

Rather than administering a finished anticancer drug, Tanaka’s approach aims to deliver relatively harmless chemical components that become toxic only after encountering suitable conditions inside diseased cells.

“This work represents an important advance in therapeutic in vivo synthetic chemistry,” says Tanaka. “In our therapeutic strategy, molecules naturally present or generated within the body—including disease-associated metabolites—are used as endogenous chemical feedstocks to synthesize therapeutic molecules directly at the site of disease.”

The researchers’ next step is to test the method in animal models, where they can investigate whether the catalyst reaches tumors, produces sufficient amounts of the drug, and avoids damaging healthy tissues.

#2470. Will bystander economies be able to finance their governments?

Will AI poison the public finances of many modern states? There is a real risk that it will.

Yesterday, I circulated a new paper, “Saving for the Machines,” which makes a simple point. Building the AI transformation will require enormous investment. The only way to induce agents to save for that investment is through a large increase in the real interest rate. For instance, in my benchmark scenario, the ten-year real yield jumps from 5.1% to 13.6% on impact.

But that means government borrowing will become much more expensive. Here is a back-of-the-envelope calculation. Take a government that owes 100% of GDP in 2025 (a typical case in Western Europe) and runs a balanced primary budget from then on (not so typical but never mind; I want to build an optimistic scenario). That means the debt ratio changes only with the gap between the real interest rate and growth.

I input the interest rate and the growth rate for my benchmark transition to full automation, and I roll over the debt at the short rate. I consider two economies. The first economy is an AI adopter, for example, the U.S., which experiences an AI boom at home. The government pays the new interest rate but also enjoys faster growth.

The second economy is a bystander, for example, Spain. This economy lives in an integrated financial market but does not adopt AI (this is an extreme case of zero adoption, just to make the exercise transparent): it pays the same world interest rate and continues growing at its old 1.6% trend.

Let’s first consider the adopter. With an intertemporal elasticity of substitution of one-half, the Euler equation implies that consumption growth is about half the real interest rate. Hence, the gap between the interest rate and growth does not close during the AI-driven growth boom. Instead, it widens. For example, with a unit elasticity of substitution among tasks, the real rate rises from 5.1% to 42% by 2040, while growth increases only from 1.6% to 22%. Debt doubles by 2038, reaches 296% of GDP in 2040, and 907% in 2045. To hold the ratio at 100%, the government needs a primary surplus of 10% of GDP in 2035 and 24% from 2045 onward. With a low elasticity of substitution of 0.2, the numbers are milder, but the pattern is the same: 204% in 2040, 397% in 2045, and a stabilizing surplus of 13% of GDP.

But the adopter has a way out. The capital share goes to one, so net capital income is about 88% of GDP, and a flat tax on it can cover the surplus. A 36% tax on net capital income holds the debt at 100% of GDP in the limit with unit elasticity, and a 20% tax does so with strong complementarity across tasks. The tax is above the gap because it slows growth, from 21.9% to 13.6% with unit elasticity and from 11.7% to 9.1% with an elasticity of 0.2, while it does nothing to the rate the Treasury pays, which technology pins at the machines’ net return. Painful, but the boom survives and the adopter can pay its debt.

The grim news is that the bystander economy has no way out. Its capital income is still a third of GDP, which grows at 1.6%, and the surplus it needs is 11% of GDP in 2030, 26% in 2035, and 44% forever with unit elasticity (18% and 24% with an elasticity of 0.2). On a balanced primary budget, its debt doubles by 2033 and triples by 2035. No tax system raises that.

Two more bad pieces of news for the bystander. First, long yields jump on impact, so a Treasury that issues ten-year debt pays more, and sooner, than rolling over at the short rate assumes: it is the same bill but front-loaded. Second, the bystander’s own capital flows to the machines abroad, so its GDP and tax base fall below trend. One relief is that households can save for other people’s machines and get rich on the returns, which the bystander’s government can tax to some extent. This back-of-the-envelope calculation is a benchmark, not a forecast of what is to come. But the arithmetic is simple, and it runs counter to the hope that an AI-fueled growth boom will take care of public debt, because growth comes with an interest rate that is twice as high. Not a pretty future for most of Western Europe. Due to AI, the choice is between default, inflation, or, most likely, financial repression: capital controls that keep domestic savers from investing in machines abroad.

I am somewhat more optimistic than this.  The assumption of zero Spanish adoption already is noted, but also ideas are public goods, and Spain can benefit from American (and Chinese) AI, and the resulting scientific advances.  The long-run supply of capital also might be quite elastic, we will see.

“The honest traveller is surprised to observe that the red sand is ringed by snow-capped mountains and impenetrable forests populated by tetchy bears,” writes Le Carrer in a chapter on the so-called Nevada Triangle. Le Carrer adds that any offer of an excursion by plane should be politely refused.

The reason is simple: thanks to the turbulence generated by the rugged landscape, more than 2,000 aircraft have gone missing there over the past five decades — more than that of the fabled Bermuda Triangle.

The author’s white-knuckle ride across America and around the globe features 40 stops ranging from Golgotha, the site outside Jerusalem of Christ’s crucifixion, to the Japanese suicide venue, Aokigahara Forest, and Australia’s Cape York, where crocodiles noted for explosive attacks approach speeds of 30 km/h.

The result is a travel guide in reverse: a gazetteer of places where the curious should keep one hand on the emergency exit. Here are just ten ‘cursed’ places I picked from the book.

Were it not for the people going about their business, Jharia might look like a place recently shelled. Smoke rises from cracks in the ground while the earth sags. The landscape has the exhausted look of a battlefield.

Except here, as Olivier Le Carrer notes, the fire “does not come from the sky.” It comes from below.

For more than a century, old coal seams beneath this district of Jharkhand in India have been burning. Such fires are not rare in abandoned mines, but Jharia is home to roughly half a million people. They live above or beside what Le Carrer calls a “steadily expanding underground inferno,” made up of dozens of separate blazes.

At first, residents even found uses for it. Steam from fissures could cook a pot. But the seeping coal fumes weren’t so useful when some houses split and roads started to buckle, all in an atmosphere of toxic fumes.

At the edge of Russia, near the Norwegian border, the Kola Peninsula first offers the usual northern temptations. There’s the never-ending tundra, the lovely fjords, and the frigid terror of polar night.

Zapadnaya Litsa has no shortage of abandoned barracks, broken factories, smashed windows, and military debris. Le Carrer gives it a harsher title: “the antechamber of hell.” The true horror lies lower down, in the fjords, where the retired monsters of the Northern Fleet wait.

Dozens of decommissioned, Cold War-era vessels — including massive Typhoon and fast Alfa class submarines — were abandoned here, left to rust and decay while awaiting complex and hazardous dismantling processes. Le Carrer mentions the old nuclear submarines sit “like harmless museum exhibits,” although they are anything but. The base once served a superpower. Now it keeps watch over radioactive fuel in quantities Le Carrer describes as “thirty times greater than that of the Chernobyl reactor.”

The Maldives sells the dream of a flawless lagoon. Thilafushi receives the bill.

Le Carrer labels it “The Toxic Lagoon,” a grim counterpoint to the country’s postcard image of overwater villas and transparent seas. In 1992, authorities began turning this narrow island near Malé into a landfill, a dump for all the garbage produced by the archipelago.

As the number of tourists to the Maldives skyrocketed, trash arrived by the ton. Migrant workers at the site burned what they could. Heavy metals, batteries, and chemicals seeped toward the water. When the island ran out of space, waste sometimes spread into the shallows, creating drifting rafts of garbage in a lagoon marketed to the world as paradise.

Le Carrer calls the result the “highly visible abscess of the infection.” Apparently, even Eden produces rubbish, and someone has to stand downwind of it

From Venice, Poveglia is almost insultingly close: a small green island in the lagoon, overlooked by a bell tower and abandoned buildings.

It might look charming but Le Carrer calls it the “Island of Death,” and the legends have done much of the work. In Veneto, Le Carrer writes, people say that “when a bad man dies, he wakes up on Poveglia.”

Stories claim plague victims and lepers were exiled there and thousands of bodies lie beneath the soil. A psychiatric hospital later added a second layer of horror. In the most lurid versions, a mad doctor tortured patients and threw himself from the campanile.

The archives confirm a quarantine station, later care homes, and then closure in 1968, but not every bloody detail the internet now repeats.

More recently, Poveglia (often called the world’s most haunted island) turned into a bizarre real estate battle. In 2014, the Italian state offered a 99-year lease on the island at an auction. A Venetian citizens’ group, Poveglia per Tutti, crowdfunded to buy it for public use, but a businessman named Luigi Brugnaro won the auction with a €513,000 bid. The deal eventually stalled, keeping Poveglia locked in a bureaucratic and spectral limbo.

The Sunda Strait is beautiful in the way a sleeping dragon is beautiful.

Between Java and Sumatra, the sea route carries ships through shallows, oil installations, new school pirates, and one of the most famous volcanic hot spots on Earth: Krakatoa. In 1883, after months of eruptions and tremors, the volcano exploded with such violence that its sound reportedly traveled thousands of miles. To this day, it’s considered the loudest thing in history. Tsunami waves smashed into nearby coasts. The official death toll reached about 40,000.

Krakatoa did not vanish after the disaster. Its offspring, Anak Krakatoa, rose from the sea in the 20th century and has continued to remind the strait that geology works on its own schedule.

Indonesia sits along one of the world’s most active tectonic regions, where subduction feeds volcanoes and tsunamis. To build, sail, and live there is to negotiate with forces that predate every port, bridge, and shipping lane.

Le Carrer calls Cape Horn the Sailor’s Nightmare.” In less than two centuries, some 800 ships wrecked near this dark rock at the end of South America, taking perhaps 20,000 lives with them. The region is blanketed by gray sea, cold rain, poor visibility, brutal wind, and “always the feeling that one’s troubles are only just beginning.”

Nobody came here for romance. Sailors came because commerce told them to. Before the Panama Canal, rounding the Horn was part of the long Atlantic-to-Pacific ordeal. Ships clawed their way through the Roaring Forties and Furious Fifties (strong, continuous westerly winds circling the Southern Hemisphere) that whip around the planet and the sea. With almost no land to stop them, the winds grew monstrous.

In 1914, the canal cut thousands of miles from the voyage and spared many ships the old punishment. Cape Horn, Le Carrer writes, “got what it deserved.”

Still, the place keeps its talent. It merely torments fewer people now.

The Houtman Abrolhos islands sit off Western Australia, low and reef-ringed, a trap disguised as an archipelago.

In 1629, the Dutch East India Company ship Batavia struck the reefs with more than 300 people on board. The wreck should have been the disaster. It became only the opening scene.

While a rescue party left for Java, assistant merchant Jeronimus Cornelisz took control of the survivors. What followed can only be described as organized murder. Cornelisz, a failed apothecary turned company official, began arranging resources by his own twisted arithmetic. Those he considered useless were sent to islands without fresh water, drowned quietly, or killed more openly once his intentions became impossible to miss. His men murdered “by whatever violent means” they had available. Women were spared only to be abused.

Rescue arrived from Java after more than two months. Cornelisz and six accomplices were hanged. Nearly 200 others were already dead.

The reef wrecked the ship. The crew did the rest.

Can a wildlife sanctuary suffer from too much nature? Kasanka National Park is one of Africa’s smallest reserves. Driven to the brink by poachers, conservationists successfully revived the park, reintroducing elephants and hippos. But nobody expected the sky to turn black. Every October, five million straw-colored fruit bats descend on a tiny forest within the park. Le Carrer describes the phenomenon as the “African remake of one of Alfred Hitchcock’s most unsettling films”.

These aggressive mammals strip the trees, devouring 330,000 tons of fruit annually. The massive swarm also attracts predators. Pythons, crocodiles, and birds of prey crowd the area, feasting on fallen bats. Ecologists study this colossal migration to understand shifting global wildlife patterns, yet the bats’ final destination remains unknown. The site remains a terrifying spectacle for anyone brave enough to visit.

Aokigahara lies at the foot of Mount Fuji, where beauty and dread share the same trees.

The forest is often called a suicide forest. It covers barely fourteen square miles, yet it claims around one hundred lives each year.

Le Carrer describes a dense, dim “sea of trees,” where the ground is uneven, mossy, and broken by crevasses. Paths are scarce and people can easily get lost. More painfully, many have gone there intending not to return. Authorities and volunteers have tried to intervene, but people looking to end their lives still venture here, as if lured by a siren call.

Pop culture has been blamed, notably Seichō Matsumoto’s 1960 novel Black Sea of Trees, where two lovers end their lives at Aokigahara forest. But locals insist the forest’s dark appeal predates modern literature. They believe the “yuurei — the ghosts of the departed who wander the earth in search of paradise — are well acquainted with Aokigahara”. Historically, families practiced ubasute here, abandoning elderly relatives to die.

In modern Carthage, amid villas, cypresses, and bougainvillea, one of antiquity’s darkest arguments lies underfoot.

The Tophet of Carthage, also known as the Salammbo, has yielded twenty thousand funerary urns, many containing the remains of very young children. Ancient writers accused the Carthaginians of sacrificing children to the gods Baal and Tanit. Some modern scholars have argued instead that the site may have served as a cemetery for infants who died naturally.

Ancient Carthaginian families allegedly offered their firstborn males to secure the gods’ favor. Despite these gruesome rituals, Carthage ultimately fell. The Romans destroyed the city in 146 BCE, and according to myth sowed the ruins with salt such that nothing would grow there. Le Carrer asks rhetorically if the city fell because “the earth of the tophet was never able to digest all that blood.”

When I first started using styled-components, it seemed like magic ✨.

Somehow, using an obscure half-string-half-function syntax, the tool was able to take some arbitrary CSS and assign it to a React component, bypassing the CSS selectors we've always used.

Like so many devs, I learned how to use styled-components, but without really understanding what was going on under the hood.

Instead of eagerly generating CSS classes whenever a styled component is defined, we wait until the component is rendered before injecting those styles into the page.

This is a good thing! On larger websites, it's not uncommon for hundreds of kilobytes of unused CSS to be sent to the browser. With styled-components, you only pay for the CSS you render, not the CSS you write*Technically, some unused CSS will still wind up in the JS bundle, but when we're server-side-rendering, that's a worthwhile tradeoff: we can paint the page before we receive the JS, but not before we receive the CSS..

The only reason this works is because JavaScript has closures. Every component generated from styled.h1 has its own little scope that holds the CSS string. When we render our Wrapper component, even if it's seconds/minutes/hours later, it has exclusive access to the styles we've written for it.
