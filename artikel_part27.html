#1301. Can James Carville Please Just Be Quietly Wrong Somewhere Else?

The omnipresent consultant-cum-tantrum-thrower has long outlived whatever relevance he once had, but cable producers, podcast hosts, and Democratic campaigns haven’t got the memo.

Democratic political consultant James Carville at last year’s New York Times DealBook summit.

He recently appeared with Bill Kristol on a podcast hosted by The Bulwark and stated that he’d rather Republicans keep the House by a five-member majority after the midterms than have Democrats win, because then “they’re hostage to the DSA.” The Daily Beast, long tethered to a clickbait-through-faux-outrage business model, ran an account of Carville’s comments under the sensationalistic headline “Top Dem Strategist Makes Shock Midterms Wish.” Unfortunately, though, the fact that Carville is bashing progressives and suggesting they’re worse than Republicans is not shocking at all: He’s been doing that for years now, at every opportunity.

This has put me in the uncomfortable position of trusting Carville less than Bill Kristol, one of the architects of the Iraq War and a Republican conservative turned Never Trumper. Unlike Carville, Kristol at least seems to understand what progressives stand for and occasionally concedes that they have good points. “I don’t agree with people saying ICE and CBP need ‘more training,’” Kristol wrote on Bluesky recently. “They’re doing exactly what this administration has trained them to—impose a reign of fear in blue cities. They don’t need more training. They need to be ripped up root and branch.” In the absurdist political world of 2026, in other words, we have Bill Kristol calling for ICE to be abolished, and James Carville rooting for ongoing Republican rule in Congress in lieu of having his political sensibilities unpleasantly challenged.

Carville loathes the left in a knee-jerk way that isn’t backed by any serious thoughtfulness about what progressives want—or even what voters at large want. A majority of Democrats want Medicare for All. Seventy-four percent of Democrats want to tax the rich more. Four in 10 Americans (not just Democrats!) think capitalism isn’t helping them and are open to socialist solutions to some problems. Sixty-four percent of Americans think we should give Israel less military and financial support. These are all things that Carville and his cohort of 1990s-era political power brokers like to characterize as views held by tiny, unrealistic, and leftist minorities.

Carville often distorts the positions of the Democratic Socialists in particular, intentionally reinforcing negative characterizations lofted by fellow pundits at one of his preferred outlets for bloviating, Fox News. In August he criticized a DSA candidate on the basis that he “has the attribute of every DSA member in the country, and that is he has a motor mouth.”

James Carville complaining that someone who is not James Carville has a motor mouth is like Donald Trump complaining that someone who is not Donald Trump has a reputation for bankrupting casinos. But self-awareness is not Carville’s strong suit generally. He also likes to mouth loyalty to the working classes, playing up his “ragin’ Cajun’” persona while being married to a former Republican consultant and from the comfort of one of his multiple multimillion-dollar homes.

Still, Carville’s greatest offense is continuing to live in the reality of Clinton-era politics while spouting advice to those of us who live here in 2026. Some of this might be chalked up to age and complacency. Carville wouldn’t be the first 81-year-old who doesn’t have his finger on the pulse of what anyone under the age of 40 is thinking or doing. He’s also not the first man to enjoy early success and dine out on it for decades—or to assume that the expertise founded on that achievement is stable over time and does not need to be refreshed or reassessed as circumstances change. He is also not the only Democrat to trot out a ’90s-era political playbook posited on the vital role played by s a nebulous center of reasonable moderates in the electorate who switch back and forth between parties. Nor is he the only aging centrist who believes, wrongly, that the far left is as dangerous as the far right.

But he is one of the people who can’t stop talking about it. And for some inexplicable reason, he’s the outraged centrist whom the media can’t stop platforming as an avatar for mainstream Democratic politics—even as he stubbornly refuses to educate himself about who progressives are and what they want, beyond crude ideological caricatures that wouldn’t be out of place in the editorial pages of The Wall Street Journal.

He’s also still leaning on the idea that elections are about one issue: the economy. I watched D.A. Pennebaker’s The War Room three years after it came out, in a political science class taught by a former Republican consultant. I don’t remember any specific lines from it except Carville’s “it’s the economy, stupid” mantra, which, more than anything, defined Carville’s brand as a strategist to the public. Like many early Carville-isms, there was truth to it, but it was also an oversimplification. Voters are always driven by economic concerns, but elections are never about one thing—even Bill Clinton’s victory over George H.W. Bush, in the midst of a deep recession, was driven by increasing disillusionment with the first Iraq War, Clinton’s personal charisma and media savviness, and a host of other things that had little to do with the real economy.

We’re in much the same position now, as voters are angry over the state of the economy, but also register strong sentiments against rampant corruption and threats to democracy. Nevertheless, Carville is relentlessly on his 1992 message, trotting out a variation of “it’s the economy, stupid” on the countless occasions he’s in front of a camera or podcast mic. In a recent Fox News appearance, he brayed that Democrats need to avoid culture-war issues and identity politics and just focus on the economy. “quit f—ing around with all this cultural stuff and start talking about people’s wages…. Start talking about what we can do to protect people who are trying to make it and particularly young people.”

By protecting young people who are trying to make it, he did not mean protecting immigrants as they seek to avoid violence and detention from ICE, young women who need abortions, or young Black men who don’t want to be killed or beaten by the police. Nor was he referring to young trans people who end up murdered because the right paints them as sexual deviants or pedophiles, and people in Carville’s cohort nod solemnly and say maybe they have a point about trans women in sports. He’s talking about wages and the ability of young people to find jobs.

All politics are identity politics on some level, but like many white straight men of a certain age, Carville doesn’t consider “white straight male” an identity. It’s simply the cultural default in his view of America. He assumes the priorities of the liberal or liberal-leaning white straight man are generally good for everybody else.

But in reality, you cannot separate culture from politics, or identity from politics. Politics is about power and its uses—and elections are about who should have it. Voter perception of the economy is heavily driven by vibes in part because the metrics of the real economy—GDP, productivity growth, even inflation—don’t necessarily reflect the situation on the ground for people. Feelings of financial security are often proxies for whether people feel safe and satisfied generally.

In other words, what constitutes a kitchen-table issue—to use the refrain favored by Carville and the centrist Democratic consultant class—depends on whose kitchen table you’re sitting at. If you’re a young woman being forced to carry a baby you don’t want and bankrupted by medical bills for complications or an unexpected C-section, your earnings potential limited because now you’re not going to college and there’s a lower ceiling on your earning potential, that’s an economic issue as well as a bodily autonomy issue.

And you cannot talk persuasively about affordability without talking about class mobility via education. That ideal is under sustained attack from Republicans who oppose DEI on the basis that they do not want women and minorities in power. Meanwhile, our political economy teems with other practical obstacles afflicting the life chances of members of less-resourced communities, from the administrative burdens of receiving public assistance (some of which, it must be said, were created by Bill Clinton’s welfare “reforms”) to the increasing cost of caring for an aging population. The country’s manifold healthcare crisis—a key driver of affordability—has been made far worse by things like caretakers’ being detained and deported and ob-gyns’ withholding care because they fear criminal prosecution for treating complications in pregnancies. It’s affordability, stupid—but not to the exclusion of the things that Carville dismisses as culture war issues. In many cases, it’s an affordability issue because of these things.

Much of Carville’s analysis is facile and blinkered because he does not understand that the electoral environment of 2026 is not the electoral environment of 1992. “It’s negative partisanship, stupid” may not be the pithiest slogan, but it applies much more directly to politics in the Trump era than Carville’s nostrums do. There is no persuading the GOP to make common cause with Democrats—which is why Republican candidates never attempt it or talk about it. But like many self-identified centrist Boomers, Carville spends more energy reaching across the aisle to hypothetical swing voters than he does attending to the real-world plight of core Democratic constituencies.

Progressives—including the DSA members and candidates Carville keeps insulting—overwhelmingly supported Kamala Harris in the last election, far more so than the weaker independent coalitions Carville seems to want to reach with his Fox News appearances. Progressives exhibit the highest levels of political engagement in the Democratic coalition, yet Carville keeps insisting that Democrats win elections in spite of them rather than because of them.

I won’t pretend to know whether he does this because he’s genuinely ignorant of these facts, or because he just sympathizes more with neoliberal interests and thinks that if he paints the left as fringe-y, impractical, or stupid, that voters will internalize that message and pull the core Democratic electorate rightward. If Carville keeps suggesting that any Democrat’s association with Hasan Piker—a streamer he’d never heard of before this election cycle—is a bigger problem for Democrats than the associations of congressional Republicans with assorted white supremacists, technofascist interests, and Donald Trump himself, I’ll assume that Carville actually wants a Democratic Party made up of MAGA collaborationists. Either way, he does Democrats no favors, because that’s not what voters have told us—repeatedly—they want.

When I ponder the stubborn tenacity of Carville’s pundit career, I keep thinking about a recent Wired article titled “Men Can’t Stop Faking How Much They Know About AI.” That headline is not exactly surprising to me, since I’ve had men explain AI incorrectly to me as recently as yesterday. (I’m a former tech equity analyst and cohost a financial news podcast, but I am also a girl, you understand.) But the phony claims to expertise from American men catalogued in the Wired story have strong correlations with Carville-style punditry: One Harvard study cited in the piece found that when quizzed about AI leaders, men were more likely to confidently assert that they knew who specific people were—even when the names presented to them were fictional. They were also less likely to admit that they didn’t know the answer to a question.

Carville fits the same dreary profile: He’s overconfident about what thinks he knows and will not admit to what he doesn’t. Democrats and media organizations do not need to keep giving him a megaphone. There are plenty of smart people in Democratic political circles who have worked on an actual campaign since the turn of the century, who have informed and helpful things to say, and who understand that the Trump-era GOP is not analogous to prior GOP regimes. Nor do they uncritically assume that today’s Democratic constituencies are analogous to the coalition that put Bill Clinton in office.

James Carville has plenty of money, social connections, and leisure pursuits to keep him busy—which is more than most Americans get to enjoy during their retirement years. If he really wants to help Democrats, he might put his energies there—or at the very least, step back, stop talking, and see if he might, at long last, learn how to listen.

Elizabeth Spiers is a digital media strategist and writer living in Brooklyn. She is the former editor in chief of The New York Observer.

#1305. German 'lawfare': When lawsuits become a political weapon

The Left Party is suing two government ministers for accusing them of antisemitism, while Chancellor Friedrich Merz has been sued several times by various parties. Is German politics descending into "lawfare?"

A verbal slip, an attempted quip, a lawsuit: Things can move fast in the rancorous atmosphere that currently rules German politics.

The brief exchange happened on a political TV talk show shortly after the Berlin election on September 20, won by the socialist Left Party. The show's host, Maybrit Illner, quickly corrected herself after accidentally saying of the Left Party's Berlin mayoral candidate, "Elif Eralp says she is an antisemite…. She is not an antisemite."

One of her guests, Federal Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt, of the conservative Christian Social Union (CSU), responded immediately: "Na doch" (or, "well, yes she is"). In the angry fallout, the Left Party's lawyers jumped on these two words, and have filed a lawsuit, arguing that this amounted to defamation of Eralp, as it falsely suggested that the Left Party politician did indeed consider herself antisemitic.

Not only that, the suit stated, Dobrindt's remark would "undermine her integrity as a political officeholder" and hinder her attempts to form a government in Berlin.

It was not the first time the Left Party has got its lawyers involved following its election victory, as it gets bombarded by accusations of antisemitism from the center right, especially over an incident at an election campaign event in Berlin when a rapper chanted "Death to the IDF" — the Israel Defense Forces, or the Israeli army.

Economy Minister Katherina Reiche also angered the socialist party by giving a speech in which she said that the party was now "openly embracing antisemitism." A video of the speech was posted on the Economy Ministry's Instagram account.

The lawsuit has become an increasingly popular weapon in German politics in recent years, usually by deploying sections 185, 186 and 187 of the German Criminal Code, which cover offenses including insult, defamation and "intentional defamation," which means knowingly asserting or disseminating untrue facts about others. Theoretically, the punishment can be a fine or up to a year in prison.

In September 2025, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) pressed defamation charges against Chancellor Friedrich Merz after he said that the far-right party's policy of remigration amounted to "ethnic cleansing."

Similarly, the Green Party in Berlin brought hate speech charges against Merz last year, when he said "we still have this problem in the cityscape" when describing the presence of immigrants.

In another case, the Green Party's own Robert Habeck was sued in 2024 by the Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) for claiming that the new upstart party was "buying votes" on the internet by building up "troll armies." Prosecutors dropped the case after Habeck agreed to pay €12,000 ($13,500) to three charities without admitting any wrongdoing, on the grounds that there was only limited public interest in a criminal procedure.

But charges like these, especially when politicians claim defamation, have little chance of success. "Prosecutors usually dismiss such cases very quickly due to a lack of sufficient grounds for suspicion," media lawyer Christian Solmecke, of the firm WBS Legal, told DW in an email.

"The Federal Constitutional Court has consistently held that, in the political arena, the boundaries of what is permissible are significantly wider than in the private sphere," Solmecke added. "Anyone who voluntarily enters the political arena must also be prepared to endure sharp, polemical, exaggerated and hurtful criticism."

Felix Zimmermann, editor-in-chief of the legal affairs outlet Legal Tribune Online (LTO), agrees, because German law makes a careful distinction between an opinion and a false statement. "'Antisemitic' or 'not antisemitic' is an opinion, especially at the moment, when people have very different definitions of it," Zimmermann told DW.

That hasn't deterred politicians from trying it more and more often. "We are undoubtedly observing a new level of intensity and scale of this practice," said Solmecke. "Political culture has become harsher and more polarized. The threshold for no longer merely engaging political opponents through debate, but instead outright criminalizing them, has dropped dramatically."

The danger, Solmecke argues, is that this often amounts to the "misuse" of law enforcement agencies as political instruments.

But lawfare appears to be effective as a political tactic, not least because it's a guaranteed headline. "From the point of view of the parties filing the complaints, they are highly effective as a PR strategy," said Solmecke. "The political benefit arises entirely independently of the legal outcome."

In the Left Party's case, Zimmermann thinks it certainly pays to show you won't stand to be called antisemitic — not least because Eralp has repeatedly underlined her commitment to the Jewish community in Berlin.

"I think it's mainly about the fact that the Left Party wants to form a coalition in Berlin and Ms. Eralp wants to become mayor," Zimmermann said. "So of course it's good if you're insulted to challenge it, as a signal."

The motion against Katherina Reiche, on the other hand, may have much better chances — because she chose to post her inflammatory speech on the Economy Ministry's official social media channels. This could, Zimmermann said, well be seen as a violation of the "principle of neutrality," a constitutional rule that forbids the state from vilifying any particular political party.

"That is of course difficult for a politician, because if they say it on their own account, they're allowed, but if they say it on the ministry's account, they're not allowed," said Zimmermann. "It's a little absurd, because you're effectively splitting one person into two."

But even though the Left Party filed the complaint against Reiche as an urgent motion, the Constitutional Court may take several weeks, if not months, to actually decide the case — by which time the coalition negotiations in Berlin may have long since finished and Reiche's speech has had its effect.

The consequences for Reiche, in other words, will be limited — even if her ministry is forced to take down the post and the Left Party can eventually claim a victory.

The Archdiocese of New York has agreed to an $800 million settlement with approximately 1,300 victims of clergy sexual abuse, marking the second-largest payout by a U.S. Catholic archdiocese.

Archbishop Ronald Hicks sent an email Saturday to clergy confirming the settlement after attorneys for the survivors of alleged abuse announced it. Hicks said the agreement will not be final until the parties sign it, an action scheduled for the week of Nov. 6.

The Archdiocese of Los Angeles reached the largest U.S. sexual abuse settlement to date, agreeing in 2024 to pay $880 million to 1,000 victims in addition to $740 million in previous payouts.

Other major Catholic archdiocese settlements include $395 million in San Francisco for around 500 lawsuits and at least $230 million approved in New Orleans.

The agreement with the New York archdiocese would end more than seven years of lawsuits. The parties agreed to a framework for an agreement in May that required everyone who filed a claim to sign off.

“The unanimous agreement of survivors who filed claims against the Archdiocese of New York marks a powerful moment in the pursuit of justice,” Jeff Anderson, an attorney representing people who filed more than 1,000 of claims against the Catholic Church in New York state, said in a statement.

“Through their courage, sacrifice, and determination, survivors have helped bring about a reckoning and a meaningful step toward accountability.”

The Archdiocese of New York covers the city's boroughs of the Bronx, Manhattan and Staten Island as well as seven counties to the north of New York City and is home to about 300 churches, more than 150 parochial schools and 2.5 million Catholics.

In his email to clergy, Hicks said that if church members ask about the settlement, “please let them know that much progress has been made.”

“We are on the verge of being able to, please God, bring some measure of healing and reconciliation to all victim-survivors,” he wrote. “This agreement will also allow the archdiocese to turn its full attention once again to its mission of preaching the Gospel, healing the sick, caring for the poor and needy, welcoming the stranger, and educating our children.”

A New York law enacted in 2019 allows survivors of child sexual abuse to file lawsuits against their abusers and institutions that shielded them until they turn 55. People who had already had turned 55 when the law was enacted also were given until August 2021 to file lawsuits, no matter when the alleged abuse occurred.

“This is an extraordinary achievement for the survivors,” said Trusha Goffe, an attorney in Anderson’s firm. "The survivors remined resolute through years of litigation to reach this monumental settlement.”

#1311. Who Was Emily Brontë?

A new biography tries to reconstruct her everyday life.

At first, I felt resistant to the idea of reading an Emily Brontë biography. After all, Brontë did not make herself the most approachable author—or person, for that matter. If you take Elizabeth Gaskell’s word for it, she was “never happy nor well but on the sweeping moors that gathered round her home—that hater of strangers, doomed to live amongst them.” Nobody knows what endowed her with such fierceness and strangeness or made her do what she did or write what she wrote. As someone who has read nearly everything she wrote that survives, I must confess that I didn’t really want to know. Brontë herself, after all, seems to have wanted her readers to know nothing about her, not even her real name.

In her new biography of the unknowable Emily, This Dark Night, Deborah Lutz doesn’t commit the prime biographical sin of trying to make Brontë too easy to understand or relate to. Instead, she asks more questions than she answers.

For this reason, while some readers may be frustrated by the paucity of new facts or explanations for why Emily was the way she was, for others (including this reader) this vagueness is the very reason for the book’s success. Weighing in at a mere 264 pages (before notes), This Dark Night is not the door-stopper that habitual readers of biography might be used to. The fact of the matter is that Brontë didn’t live very long (she died of tuberculosis at age 30), and very few traces of her short life remain. Much of what we “learn” here is speculation, gleaned from the poetry, prose, and fragments of writing—sometimes literal scraps as well as ﻿minuscule productions that Lutz calls “paper crafts”—that she left behind.

From these pieces, as well as those by her siblings Anne, Charlotte, and the good-for-nothing Branwell, Lutz imagines what Emily’s days would have been like. Born in 1818 to an Irish clergyman father and a mother who came from a wealthy Cornish merchant family, Emily had to grow up fast. While she was still a young child, she lost her mother and two older sisters, and over the next two decades her beloved remaining siblings moved away: Charlotte continued her schooling and became a disgruntled teacher, Branwell tried and failed at a variety of professions, and Anne found some success as a governess.

As a result, Emily became the de facto head of the Brontë household at the Haworth parsonage in Yorkshire. While her father, Patrick, was busy with his spiritual and intellectual pursuits as the parson of Haworth, Emily took on some of the domestic tasks of cooking and cleaning. Even after her siblings returned from their positions elsewhere, Emily remained the main caretaker of the family’s home.

Hedging language (such as would and may) predominates here, as Lutz tells us what life in the parsonage was like and describes Emily’s day-to-day tasks, which included “turning” clothes (reversing and restitching them so the less-worn sides faced outward), peeling potatoes, and feeding the dogs, as well as her role in the famous imaginative worlds of the Brontë siblings.

Lutz also has to rely on this hedging language when exploring Emily’s intellectual formation in these years. She makes the assumption that the Brontës’ mother, Maria, had “bluestocking ways” and likely read Mary Wollstonecraft based on her membership in various lending libraries, including the Penzance Ladies Book Club, which seems a little too easy, but otherwise sticks to what we can reasonably know or assume. She tells us about Emily’s attendance at church; her time spent in Keighley, where she borrowed books; and her famous wanderings on the moors with her siblings and her mastiff mix, Keeper.

To that end, Lutz wisely keeps out of the debate surrounding what Emily may or may not have done to get sent away to school at age 17. Emily and the other Brontës, Lutz reminds us, had a social existence in Haworth and in Yorkshire more generally. They weren’t, as is often depicted, merely hermits living alone on the heath.

The mythology of Emily as a free-range “moor nymph” was partly started by Charlotte in her editor’s preface to the posthumous second edition of Wuthering Heights. In her grief, and perhaps also because of an inability to communicate what Emily was really like, Charlotte depicted her genius sister as a kind of feral savant—a “native and nursling of the moors,” as she put it—who was somehow otherworldly.

Lutz seeks to remind us that this was not the case by returning Emily to the domestic sphere as well as the “wild workshop” of West Yorkshire that she loved so much. Her Emily is not some weird sprite haunting the landscape, but something even stranger: a real woman.

Emily was also a writer. With Charlotte and Anne, she wrote a series of poems that were published under their male pseudonyms: Ellis, Currer, and Acton Bell. Although there had been plans for Emily and Anne to publish their novels first (Wuthering Heights and Agnes Grey, respectively, which were published together in 1847), they did not appear until Charlotte’s hugely successful Jane Eyre came out separately, also in 1847.

Unlike Jane Eyre and Agnes Grey, Wuthering Heights does not tell the story of a poor governess making her way in the world. Instead it draws on the twisted tales of violence and drama that Emily had heard in her Yorkshire youth, as well as the stories of the bog bodies and other mysteries that pervaded the local landscape. There is nothing nice or educative about the novel; all of its characters, from the pain-wracked lovers, Cathy and Heathcliff, to the miserable servants who inhabit the Heights alongside them, are at best tortured, and at worst torturers themselves.

Ever since Wuthering Heights first appeared (also under the nom de plume Ellis Bell), it has brought out strong feelings, both good and bad, in its readers. According to Lutz, critics at the time largely agreed that the novel was “coarse, strange, disagreeable.” But as the years have gone on, many others have been drawn to it precisely for its strangeness.

Feature / Sasha Abramsky

One such reader, who found in Brontë an unsettlingly kindred spirit, was another unrelatable Emily, Emily Dickinson, who called her “gigantic” and wanted one of Brontë’s poems read at her funeral. One can only speculate about what drew one Emily to the other, but I fancy that at least part of it was their shared sense of estrangement. Both insisted on a certain distance between themselves and the world; they asked readers not to get closer to them but to remain at a remove.

This challenge—to be drawn into another’s writing but also to stand at some distance from them—persists today. In a post-theory world still laboring in the shadow of the New Critics, most graduate students in literature are taught that identification is the cardinal sin—that to identify with a character, or to identify the speaker of a poem as the author, is naïve. Yet as a teacher, I’ve noticed my undergraduate students using the word relate as though it were a prerequisite to being drawn toward a text rather than a pitfall to avoid. I think that sidestepping this shift—from seeing identification as an intellectual mistake to embracing it as a feelings-based relation—is at the core of how I approach Brontë’s life. I don’t want to view her simply as a cold and intellectual figure. But I also don’t want to relate to her; to the extent that I do, it shows me monstrous capabilities in myself.

The point is not to bring Emily Brontë down to earth by claiming (as the 2019 show Dickinson did to that later Emily) that she was just like us, all anachronistic feelings and hidden Sapphic longings, but to bring her down to an earth that was uniquely hers. Lutz’s book does just that. Her Emily is a thrillingly earthbound creature, as individual and ultimately as inaccessible as we all are. To think we know her or understand her would be reductive. But to realize that we don’t fully know or understand her is to realize that she is just like us.

After reading This Dark Night, I don’t feel like I really know Emily Brontë any better, but I do think I understand her world a bit better—her terroir, the environment she was steeped in and that infuses her verse and prose. Lutz does not try to explain Emily—rather, she leaves her where she wanted to be, on her own. Lutz doesn’t make her do anything; instead, she wonders often about how Emily might have found her eventual role as the keeper of the Haworth parsonage, but she is careful not to put words in her subject’s mouth.

We don’t learn much that’s new about Emily herself, but we do learn what it would have been like to be in her world. Anyone looking for easy answers to Wuthering Heights, a brutal and confusing book, or its strange author will be disappointed—but those readers for whom the mystery is the point will certainly be pleased.

#1316. Germany's population is shrinking, but not everywhere

By 2045, Germany will have more older people, fewer workers and a smaller population overall. Yet some regions will defy this trend. Their success depends on how much they have to offer.

Germany's population is expected to decline from 83.5 million in 2025 to around 81.8 million by 2045, according to a new study from the Berlin Institute for Population and Development (Berlin-Institut für Bevölkerung und Entwicklung), in collaboration with the Wüstenrot Foundation, a nonprofit that supports research on housing, urban development and social change.

The decline in population alone is not the central challenge. More significant is the continuing rise in the number of retirees alongside a steadily shrinking workforce. Across Germany, the working-age population is projected to decrease by 4.7% by 2045.

"The baby boomer generation is retiring, while significantly smaller cohorts are following. Birth rates are falling, and immigration is not sufficient to offset the growing surplus of deaths over births," said institute President Catherina Hinz when presenting the study in Berlin.

The term "baby boomers" refers to those born in the 1960s, when annual births exceeded 1 million. Thereafter, the birth rate fell sharply, effectively halving.

Germany's population may be shrinking, but according to the study, this trend is far from uniform across the country. In the years ahead, a region's economic attractiveness, and the financial strength that comes with it, will become even more important than it is today.

"Some regions will lose substantial numbers of residents and workers in the years ahead, while others continue to grow. Even within eastern Germany, southern Germany and rural areas, the demographic and economic divide is widening," said Hinz.

"Demographic change is unfolding before our very eyes, and its effects are being felt across the country," Hinz added. "Its consequences can still be mitigated, but they can no longer be avoided. And the longer we wait, the narrower our scope for action will become."

Hinz's warning is echoed by Frederick Sixtus of the Berlin Institute. Almost every region now records more deaths than births, he noted, a consequence of decades of low birth rates. The implication is clear: Migration, both inward and outward, now shapes a region's demographic trajectory. Over recent decades, immigration from abroad has repeatedly helped stabilize population trends. Yet migration alone is no longer enough to halt the aging of society.

According to the study, several factors are critical if a region is to remain attractive to people relocating from abroad or from other parts of Germany: High-quality jobs offering good pay and working conditions, a sufficient supply of skilled workers for local businesses, financially robust municipalities, reliable childcare, and efficient transport and mobility infrastructure.

Many major cities are expected to continue to grow in the years ahead, according to Manuel Slupina of the Wüstenrot Foundation. The study projects that Berlin will lead the way, with the capital's population increasing by almost 10% by 2045. Other cities expected to attract people include Hamburg, Frankfurt am Main, Munich and Düsseldorf in western Germany, as well as Leipzig, Dresden and Potsdam in the east.

The study's authors argue that the familiar distinction between urban and rural areas does not hold true everywhere. The hinterlands of many major cities are also expected to grow. While young people are increasingly drawn to metropolitan centers, families with children tend to settle in the surrounding areas.

Among the places likely to lose population are several large cities in western Germany, including Bremen, Duisburg, Dortmund and Kaiserslautern, whose economic appeal has gradually diminished. At the bottom of the ranking are many sparsely populated regions, a large number of them in eastern Germany.

Twelve of the 20 districts and independent cities with the weakest overall ratings are now located in eastern Germany, up from five in the previous assessment in 2019. According to the study, these regions are also expected to see the steepest declines in their working-age populations, with losses of up to 18% by 2045. The authors conclude that competition for workers is therefore set to become one of the key demographic challenges of the decades ahead.

In this competition, the southern German states of Bavariaand Baden-Württemberg are regarded as having the strongest prospects. Bavaria ranks particularly highly thanks to its robust economy, which is thriving across much of the state, and its strong labor market. Women, older people and people without German citizenship are also more likely to be employed in Bavaria than elsewhere. The ranking shows that economically successful regions are more effective at integrating different population groups into the labor market than weaker ones.

#1319. Hungary’s Rebirth

The fall of Trump’s favorite dictator—and what comes next.

With the midterm elections underway and the MAGA movement primed to face a reckoning of its own making, the Democratic Party is beginning to wrestle with the question of how to rescue the country from authoritarianism. Hungary—where, in April, voters ousted the long-standing far-right strongman Viktor Orbán, along with most of the members of parliament from his Fidesz party—offers insights into the opportunities and challenges that lie ahead.

As a journalist and author who has covered Donald Trump’s mobster-style leadership in both its 1.0 version and its more extreme 2.0 form, I believe that Hungary’s example is apropos, especially given Trump’s admiration for Orbán and his personal endorsement of the autocrat’s failed reelection campaign. So this summer, I traveled to Budapest, the capital of Hungary and long the heart of resistance to Orbánism, to see how the new government, under Prime Minister Péter Magyar, is faring in its attempts at re-democratization after nearly two decades of autocratic rule.

My first stop was the radio station Klubrádió, one of the few Hungarian media outlets that remained independent during Orbán’s final term in office. Even as Orbán expanded his control over state media as part of his project to unravel the country’s democracy, Klubrádió bucked this assault and provided a vital platform to a new generation of political leaders.

The station is housed in an elegant and eminently Mitteleuropean stone-fronted building near Budapest’s Heroes Square. A vast open space, the square is home to several huge statues of military heroes from Hungarian history riding on horseback, including the country’s founding monarch and his successors in what became a 1,000-year kingdom, as well as towering figures in the Austro-Hungarian Empire, a dual monarchy that united Hungary and Austria into a huge, ethnically diverse Central European mega-state. At the end of World War I, that mega-state splintered into numerous smaller ones. Hungary veered toward fascism and, in the post–World War II era, became a Soviet satellite state. The country liberalized with the breakup of the Soviet Union in 1989, but this period of democracy would prove to be a brief interlude before Orbán and his Fidesz party won power in 1998 and, more durably, again in 2010.

During his five terms in office—four of them consecutive—Orbán succeeded in gerrymandering districts to lock in majorities for Fidesz. He also gained control of the judicial system, which then rubber-stamped Fidesz policies; stifled the free press; and handed the reins of the economy to Fidesz-affiliated oligarchs. Orbán’s actions made the words on the plaque in front of the grandiose, neoclassical Museum of Fine Arts especially poignant. They were from a sermon that Pope John Paul II had given in the country two years after the fall of the Berlin Wall: “Be  mindful of the blessings that freedom, which you have definitively secured, means for your future. Cherish it and make good use of your liberty.”

At Klubrádió’s airy, plant-filled third-floor offices, György Bolgár has just finished his live call-in radio show, Let’s Talk About It, which he hosts every weekday afternoon. It was on this show that, in 2024, a relatively unknown politician, then-43-year-old Péter Magyar, nourished his political ambitions. In the spring of that year, during the darkest days of Orbán’s iron rule, Bolgár invited Magyar for an interview. A former Fidesz insider, Magyar had recently begun criticizing the government in online comments, catching the attention of Hungarians desperate for the country to reclaim the democratic potential unleashed in 1989. Unlike Orbán, who had consistently refused to appear on the program, Magyar accepted the invitation and soon became a regular guest. He hammered home the need to end the culture of cronyism and corruption that had enabled Orbán’s inner circle to enrich themselves at the public’s expense, and to invest more in education, transportation, and healthcare. He explained that in order to do so, a new government led by Magyar and his upstart Tisza Party would need to access the €16 billion in European Union funds that Brussels had frozen as a response to Orbán’s autocratic rule.

Magyar’s anti-corruption, pro-democracy message arrived at exactly the right moment for Hungarians eager for a political reset. That he was youthful, telegenic, and supremely self-confident didn’t hurt either.

The host of Let’s Talk About It understood all too well that Magyar was far from left-wing. On some key issues, including immigration, Magyar’s policy proposals largely mirrored Orbán’s intolerant approach. That wasn’t a surprise, since Magyar had spent his early career as a Fidesz insider: For nearly a decade, he’d lived in Brussels as a diplomatic liaison to the EU, and for many years he was married to Judit Varga, Orbán’s pliant minister of justice. But talking with him week in and week out, Bolgár came to believe that Magyar had experienced a road-to-Damascus conversion regarding the need to scrub the political system clean of Fidesz’s corruption.

“It’s a people’s party” was the way that Bolgár described his guest’s vision for Tisza, which had been a small and powerless opposition party until Magyar joined it in 2024. Magyar was promising a genuine social compact with the people and a return of decency to politics. “His intention is to restore some kind of balance,” Bolgár tells me. Even the name of the party, an acronym in Hungarian for “Respect and Freedom,” signified a desire for a fresh start after Orbán.

To defeat the then-61-year-old autocrat, Magyar knew he needed to build an ideologically diverse coalition united around the popular disgust with Fidesz’s corruption and cronyism, as well as the economic malaise triggered by Orbán’s fight with the EU; a burgeoning scandal over the presidential pardon of someone who had attempted to cover up years of sexual abuse by the director of a children’s home in rural Hungary, which belied Fidesz’s claim to be the party of family values; and the regime’s escalating assault on the rule of law and freedom of the press. “He understood the political opening here,” Bolgár says. “He was convincing. He provided something no other opposition politician could provide in the first 14 years of the Orbán regime: He could provide hope.”

In the months that followed his initial interview, Magyar appeared regularly on the Klubrádió show, and in the spring of 2026, he pulled off what many regarded as something close to an election miracle. With 80 percent of the country’s voters flocking to the polls—enough to swamp all of Fidesz’s gerrymandering efforts—the new Tisza Party leader ousted Orbán and established himself as a model for anti-autocratic movements around the world. “It [was] really astonishing,” Bolgár says. “Nobody would have believed it.”

Bolgár knows firsthand the costs of living under an autocratic ruler bent on destroying civil society. After honing his English-language and broadcasting skills as a US correspondent for Hungarian state radio in the dying days of Communist rule, when he’d worked out of an office in Cliffside Park, New Jersey, Bolgár had returned to Hungary, where he brought some of Rush Limbaugh’s methods—albeit in the service of a more social-democratic message—to the newly liberalized airwaves in his homeland. But in 1994, during Orbán’s first administration, he was abruptly fired as part of a purge of hundreds of journalists who were critical of the Hungarian leader.

Bolgár was rehired in the interlude between Orbán’s first and subsequent terms in office, then fired once more as conservative attacks on the media picked up steam. In 2010, when Orbán returned as prime minister, Fidesz passed a law in parliament that placed the media under the control of a quiescent National Media and Communications Authority, allowing fines to be imposed on outlets that were deemed critical of the government. The new Orbán administration used state media outlets to push Fidesz propaganda; stripped hostile TV and radio stations of their broadcasting rights; encouraged pro-Orbán oligarchs to purchase newspapers, magazines, TV and radio stations, and even some Internet sites; and then heavily subsidized those pro-regime outlets by funneling huge sums of government money their way via the purchase of overpriced ads.

Ostracized by his former employers at the state radio outlets, Bolgár landed at the private Klubrádió station—but the attacks followed him there. In February 2021, the station was deprived of its broadcast frequency as the Fidesz government, by then positioning itself at the head of an international hard-right nationalist movement, turned the screws ever tighter on what remained of the free press. Bolgár, who is now 80, ended up being exiled along with many of his colleagues to the Internet, where, he says, his show now gets up to 80,000 listeners a day.

Despite the government’s ruthless crackdown on the print media, TV, and radio, it never succeeded in controlling the online space, perhaps because Orbán himself never fully appreciated how influential online media could be. Magyar, however, saw its promise early on: Much like Trump the insurgent in 2016, he cultivated an extremely loyal online following. Before he appeared on Let’s Talk About It, Magyar had begun fine-tuning ways on social media to expose the fissures within the Fidesz government and to argue that it was irredeemably rotten, building up a huge following on Facebook in particular. He then announced his opposition to the government on a popular Hungarian YouTube channel, Partizán, while talking with its founder, Márton Gulyás, who had built a career on YouTube during the final years of Fidesz rule by interviewing people and investigating stories that the government and its allies in the legacy media preferred to ignore. “Certain things have to be said now,” Magyar declared in that interview, before laying out the crisis of legitimacy faced by Orbán’s regime and describing how Fidesz’s propaganda was unraveling what was left of the country’s democratic institutions. “The prime minister is the sun in the Fidesz solar system,” he continued, detailing the cult of personality that surrounded Orbán—one that everyone knew existed but that no political insider had dared to articulate before.

Sitting in a fashionable bakery in the center of Budapest, Gulyás—who was born three years before the fall of the Berlin Wall—told me that Magyar’s style came across as a breath of fresh air. “When the interview was on-air, I saw the numbers of live viewers,” he recalls over a large croissant and a glass of orange juice. In the days that followed, their conversation was watched by more than 2.5 million people—an extraordinary number, given that Hungary’s population is slightly less than 10 million—and it catapulted Magyar overnight from relative obscurity to a national sensation.

It also marked the moment that Orbán’s public image began to crack.

Before finally losing the prime ministership this past April, Orbán had conducted, for more than a decade, a master class in state capture. The authoritarian strongman found ways to control and leverage the country’s political, academic, economic, and media ecosystems to consolidate his power and channel resources to himself and his inner circle. Everyone within these ecosystems—as well as many outside them—knew they had been corrupted, but enough people benefited from the corruption to keep Fidesz’s winning streak going.

Orbán turned civil society on its head, promoting a no-holds-barred form of political extremism that he labeled “illiberal democracy.” In reality, this meant a form of exclusionary hard-right populism characterized by the demonization of immigrants, the LGBTQ population, and other marginalized groups, wrapped up in the language of Christian nationalism and family values. In her book Tainted Democracy, the former liberal MP Zsuzsanna Szelényi wrote that because of Orbán’s efforts to amend the country’s Constitution and personalize its politics, “Hungary came to acquire a level of concentration of power never seen in European democracies.” This agenda was never popular in urban areas—Budapest’s city government remained liberal and pro-European throughout Orbán’s tenure—or with young people and intellectuals. But, as with Trump in the United States, it appealed to many rural residents, working-class voters who felt sidelined by the priorities of the previous governments, and elites who found ways to benefit financially from Fidesz’s policies.

It’s no accident that Trump and his henchmen—as well as America’s latter-day oligarchs, leading conservative media figures such as Tucker Carlson, and hard-right coordinating bodies like the Conservative Political Action Conference—have long lionized Orbán. In his multiple election victories, they saw a template for attacking what they decried as a decadent “woke” culture, for injecting Christian nationalism into public life, for locking out immigrants, and for dismantling the administrative state, as Steve Bannon has long advocated.

When, in the weeks leading up to the election, it became clear how politically vulnerable Orbán was, MAGA leaders launched an all-out effort to get him over the finish line, with Vice President JD Vance flying to Budapest to stump for the prime minister. Vance and Orbán drew parallels between the United States and Hungary in their speeches, with Orbán saying about Trump’s agenda, “Our solutions are similar for fighting liberals: God, family, homeland.”

For Trump, Orbán represented a beachhead from which to pursue his increasingly nativist, anti-EU agenda. Orbán’s status as a thorn in the side of EU bureaucrats delighted Trump—and thus his downfall was a painful setback for MAGA ideologues. It may also have been a warning sign. As David Koranyi of the nonprofit Action for Democracy told NPR, “You can rewrite the constitution, you can capture public media, you can gerrymander election districts, but as long as people still enjoy the freedom to vote and to vote for whomever they want to, these systems can be reversed under the right circumstances.”

For years, Orbán’s opponents had searched for a pathway to unity that would allow them to overcome all of the structural advantages that Fidesz had baked into the political process. In Magyar, the various opposition parties and their supporters saw a charismatic leader who could take the fight to Orbán. “He was capable of reenergizing people and giving them the hope that change is feasible,” Gulyás says.

Dávid Bedő, who was elected to parliament in 2022 at the age of 29, was also impressed, despite the fact that, as a member of the left-wing Momentum movement, he disagrees with Magyar on many issues. Magyar “surrounds himself with the right people, and that’s very important,” Bedő says. “I know quite a few of them personally. They are not yes-men.” Leading up to the election, Bedő was one of several MPs who agreed to stand aside and make way, under a unity banner, for the election of a Tisza candidate. All over the country, sitting MPs as well as neophyte candidates for open seats made a similar decision, sacrificing their careers, at least in the short term, for a higher good: to deliver a large-enough majority to allow the opposition to undo the worst of Fidesz’s policies.

Despite the fact that Orbán and his cronies had spent more than a decade stacking the deck in their favor, Magyar’s campaign picked up steam, and as thousands of “Tisza Island” volunteers spread his message into Orbán’s strongholds in the rural heartlands and began peeling off more and more supporters of Fidesz, it became clear that the Tisza leader had a real chance at victory.

On the eve of the election, Magyar’s closing rally in Debrecen, the country’s second-largest city, drew a large crowd estimated at roughly 10,000 people; Orbán’s rally, on the grounds of the imposing castle on the Buda side of the Danube River in Budapest, attracted barely 3,000. The next day, April 12, 2026, Hungarian voters went to the polls in record numbers. That night, as the votes were tallied, it became clear not only that Magyar had won but that Tisza had secured an electoral landslide—one that Orbán had no way of chalking up to fraud. Hours after the polls closed, he meekly conceded defeat. There would be no equivalent of January 6, 2021, in Hungary. A month later, there was a peaceful transfer of power, and the new Tisza government rapidly set to work implementing its vision.

For liberal Budapest, which had long been held hostage to the conservative cultural leanings of Fidesz’s rural base, it was as if a great weight had been lifted. The year before, the government had attempted to ban the city’s Gay Pride march, going so far as to deploy drones equipped with facial-recognition software to identify the participants and potentially impose criminal penalties on them. In response, hundreds of thousands of defiant city residents had flooded the grand streets of the city center to take part in the banned demonstration. Now, under a new government, the festivities would take place without incurring the wrath of those in power, marking the start of a much broader liberalization that was taking hold in the city. In late June, huge crowds flooded Budapest’s streets, this time not in defiance but in celebration, not only of LGBTQ pride but of what many saw as a rebirth of their country’s democracy.

Indeed, even as it was still staffing its ministries, Magyar’s government immediately set to work negotiating new anti-corruption measures with the EU, including an agreement to allow Hungary to participate in the European Public Prosecutor’s Office. These steps would enable the €16 billion in frozen EU funds to be quickly sent Hungary’s way. By late June, Magyar’s team had unveiled plans to set up a new agency that would be charged with identifying and recovering public assets that had been stolen by Fidesz elites, as well as five parliamentary committees to investigate Orbán-era corruption. As László Andor, once the European Union’s commissioner for employment, social affairs, and inclusion, told me, there was a growing pressure to prosecute the former prime minister, as well as those members of his inner circle who had amassed their own personal financial empires.

“It’s classic anti-corruption machinery,” says Márta Pardavi, cochair of the Hungarian Helsinki Committee, a human-rights organization, sitting on a red-leather armchair in her second-floor office in the Central European University’s main building. “It’s not inventing the wheel—it’s implementing practices from elsewhere and enforcing existing Hungarian legislation.”

The new government also quickly axed the advertising subsidies to the Fidesz-aligned press and put the state media outlets on notice that the old ways of doing business no longer applied. One after another, the components of the propaganda machine disintegrated, with many outlets folding and others firing their editors and reinventing their newsrooms. The pro-Fidesz oligarchs’ grip on the media was broken—and in a remarkably short period of time. Just one month after the transfer of power, Hungary’s media landscape had been upended, with the restoration of a freedom of the press and a marketplace of ideas that were starting to resemble those of other EU countries once again.

But the reforms went well beyond simply reestablishing a competitive media environment. Faced with a Fidesz-era president who refused to resign and the upper echelons of a court system still dominated by Orbán’s appointees and loyalists, Tisza announced legislation to amend the Constitution and allow the new government to dismiss the president. Party leaders also teased the notion of one day moving to a system of direct election for the ceremonial head of state. And they unveiled a campaign, “Cleansing Fire,” to remove the top echelons of the judiciary and permit lower-level judges to elect the president of the National Office of Judges. This included legislation to enforce age limits on the judiciary as a way to clear out the old guard of pro-Fidesz judges who would otherwise seek to block many of Tisza’s reforms. Additional legislation imposes term limits on members of parliament—including the prime minister—so as to prevent the accumulation of power that Orbán had achieved over 16 years of continuous rule. The party has also floated the idea of a referendum on a broader revision of the constitutional framework to clear out two decades of amendments, regulations, and legislation that Fidesz had pushed through as it remade the country in its own image. (The Tisza press office did not reply to repeated requests for comment from ministers and government advisers.)

Other changes were aimed at keeping the Tisza coalition intact after its initial job of defeating Fidesz at the ballot box had been accomplished. For young people concerned with their education, the government quickly began integrating the country’s universities into the continent-wide Erasmus system, which would allow Hungarian students to study at institutions all over the EU. And for parents, it embraced a center-left proposal to double child-benefit payments.

This was an ambitious agenda, and one that not every analyst was convinced Tisza could fully implement. “You have to convert from a Trabant to a Porsche while it is on the move,” says Endre Sik of the Center for Social Sciences, who’s old enough to remember when Soviet tanks rolled into Budapest in 1956, crushing an earlier moment of potential reform. Magyar was promising a clean break with the past, something that Sik felt was well-nigh impossible. “When you rebuild a house,” he says, “you use the scaffolding of the previous one simply because it was there.” Purge the entire elite, and you’re left with no expertise, no scaffolding; retain too many of the old elite, and you ultimately risk preserving the previous government’s structures of cronyism and corruption. And even if you can thread that particular needle, he adds, you may not completely eliminate the temptation to revive Orbánism.

“To win the election, it took two years of hard work and a few months of campaigning,” Sik tells me. “To change the political environment, it’s relatively simple. But to change the value system of the society, there is no way to do it in months or even in years.”

A similar refrain—that of optimism tempered by skepticism—is frequently heard in Hungary’s political circles these days. There’s a surging desire for reform, but also a nagging sense that things could still head south. For Eszter Kováts, a political scientist at the CEU Democracy Institute and a fellow at the University of Vienna, Tisza faced a damned-if-you-do, damned-if-you-don’t scenario: If the party didn’t quickly take advantage of its parliamentary supermajority, it would risk squandering a unique chance to undo Orbánism. But if it did move full-speed ahead, it would risk trampling important constitutional protections and eviscerating deliberative processes—and might even acquire a taste for a bulldozer approach to politics that could persist beyond the current de-Orbánization moment. “It’s hard to believe, on the basis of what we see, that Tisza will stop—that it will do this only for restoring the rule of law,” Kováts says. “What is the basis for trust? That is why we like liberal democracy: because you can’t trust politicians to be the good kings.”

Whether Magyar succeeds in his efforts to de-Orbánize the Hungarian political system—and whether he can do so without replicating at least some of the authoritarian tendencies of the Fidesz era—will have huge global ramifications. In the United States, the forces opposing Donald Trump responded with glee to Orbán’s downfall. And Democrats hoping to take back Congress in the midterms and use that victory as a springboard for the 2028 presidential election clearly see Magyar’s big-tent, pro-democracy strategy as one to emulate over the coming months. The night of Hungary’s elections, Democratic Senate minority leader Charles Schumer issued a warning, as quoted by Reuters: “Pay attention, Donald Trump. ​Wannabe dictators wear out their welcome.” If the Democrats win Congress this November—as well as the White House two years later—on the back of popular revulsion with Trump’s corruption and autocratic behavior, the party will likely be closely studying the early months of Magyar’s new government.

Dávid Bedő, the former member of the Hungarian parliament, is optimistic. “It looks liberating,” he says. “After the election, the crumbling of the old system began. The superficial power, [Fidesz] already lost; the propaganda machine already fell apart. Most of the propaganda outlets are closing up shop.”

For Márton Gulyás, however—despite his elation at Orbán’s defeat and the country’s rapid reimagining of its politics—it’s crucial that the defenders of democracy remain vigilant, even with Tisza’s popularity continuing to increase during the first months of the new government. The YouTube journalist, whose 2024 interview with Magyar kick-started this improbable journey, is slightly more ambivalent than Bedő about the early months of Magyar’s prime ministership. “I’m deeply moved by the beliefs of the people and the willingness of the people to be part of the change,” he says, speaking slowly as he sorts through his feelings. “I really enjoy the times right now in Hungary, because most of the people are really optimistic. But managing the perception is one thing, and democratizing a country in a substantive way is different. I need more proofs and signs of that happening in order to give [Magyar] my green light.” He stops, takes another bite of his croissant, and then continues: “When it comes to politics, suspicion is always necessary and always a good strategy, because the stakes are so high. If we are not suspicious, we are not doing our job.”

But security researchers are warning that the situation isn’t over yet, and that a large number of systems around the world remain unpatched and vulnerable to hacks and data theft.

This is also unlikely to be the last major internet software scare, with some expecting the advent of AI to rapidly uncover as-yet-undiscovered vulnerabilities that are waiting to be found and abused.

Security researchers at Calif found two bugs in PHP, a programming language widely used around the world for building websites and web apps, both on the internet and hosted locally on company networks, like intranets and internal sites.

PHP is open-source, meaning it’s free and available to be used by anyone for almost any reason. As such, it’s become one of the most popular and broadly used web programming languages on the web today.

The two bugs were first discovered in 2024 but weren’t considered exploitable at the time, and weren’t patched. Security researchers recently found a way to exploit them, prompting them to alert the makers of the software to get them fixed.

Calif alerted the PHP Foundation, which began a period of fixing the bugs and coordinated disclosure. The foundation did this by offering the information and patches about the security issues under a tightly guarded embargo — essentially held confidentially until a specific deadline — to give big companies an opportunity to get ahead of installing the fixes.

PHP2Shell is pretty serious because it can be abused remotely over the internet and without the victim having to click on a link or open a file. This is known as remote code execution, and it’s one of the more damaging kinds of bugs to exploit because they can grant a hacker anywhere in the world effectively full control over an affected and unpatched system.

Worse, these specific PHP bugs make it possible to break into any unpatched system on the internet without needing a password or any other credential over the internet, known as unauthenticated access.

This would allow a malicious hacker to then plant malware on the computer, such as to steal data or simply to maintain a long-term foothold that they can return to later.

The bugs are particularly risky because PHP is wildly popular software, in part because it’s free and relatively easy to use, but also because it’s been around for decades. Around 70% of the web-facing internet relies on PHP, though its usage has been slowly declining over the years in favor of newer, more secure programming languages.

Given its popularity, some of the world’s most popular websites are run on web software that rely on PHP, such as WordPress (which hosts TechCrunch!), as well as Drupal, Joomla, and MediaWiki, which powers online encyclopedia giant Wikipedia.

PHP is just one of many aging but functional technologies that have survived waves of the internet and generations that are now so ubiquitous, they’re akin to the glue that holds much of the internet together. Oftentimes these tools are developed by open source maintainers, who are not paid for the work that they do, and as such cannot afford third-party security reviews — but are still widely relied on by companies who do not contribute financially to the projects.

Security researchers at Calif were credited with discovering the bugs earlier this year as part of a security audit of PHP’s code. Calif was participating in the Hack the Planet initiative by AI model maker OpenAI and cloud security giant Wiz, which subsequently verified the proof-of-concept exploits that Calif made.

In Calif’s write-up (featuring comics — it’s great, check it out), the researchers explained that AI was used in assistance with exploiting the bug, but that the bulk of the work relied on knowledge that the researchers already had, and that they knew PHP’s codebase and exploitation history well.

“No model is simply capable of unearthing ‘all bugs’ with simple prompts. At least for now,”wrote Calif.

Despite efforts to keep details of the bugs under wraps until Wednesday, it does appear that there was some PHP2Shell exploitation prior to the release of patches.

Amazon, which has enormous visibility into online threats due to its size and scale, said in a blog post that it detected exploitation of PHP2Shell about a week before the PHP patches were available, suggesting that someone, somewhere, knew how to exploit the bugs before their details were broadly available. Amazon said it wasn’t clear how the hacker learned of the vulnerabilities, but that defenders should look for evidence of exploitation as early as October 17.

There are a few theories as to what happened. Once a bug is patched, the details of the patch can become immediately apparent, especially if the code — like in PHP’s case — is open source, making it public and inspectable. Exploiting the bugs might not be easy, but knowing where the bug is and how it was fixed can allow malicious hackers to understand where to target and how to develop their exploits.

It’s possible that there was a leak or a breakdown in communication somewhere during the embargo. The open source community is enormous and controlling information in this fashion relies on good-faith.

It’s also possible that hackers discovered the bugs ahead of time, either independently or through espionage.

The PHP Foundation blamed one company for pushing the patches live hours before the embargo period was up, which could have given some attackers a headstart if they obtained details of the patch before others. But this doesn’t account for the week-long activity detected by Amazon.

Researchers say that bugs require a lot of work to become exploitable and weaponizing the bug has been a challenge — but not impossible — which explains why we haven’t seen a scale of mass-exploitation just yet.

But as of late Friday, there are now confirmed compromises and post-exploit activity related to the PHP bug, according to security giants Palo Alto Networks and Trail of Bits. That means the bug was detected being abused to hack in and actively do malicious activity on an as-yet-unknown target.

It’s still early days but it’s an encouraging sign that there haven’t been any signs of mass-exploitation — but we’re not out of the danger-zone just yet.

Matthew Prince, the chief executive of network security and monitoring giant Cloudflare, which also has telemetry into a vast portion of the internet, said this week that it’s seen an exponential number of attempts to exploit the bugs following their disclosure. This gives some perspective as to how quickly malicious actors are seeking to abuse the flaws for their own gains.

PHP isn’t just found on web servers, but also used in developer tools, command line and terminal packages, and other software components used by routers, storage devices, and enterprise security devices, like firewalls and VPNs.

We should expect to see exploitation of PHP2Shell down the line as smaller IT teams and sole developers may be slower to update. There will also be a period in which various companies that rely on and integrate PHP into their own products and software, which will also need updating.

As more devices and systems get updated, the attack surface will reduce in time. But the ripple effects of these bugs could still be felt for years to come, particularly in legacy systems that have no means to be updated.

This was one of those rare internet-stopping days I’ve seen during my time as a security reporter. In 2016, a massive botnet flooded internet registry Dyn with junk traffic causing much of the web’s top services to crawl to a halt. In 2017, North Korea used leaked U.S. government hacking tools to infect the U.K.’s health system with ransomware. And in 2024, a faulty CrowdStrike update sparked a massive global outage causing most of the world’s Windows computers to crash at once.

By all accounts with PHP2Shell, the internet escaped what could have otherwise been an extremely painful and damaging day. But it’s unlikely to be the last major incident to hit the web, and this may be the first in a trend of incidents that exploit weaknesses in legacy technology. Much of this is due to inherent flaws in aging but omnipresent software that haven’t yet been discovered, coupled with AI tools that are making it easier to find flaws.

PHP, much like other web programming languages and technologies from decades ago, are “memory unsafe,” which means they can be attacked through an entire class of vulnerabilities that can force the software to leak or spill sensitive data from the software’s memory storage. The National Security Agency says around 75% of all actively exploited vulnerabilities abuse memory-related flaws, which is to say, hackers use them a lot.

This has helped to prompt a move to using memory-safe languages, like Python, Rust, and Swift, which can’t be abused in the same way that memory vulnerabilities can be. As such, these languages make them broadly immune to a broad swath of vulnerabilities that still affect their predecessors.

But that still leaves an entire chunk of the internet running a bunch of technologies, like PHP, media and video codecs, compression libraries, and other software from decades ago — which, when they break, will have massive implications for the rest of the web.

#1326. 'Ragebait' is the 2026 German Youth Word of the Year

Already named the 2025 Oxford Word of the Year, "rage bait" has also been widely adopted in German slang.

The 2026 Youth Word of the Year, "Ragebait," was announced on Saturday at the Frankfurt Book Fair by publisher PONS Langenscheidt.

The "Jugendwort des Jahres," or German Youth Word of the Year, is an annual campaign that documents the popularity of new slang expressions.

"Ragebait" (in English: rage bait) refers to social media content that is deliberately designed to make people angry, frustrated or morally outraged.

It aims to drive engagement, since the algorithms of platforms like Instagram, TikTok and X reward high-energy interaction. A post that generates a heated debate through angry comments, or that is widely shared due to the emotional reaction it provokes, counts as high engagement, which leads the platforms to push the content to more feeds.

"Ragebait" was on a shortlist of three words for the 2026 Youth Word of the Year, alongside "Peak" and "Crashout."

All three terms are borrowed from English, demonstrating once again the language's significant influence on German youth culture.

Around 1 million young people, aged 11 to 20, have voted since August 28 for their favorite term.

"Ragebait" obtained 36.5% of the votes, followed by 33.8% for "Peak" and 29.7% for "Crashout."

"The close result shows that all three terms have a firm place in the vocabulary of generations Z and Alpha," said Artur Raiser, marketing manager at PONS Langenscheidt, in a press statement. "'Ragebait' held a slight lead from the start," but only by a narrow margin throughout the voting period, he added.

"It remained an exciting race for us at the publishing house, too, until the very last moment," said Raiser

"Crashout" (used by young Germans as "crashout gehen," or "going crash out") originally comes from the English "to crash out," which traditionally means to fall asleep very quickly due to exhaustion.

But in newer internet slang, popularized on gaming and social media platforms, "crashing out" means having an intense emotional breakdown, losing control of one's temper or reacting with sudden, uncontrolled anger or stress. It is often used with a touch of humor, to emphasize that the reaction might be somewhat exaggerated.

As for the other shortlisted word, "Peak," it refers to something that is the absolute best. In German youth slang, it functions like an adjective: "That was peak football" or "that is peak comedy."

PONS Langenscheidt, a publishing company that specializes in language reference works, has been selecting the German Youth Word of the Year since 2008 — originally via a jury, and since 2020 through an online vote. Offensive, racist or discriminatory terms are not permitted. The multi-stage public nomination and voting process for next year's word will start in the summer of 2027.

"Das crazy" was selected last year, while "Aura" was the Youth Word of the Year 2024.

#1329. The Bookstore Revolution in Taiwan’s Conservative Heartlands

After Taiwan legalized same-sex marriage, gender-themed bookstores are working to build the social acceptance that the law alone couldn’t deliver.

Tucked inside a labyrinthine wet market in Taichung, a city of 2.8 million in west-central Taiwan, A Room of One’s Own looks less like a landmark bookshop than the home of a reclusive aesthete. At its doorstep, an acrylic sign asks visitors to take off their shoes before entering the narrow, three-story townhouse. Owner Shan-Wen Tsai greets customers from behind a café counter. Behind her hangs a postcard collage with Virginia Woolf beside Frida Kahlo. Before her, a packed bookshelf displays Stefan Bollmann’s Women Who Read Are Dangerous.

“It’s a little cut off from the outside world,” Tsai said of the bookshop’s location. “I kept a bit of distance from the outside—and that was the distance I wanted to maintain at the time.”

Tsai initially avoided outside attention to protect the mission of the independent, gender-themed bookshop. She founded A Room of One’s Own in 2009, naming it after Woolf’s 1929 essay. At the time, LGBTQ groups in Taichung were largely underground and frequently ostracized by the cultural mainstream, she said. Witnessing the expanding queer and feminist scene in Taipei, Tsai, who is bisexual, wanted to usher in gender resources to the more conservative Central Taiwan. She also longed to bring its underground community “above ground.” Most of all, she wanted to do so with books.

“I treated books as a well-intentioned medium for advancing a cause,” she said. “I felt that if I wanted to push a new issue forward, a bookstore would be a pretty good way to go about it.”

Over the last 30 years, gender bookstores specializing in feminist, queer, and trans-inclusive texts have become a cultural fixture across the socially conservative island. In Taipei, Fembooks, founded in 1994 by women’s activists, stands as one of the oldest feminist hubs in the Chinese-speaking world. GinGin, an LGBTQ-themed bookstore that was open from 1999 until 2024, was the first of its kind in the Sinophone world. Beyond the capital, bookstores find themselves serving as gathering spaces, resource centers, and cradles of taboo knowledge, paving the way for a hard-won legislative milestone.

In May 2019, a decade after Tsai had founded A Room, Taiwan appeared in international headlines as the first nation in Asia to legalize same-sex marriage. Queer communities and allies celebrated what appeared to be a triumphant culmination of a two-and-a-half-year legislative struggle. Scholars scrutinized its success and the backlash that followed.

Yet, as gay rights moved towards institutionalization, new cultural flashpoints surfaced in Taiwanese society, much as they have in the United States: Should gender be included in school curricula? Can gay couples adopt? What bathrooms should transgender people use? Across the island, where progressive values regularly confront Confucian norms and conservative Christian movements, NGOs and activists strained to turn legal recognition into social acceptance. But the bookstores offer a different model of everyday engagement—public spaces that treat gender as a subject of civic discussion and education, rather than partisan debate.

For the three gender bookstore owners I spoke with outside Taipei, advancing LGBTQ rights in conservative communities is an exercise in quiet persistence. As each faces local resistance, they decide what it means to stay open among neighbors who oppose their stance. Customers wander in, some linger, and a few carry books and conversations out. The owners, however, struggle to quantify how much of a tangible difference they are making. With no ballots to win and no headlines to earn, many question how to advance a cause with no finish line—and what to offer the people who do walk through the door.

Between 2009 and the mid-2010s, A Room of One’s Own functioned much like its name: a gathering place for the LGBTQ community’s own. Tsai joined a bisexual group called Bi the Way and, through it, fell into a network of the city’s queer circles. After it opened, A Room quickly became their designated home base. “The connections were incredibly tight-knit,” Tsai reminisced, holding a glass mug of black tea in front of a wall of bookshelves organized by themes—gay, trans, society and culture, motherhood.

Many of Tsai’s peers were estranged from their biological families. Thus, the group found a chosen family in each other. “Our closeness surpassed every kind of relationship,” Tsai said. She called this era “utopia-like.”

For a community that largely existed underground in the early aughts, having a physical gathering place at A Room mattered more than Tsai initially expected. She distinctly remembers one longtime customer telling her the store’s existence transformed how she viewed her hometown. “There’s a real feeling that a bookstore can open up another world,” Tsai said. She wanted customers to flip through texts and experience the kind of epiphany she felt after reading Simone de Beauvoir’s The Second Sex. “That process of opening up a new perspective—I genuinely felt it was wondrous.”

For many, A Room also carried immense representational value. One young visitor, Tsai recalled, passed through the market one day and stopped in her tracks after seeing A Room’s rainbow decor. Stunned, she stood at the entrance and wept. “She couldn’t believe that there was a place devoted to these issues. So she got overwhelmed, and it took a long time before she worked up the nerve to walk in.” Tsai learned that the young woman came from a Taichung family that kept her under tight control. She wasn’t able to move out until her early 30s.

That woman’s situation was hardly anomalous. Tsai said she knew many young people who, years into adulthood, still had curfews and weren’t permitted to live on their own. She also knew many who had overcome “enormous” pressure to love and live freely. Working within a society that hewed firmly to traditional family values and gender roles, Tsai focused on what she could control: broadening the public’s acceptance of progressive values, and urging her community to be daring, but safe. The bookstore, she decided in its early years, should be a place that encourages women and LGBTQ people to challenge the status quo and take a leap toward the life they want.

“What our existence offers is this: Someone with few resources can still hold onto the idea that ‘someday, I can rely on myself,’” she said. “‘I don’t have to be bound by what other people think of me. I still have a choice.’”

As Taiwan’s marriage-equality movement crested in the mid-2010s, Tsai noticed the climate shifting. In May 2017, its constitutional court ruled that the civil code, which defines marriage as the union between a woman and a man, violated core constitutional tenets. The high court gave the Legislative Yuan, Taiwan’s supreme law-making body, two years to pass legislation that addresses the ruling. If they failed to act within two years, same-sex couples would automatically be able to marry.

Opposition arrived quickly. A conservative Christian family-values group, Coalition for the Happiness of our Next Generation, pushed for a popular referendum on marriage and gender education, which would go to voters in November 2018. Pro-gay-marriage groups soon began signature collection for their own referendum questions, while overseas donations poured in. For supporters of LGBT rights, Taiwan became a global battleground for marriage equality.

Stigma surrounding gender and sexuality quickly loosened. Tsai noticed that older relatives who once seemed adamant on the subject registered that “the world is changing.” Progress, however, created its own disorientation. Within the queer community, Tsai said, the old certainty of “us against the world” no longer held. She watched her circle disintegrate as peers sought support in other places—chief among them, the Internet. At the same time, she felt society more generally was lost; gender and marital norms had shifted, but no new order had yet emerged. “In the process of searching for new answers, people were very confused,” she remembered.

Tsai began adapting A Room’s mission and audience to fit the times. The LGBTQ question was no longer just a minority group’s personal turmoil. It was a civic issue. “The time has come for me to break through what you might call our own in-group bubble, and start having a dialogue with the general public,” she said. “There are a lot of issues now that I must talk about directly with them.”

Tsai hosted events and workshops meant for a wider demographic. She even entered electoral politics in 2023, running as a third-party candidate for a Taichung seat in the Legislative Yuan. “I treated this election as a good opportunity to reach outward, a strategy for letting more of the public see gender issues,” she said. Gender equity, children’s rights, and education were among her top campaign priorities. She lost to the Nationalist Party candidate.

The limelight on same-sex marriage also invited clearer upsets. An hour north of Taichung by high-speed rail, Mia Chang runs Langhuan, a gender-themed bookstore in Taoyuan. After the 2017 constitutional court ruling, Chang, who identifies as lesbian, dove into public-facing activism and registered Langhuan as an official signature-collection site for pro-LGBTQ petitions ahead of the 2018 referendum. Taiwan required 280,000 signatures (1.5 percent of the electorate) to get a question on the ballot. “We did not hesitate,” Chang said.

The infamous referendum eventually included five questions addressing LGBTQ rights, education, and same-sex marriage, proposed by two rival camps. The conservative coalition put three on the ballot, including one asking whether marriage in the Civil Code should remain a union between a man and a woman and keep same-sex unions out (the Central Election Commission approved the questions, despite the May 2017 ruling). The pro-LGBTQ side put forward two, one of which asked for same-sex marriage to be included in the Civil Code. In the end, 72 percent of voters sided with the conservatives on the marriage question. The legislature opted not to amend the civil code, but instead passed a “special law” in May 2019. It legalized gay marriage but, in Chang’s opinion, fell short of full equality.

For Chang, the post-referendum setback, though demoralizing, was purpose-affirming. Similar to Tsai, Chang saw her home city, Taoyuan, as an LGBTQ resource desert. She wants to run Langhuan until women and LGBTQ groups are “equal to the point where it’s no longer needed.” The results showed that she must carry on. “There’s a lot of work left to do,” she said.

Public-facing political involvement also invited covert backlash against Langhuan. During the referendum period, conservative Christian and family-values advocacy groups had risen in vocal opposition to same-sex marriage legalization. Chang remembered members of the church coming into the store, pretending to browse, and slipping anti-gay-marriage pamphlets between book pages. “I accidentally found them later, while I was reorganizing the books,” she said. “It was actually pretty funny, really quite discreet.”

Less-reserved opposition also surfaced. Across the coast in Hualien, a picturesque, seismically restless seaside city, Peggy Chiang opened Haihao Bookshop (孩好書屋), or Good for Kids, as a birthday present in 2017 to her then-1-year-old son. Chiang, who is bisexual, knew she wanted to set up a dedicated gender section in the store, but never intended it to become a defining theme. That changed right before the referendum period, when an anti-gay-marriage coalition called for a boycott against her business.

Just before its grand opening in 2017, Good for Kids agreed to cohost and sponsor a civic lecture series on gender organized by Hualien’s district office. After the promotional poster went out, a local “family values” organization altered and recirculated it—the new version condemned the event and cast the list of partner venues as businesses to boycott. Good for Kids was one of them. “We thought, ‘OK, we hadn’t originally intended to position ourselves around this issue [of gender] specifically,’” Chiang said. “But it turned out they basically did our marketing for us.”

Overt gender advocacy was especially fraught in Hualien, one of Taiwan’s most socially and politically conservative cities, Chiang said. Since the early 2010s, local politicians and family advocacy groups have held large-scale rallies protesting the “undermining” of traditional family ethics. Chiang remembered a 2013 march opposing an LGBTQ “diverse family formation” draft bill. At the rally, Hualien’s sitting county magistrate, Fu Kun-chi, vowed that if such a bill passed, Hualien’s household registration office would simply refuse to process the paperwork for gay couples.

“That’s just what the social atmosphere in Hualien is like,” Chiang said. “A county magistrate, in a public setting, voluntarily declaring he’d do something illegal.”

If Taoyuan is an LGBTQ resource desert, Hualien is a resource vacuum. Almost all active queer groups were based exclusively at local universities, Chiang said. Among residents, traditional values still reigned. An outspoken LGBTQ advocate, Chiang guides customers she hardly knows to critical resources. She once offered a first-time visitor step-by-step instructions for accessing gender-affirming surgery abroad, in Thailand. She pays close attention to gender issues at the national level—and bears in mind how much Hualien lags behind. Currently, she said, Taiwan is debating topics like transnational marriage and reproductive technology. “That’s the more advanced end of the gender conversation. In Hualien, we’re still stuck at basic gender equality between men and women.”

Still, the very appearance of Good for Kids, which hangs a large rainbow flag above a tatami-style kids’ reading area, repeatedly sparked controversy among local residents. Over the past decade, Chiang has become a veteran at responding to (or ignoring) online vitriol and backlash from relatives and strangers alike. Everyday opposition, though, comes in quieter moments. She recalled an instance when a mother and daughter browsed the store, flipped a sex-education comic book face-down, and left without comment. “There are still a lot of people in Hualien who are afraid of gender or of sex,” she said. “There really is still a very long way to go.”

As a result, Chiang chose not to let gender alone define her bookstore. Today, she curates Good for Kids’ shelves around several overlapping themes that emerged over the past decade: gender, marine conservation, picture books, special education, and civic participation. She hopes the breadth of themes will let customers have accidental encounters with gender. Perhaps, then, certain ideas, questions, or senses of acceptance will take hold. “One step at a time,” she said.

But how do you change minds? Tsai, Chang, and Chiang all responded the way one would expect bookstore owners to: the transformative power of reading. As for those who won’t pick up a book? In-person events that enable civic engagement, earnest interactions, and, crucially, chance encounters.

“Every talk we hold might gather just a little bit of strength,” Chang said. “It builds up slowly.”

All three owners host speaker series, reading groups, courses, and book talks that have grown crucial to their incremental style of activism. Most recently, Tsai led a workshop on The Ethical Slut, based on the book of the same name about open relationships. A few years back, she staged a workshop titled “A Dating Course for Straight Men,” which, to her chagrin, saw zero registrants. “Nobody dared to come. It’s actually hilarious,” she said.

Chang acknowledged that most people who come to Langhuan’s events are LGBTQ community members and allies. But she’s always on the lookout for conversations with people holding opposite beliefs. “What you really hope for, more than anything, is that the issue can spread beyond your camp. That people from outside the bubble actually show up—whatever their motivation, even if they’ve come to stir up trouble.”

One of Chiang’s most vivid memories came after a talk about the history of Taiwan’s gay rights movement. An older woman, a nearby resident who simply wandered in for the event, approached the speaker after the talk, visibly moved. She said she was a devout Christian, but had no idea the gay rights struggle had been so difficult and had lasted for so long. “She told the speaker, ‘You’ve all worked so hard. You really haven’t had it easy.’”

Moments like this make Chang realize what her work is all about. “[Those kinds of attendees] pass a message along to the people around them, to their close friends, their relatives.” Slowly, she hopes, enough voices will permeate the gender desert to create an oasis.

Beside A Room’s signature pink spiral staircase, Tsai explained that doubt and skepticism, more than anything, led to her bookstore’s founding. “I knew from a very young age that things weren’t the way adults told me they were,” Tsai said. “So I’ve always approached what adults told me about gender with a certain sense of doubt.”

The doubts she harbored in her youth led her to search for alternatives to gender and marital norms. “A lot of these gaps, these differences, kept pushing me to go looking for answers,” she said. Andy Burnham hasn’t even been Prime Minister for 100 days and already they’re advising us to stockpile bottled water and toilet paper.

It’s all part of the UK Government’s Be Ready campaign, which wants us to prepare for a national disaster.

Among the recommendations from Whitehall are that we buy bottled water, canned goods and toilet paper.

Time was when that would get you on an MI5 list but, instead of mocking preppers for their paranoia and panic- buying, ministers seemingly want us to follow their example.

The idea behind it is that Britain faces a panoply of risks, from natural disasters to foreign attack, and it’s better to be ready in advance rather than scramble to respond after Armageddon strikes.

I’ll admit, given the stormy conditions in Scotland this weekend, I briefly considered whether my own emergency supplies – a box of LED Halloween jack-o’-lantern candles and three tins of Cock-a-leekie – would be sufficient.

Should I brave the flooded streets and flying wheelie bins for a spot of shelf-clearing? What do you even buy for the end of the world? My instinct would be to push out the boat on mineral water and food tins.

If I’m going to be incinerated by an Iranian nuke, I want to go out surrounded by crates of Vichy Catalan and cans of beluga caviar, not a flask of tap water and Aldi’s own-brand sardines.

Defence Secretary Wes Streeting is the face of the Government's Be Ready campaign

No one objects to the Government promoting preparedness. The problem is the way ministers have gone about it.

By making Defence Secretary Wes Streeting the face of the campaign, it can only heighten public alarm.

We know from the pandemic that over-reaction can be just as dangerous as under-preparedness, and causing panic, particularly among elderly and vulnerable people, is ill-considered and counterproductive.

These groups have enough anxieties without the Government suggesting they get down the shops pronto before all the loo roll is snatched up.

Streeting proffered a cyber attack as one possible crisis, asking: ‘How would we cope if the payment systems at the local supermarket went down?’

By tutting passive-aggressively, I’d imagine, and loudly reassuring other shoppers that our card wasn’t being declined for lack of funds.

‘Those are the sorts of everyday challenges and threats that we could well face,’ Streeting added. ‘And it’s right that we ask the country to be ready for those things and to ask everyone to do their bit.’

This is another reason why it was a mistake to put Streeting front and centre. This is a government which refuses to say when it will meet the target for spending 3 per cent of GDP on defence, despite issuing dire warnings about the sundry threats Britain faces.

If the Government wants to do its bit, it can start by reversing its £2billion cut to the armoured vehicle procurement budget and its decision not to move ahead with the purchase of specialised recovery vehicles for rescuing Boxers that break down or come under fire.

While we’re at it, it’s fairly galling to be lectured on threat readiness when the Government is constantly caught off-guard by illegal boat crossings.

So much of what goes on in politics makes sense only when you realise that the political class considers the average voter dumber than a bollard. The notion that most members of the public are intelligent, capable people with enough common sense and life experience to manage in a crisis is simply beyond many MPs and civil servants.

The Be Ready campaign hits that sweet spot between awareness-raising and nannying where Labour ministers feel most at ease. Assign the public some reading homework in the form of leaflets, then scold them on the contents of their food pantry.

Some politicians and officials enjoyed Covid and the power it brought them. They desperately want another national emergency that puts them in control again.

Were a cyber attack or wildfires or hurricanes to befall Britain tomorrow, we would muddle through. Most families have the basics they need to survive several days stuck indoors and would gladly share food, water and candles with elderly neighbours. We don’t need to be told to do this by government. This is basic human decency.

What is most likely to frighten people out of their senses and get them acting rashly is a government campaign like Be Ready. When those in authority, whom we expect to be calm and level-headed, begin speaking like the apocalypse is upon us, otherwise rational people are bound to over-react.

I wouldn’t advise it, as it happens. We’ve all seen those disaster movies.

The doomsayer who has spent months hoarding food and battening every last hatch always makes some fatal oversight and gets carried off in the tornado or tsunami, or munched on by the zombies, while the leading man, too busy arguing with his ex-wife to notice the signs of impending disaster, saves the day with quick wits and a spot of heroism.

In real life, your best chance of surviving is by keeping the head and letting the emergency services do their jobs. Plus, not living your life in constant expectation of catastrophe.

There are real threats out there and sensible preparations should be made, but beyond that the average person should go about their life as normal.

It’s when people panic that things go wrong, but who wouldn’t panic when the Defence Secretary is talking about supermarkets being crippled and families drawing up escape plans?

Naturally, the Scottish Government has its own version of Be Ready, because of course it does, though its branding is different from the English programme, because of course it is.

When you’re being winched from your roof amid flash-flooding, I suppose you want the reassurance of knowing the helicopter pilot is trained to rescue you in both English and Scots.

Ready Scotland offers almost identical advice to Be Ready, though some of its tips are more ambitious, such as the recommendation that you keep your GP’s phone number written down.

I struggle to get an appointment at the best of times; I doubt a Russian invasion will improve my odds. Truth be told, I’d rather face the Red Army than be interrogated by a doctor’s receptionist.

The advice also urges us to invest in wind-up radios and keep a note of the frequencies of our favourite stations. Good luck with that. Gen-X will have to explain to millennials how a wind-up radio works, and millennials will have to explain to Gen-Z what a radio is.

And I won’t need to know the frequency to find Radio Four. I’ll just keep turning the dial till I hear a news bulletin on how the cyber attacks are affecting non-binary people.

The public doesn’t ask all that much of government. All we want is our tax money spent wisely and our public services sufficiently well-managed that they serve our needs.

Instead we get alarmist campaigns that will do more harm than good and public funds poured into producing websites and pamphlets chock-full of patronising prose. If ministers insist on talking down to the country on disaster preparedness, they should Be Ready for the country to stop listening.

#1336. Operations Plan Germany: How the Bundeswehr trains for NATO collective defense

How prepared is Germany for national defense? The answer lies in the Operations Plan Germany. Military exercises such as "Red Storm Charlie" are designed to put these preparations to the test in realistic scenarios.

In a residential district of Hamburg at midnight, the streets are quiet and deserted. Suddenly, the silence is shattered: Several helicopters circle the night sky, and flashing blue lights illuminate the darkness. A long convoy of vehicles moves through the streets. Soldiers stand atop armored Bundeswehr vehicles, their machine guns at the ready. For local residents, it's an unusual, perhaps even unsettling sight in an otherwise peaceful port city.

"Red Storm Charlie" is the name of the exercise that brought parts of Hamburg, northern Germany's largest city, to a standstill for three days. The Bundeswehr, police and civilian organizations were jointly testing how well-prepared they are to respond in a crisis.

The scenario centers on a deteriorating security situation on NATO's eastern flank. NATO troops, represented in the exercise by Bundeswehr soldiers, arrive at the port of Hamburg, Germany's largest seaport, before moving eastwards through the Hanseatic city.

Acts of sabotage and demonstrations, also included in the scenario, hamper their advance. With around 1,700 participants, including 500 soldiers, the exercise held in late September was the largest of its kind to date.

"Red Storm Charlie" forms part of a classified strategy known as the "Operationsplan Deutschland" (Operations Plan Germany). Through this document, the Bundeswehr is preparing for the possibility of a Russian attack on NATO territory. The Operations Plan Germany is an integral element of NATO's broader defense planning and is closely aligned with the Alliance's overall defense framework.

In the event of war or a major crisis, not only the city of Hamburg but Germany as a whole would assume a pivotal role as a logistics hub for hundreds of thousands of NATO soldiers, weapons and pieces of heavy equipment.

"This is a highly detailed and strictly classified plan that specifies exactly where NATO allies would arrive in Germany, including which ports they would use and which routes would be required for their transit across the country," security expert Aylin Matle of the German Council on Foreign Relations told DW.

The Baltic states are widely regarded as the region where NATO considers a Russian attack most likely. In such a scenario, the alliance would concentrate large troop contingents from its 32 member states there. To reach the region, soldiers from southern and western Europe would need to pass through Germany.

"In concrete terms, this means that around 800,000 soldiers and 200,000 vehicles would have to move through our country within six months. Those are the calculations," said Marcel Bohnert, a Bundeswehr officer who contributed to the first version of the Operations Plan.

Speaking with DW in a personal capacity rather than as a representative of the Bundeswehr, Bohnert explained: "We must ensure that soldiers can use our roads and ports safely. That is why we take care of all their needs: We arrange accommodation, provide food, ensure their security and guide them through our transport network."

The plan also covers transport in the opposite direction, from east to west, including the evacuation of wounded soldiers and civilians fleeing the conflict. Its development was commissioned by Bundeswehr Inspector General Carsten Breuer in the wake of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

As Germany's highest-ranking military officer, Breuer is far from alone in his concern that Russia could target additional countries. Germany is already confronting a wide range of Russian hostile activities, from cyberattacks to acts of sabotage directed against the Bundeswehr.

A new dimension of the threat emerged with the attempted attack at Leipzig/Halle Airport in early August. An explosive-laden drone was deployed there and failed to detonate only through luck. The German government holds Russia responsible.

"The threat is becoming more tangible. It is serious, acute, and increasingly visible. This is exactly what the Russian president wants. Moscow is testing our response," Defense Minister Boris Pistorius of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) told the Bundestag on September 23.

The Bundeswehr is also addressing this threat through the Operations Plan Germany, which is continuously being refined and updated. Lessons learned from exercises such as "Red Storm Charlie" are incorporated into the plan. According to Bohnert, the plan also serves as a deterrent.

"We hope to discourage Russia from further aggression by demonstrating our strength through the rapid buildup of military capabilities and the highly organized, swift deployment of forces to the borders," he said.

How well-prepared is Germany for its role as a military logistics hub? Matle identifies one major vulnerability in the country's ageing and, in some cases, deteriorating infrastructure. Germany, she argued, must address the maintenance and repair of the bridges, roads and transport routes required for troop movements "far more quickly and comprehensively."

"Some of this infrastructure dates back to the Cold War and was not designed to accommodate heavy loads or military equipment," she pointed out.

A further challenge lies in striking the right balance between confidentiality and public involvement. To guard against acts of sabotage, sensitive details such as the precise transit routes for NATO troops must not fall into Russian hands. At the same time, the plan can only succeed through close cooperation between the Bundeswehr and civilian society, including local and regional authorities, the police, and relief organizations.

"Parts of the plan must be publicly accessible because we need support from society, as well as from ministries and governments, to make it work," said Bohnert.

The debate over the confidentiality of the more than 1,000-page document recently resurfaced after the far-right Alternative for Germany(AfD) emerged as the strongest party in recent elections in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt.

Security experts expressed concern that the party, which is classified in part as right-wing extremist and is regarded as Russia-friendly, could pass information from the plan to Moscow if it were to assume the office of state premier.

The Defense Ministry has rejected these concerns, noting that no federal state is given access to the complete document. Instead, the sensitive sections of the Operations Plan are shared only in excerpts and strictly on a need-to-know basis.

According to observers, however, the growing number of hybrid threats is underscoring the need for secrecy. During the "Red Storm Charlie" exercise in Hamburg, accredited journalists were not informed of routes or locations until the very last moment because of concerns over potential disruptions and acts of sabotage.

#1341. Wallhack M-001 Review: When Fun Overrides Specs

Batteries. They're not typically thought of as a significant source of innovation. Most companies have spent the last few years removing mice in favor of more lightweight designs.

That's what's made the return of the replaceable and rechargeable battery surprising—something I once thought to be a lost technology from a bygone era. A few companies have recently realized that the ability to pop out and replace your battery in seconds is a great way to extend mouse life without needing to increase battery size or efficiency.

From the handful of mice I’ve tested with hot-swappable batteries so far, Wallhack does it best. The mouse is designed around the function’s ease-of-use, and everything else comes together to make a mouse that feels not only competitive for gaming, but also well thought-out and satisfying to use in everyday life.

The M-001’s shape is a standard right-handed, symmetrical design. This means the mouse’s left and right sides are shaped identically, but only the left side has thumb buttons. There are two main buttons, two side buttons, a standard ratcheting scroll wheel, and a power switch on the underside.

With a length of 120 mm, the M-001 is slightly shorter than contemporaries like the Razer Viper V4 Pro (125 mm) and Logitech Pro Superstrike X2 (127 mm). The overall width and height are comparable, at 63.5-mm tall and a 39-mm wide. The largest difference is the profile of the mouse: The front end sits significantly lower than other mice, and the rear has a more significant hump, which creates a more angled shape overall. When using a palm grip, this puts your hand at a slightly more aggressive forwards angle, with your palm sitting higher and your fingers pointing more downwards. It’s a slight difference, but I found this angle to be better for quick movements compared to a more “flat” design. However, like most shape-related choices, this is ultimately down to individual preference. (To compare mouse sizes, I typically use a website like eloshapes)

The two main buttons are lightly sculpted, with subtle concavity for fingers to comfortably rest in. Similarly, there is a subtle taper at the bottom center of the mouse which makes lifting and moving easier. The side buttons use a simple, rounded shape placed just above the taper, making it easy to rock your thumb upwards to press them without getting in the way. They’re just elevated enough that the buttons don’t recess into the shell when pressed. The only shape-related issue I had was the scroll wheel, which is a good bit more elevated than other mice. This isn’t a huge issue, and I adapted to it after a few hours of use, but it’s something I could see being off-putting for users accustomed to a lower-profile scroll wheel.

The most important part of this mouse’s construction is the magnesium shell, which provides significantly more rigidity than the plastic shells used on most mice. Because of this, the mouse feels incredibly sturdy and solid. The metal shell also gives the mouse a cool, slick feeling underneath your fingertips, which I prefer to the matte plastic feeling that most mice have.

Despite using metal, it only weighs 53.6 grams, making the M-001 quite light by most standards, It still falls slightly behind the Viper V4 Pro’s 50.3 grams and lags way behind Corsair’s superlight, 36-gram Sabre V2 Pro Ultralight. But the M-001 is incredibly solid, with none of the flexibility or flimsiness that most plastic mice have. When compared to other magnesium mice like the AngryMiao Infinity .97 or Corsair’s magnesium-shelled Sabre, the M-001 has an entirely solid upper shell without any cutouts or holes, which feels much better for regular use with a multitude of grip styles (plus, it’s much less likely to have dust, dead skin, or anything else build up inside of it). While the full magnesium shell increases weight, it’s a worthwhile trade-off for a smooth and rigid shape.

It’s also worth noting that some of Wallhack’s promotional material depicts the mouse as being a solid black color, but this isn’t quite the case. The unit I received is much closer to a gunmetal color, with a significant amount of metallic flake across the finish. The color looks fantastic in person, but it's difficult to properly capture in images.

Flipping over this mouse reveals one of the more atypical design decisions being made: the lack of a bottom shell. Instead of an entire underside, there is a thin frame around the circumference of the mouse that holds six feet in place. Between them is a chasm that reveals the entire PCB and battery.

Of particular note is the PCB’s function inside the mouse: Instead of simply sitting inside of the shell, the PCB works as a primary structural component. Similar techniques have been used in designing supercars and sport bikes, with the Ferrari F50 using the engine block as a stressed member that provides rigidity to the entire car, a technology borrowed from Ferrari’s F1 team. This provides some obvious benefits to rigidity and weight-savings, but it’s also just undeniably cool to be able to flip over your mouse and see the entire circuit board.

Right behind the PCB is the star of the show, a hot-swappable battery. While I’ve tested quite a few mice with this technology, none of them have managed to make the battery-swapping so satisfying. Instead of being attached with magnets or a rotating locking mechanism, the batteries use a custom-made -spring-loaded ejector similar to an SD card or a Nintendo Switch cartridge. To insert the battery, simply press until you feel a click, and it will lock into place. To remove it, do the same: press until you feel a click, and it will pop out slightly. Of all the mice I’ve tested with removable batteries, this is by far my favorite mechanism to use: It’s intuitive, fun, and feels sturdy.

This battery system does come with a trade-off. Because hot-swappable batteries means an effectively-infinite battery life (as long as you remember to plug the old one into the dongle when you swap), Wallhack has decided to entirely remove the USB-C port on the mouse. This means it only functions as a 2.4G wireless mouse and cannot be plugged or charged in directly. This isn’t a huge issue (it never inconvenienced me, since there was always a fully charged battery ready to swap in), and it theoretically means the connection quality will be better, since there’s fewer components between the antenna and the receiver. But it is worth noting that, if a battery dies or your dongle craps out, there’s no other way to use this mouse.

In every high-polling mouse I’ve reviewed, the dongle has been a bit of a pain point. A high polling rate necessitates proximity, which in turn requires in a small thing sitting on your desk. In most cases, these things have only one or two functions: They connect to the mouse, and they maybe have a small number of LED indicators (between 1 and 3) that can display battery life, connection quality, or some other setting. Almost every time, they’re small and light enough to slide around on your desk and will inevitably end up falling behind it once or twice due to the weight of the cable pulling it back.

With the M-001, nearly all of these issues are resolved: The dongle is given purpose, function, and I’d even argue dignity. Instead of the small, existing-by-necessity designs that most manufacturers ship with their mice, Wallhack has managed to make a beautiful piece of industrial design. The dongle is a medium-sized metal wedge sporting a gunmetal gray finish identical to the mouse. It has two camera-like dials up front, a button, and a slot for a rechargeable battery. The DPI dial features numbering from 400 to 25,600, and the polling dial goes from 125 Hz to 8,000 Hz.

The dials both have LED indicators next to them, which illuminate alongside the mouse to indicate a setting being changed. When rotated, they both have a satisfying ratcheting mechanism that’s stiff enough to avoid accidental inputs, without being difficult to turn using a single finger.

While I loved these dials from the moment I saw them, I really didn’t expect to use them much. I’ve always been more of a set-and-forget type of person. But after testing this mouse for a while, I regularly found myself using both of the dials: On-the-fly DPI adjustments were great for games like Marvel Rivals. I tended to prefer, for example, a higher DPI for Thing and a lower for Thor. Polling adjustments, too, were useful in situations where older games (like Destiny 2) got grumpy when I was using a mouse above 1,000 Hz.

Like many mice today, the M-001 uses an in-browser customization client. Wallhack’s web client, Wallhack Terminal, takes a unique stylistic approach, relying on a black-and-white, text-based system that resembles Command Prompt more than a typical gaming software. This is both a breath of fresh air stylistically, and much more straightforward than some of the software I’ve used in the past.

The configurator has an extensive feature set, including adjustments for mouse rotation, acceleration, and lift-off distance, which is adjustable by millimeters instead of the vague Low/Medium/High that some softwares use. The sensor can also be pushed to scan faster than the mouse’s 8,000 Hz polling rate, which can potentially improve the accuracy of the sensor, but limits battery life.

Along with adjusting DPI and polling with the dials on the dongle, switching them to the red “DPI” And “Hz” text allows for more granular adjustments within the configurator.

The sensor inside the mouse is a Pixart PAW3950. For anyone who doesn’t have an exhaustive list of mouse sensors floating around their head, this is the same type of sensor used in the 2022 Razer Viper V2 Pro (branded as the Razer Focus Pro 30k). That's four years old at this point. It’s not quite a cutting-edge sensor anymore. But unless you're in the top-tier of competitive play, the sensor will feel consistent and reliable enough, being able to easily track and manage direction changes during fast-paced shootouts. My personal daily driver is a Viper V2 Pro with the exact same sensor (I prefer the shape to the V3 and V4), and it’s still perfectly serviceable after four years of use.

The main buttons inside this mouse use Omron Optical switches, while the side buttons opt for low-profile Omron blue-dot mechanical switches. The scroll wheel’s encoder is a mechanical unit as well, which means it doesn’t have quite the same longevity as the optical units some mice are beginning to use. However, they are easier to replace or fix if something does go wrong.

The internal design of this mouse is impressively simple: There are six feet on the underside, and the outer four all have screws underneath. When you remove these four screws, the bottom half of the shell comes off cleanly (no flimsy plastic clips!), and a few more screws separate the internals from the top shell.

I’ve said many times that, when it comes to peripherals, technical specs are only one part of the story. More than that, it’s important to have a device you enjoy using. Of all the mice I’ve tested, the M-001 is by far one of the most enjoyable. The shape is comfortable and natural for multiple grip styles, and the material ensures that the mouse feels rigid and stable despite its light weight. The dongle is a brilliant design that elevates itself from a sad desk tchotchke to a practical and functional device. And the battery mechanism is one of the most clever and fun designs I’ve seen in ages.

Everything about this mouse is deeply satisfying, and I found myself excited to use it every time I sat down. Of course, this mouse isn’t at the cutting edge of sensors or switches, and because of this, I can’t exactly recommend this mouse to someone looking for an ultra-cost-effective option with min-maxed specs. But in using the mouse on the daily, I didn't find that it mattered as much as you might think. 8,000 Hz polling and a 25,600 DPI sensor are enough for all but the pros. Instead, it uses tried-and-true internals that function perfectly well and, more importantly, have crisp and precise tactile responses.

Until recently, American property owners could reasonably expect swift sales that yielded prices above their asking rates.

This trend marks a pronounced shift from earlier in the decade, when low mortgage rates energized buyers, creating an intense market that routinely drove property values significantly above asking prices.

Today, elevated mortgage rates, cumulative price growth from recent years, and high inflation have caused market activity to stall. These factors are placing homeownership out of reach for many while others delay purchases until conditions improve.

Sellers now far outnumber buyers, leaving owners faced with difficult decisions: implement steeper price cuts and financial concessions to attract buyers, or remain listed longer and delay significant personal plans.

"The dynamic has shifted back toward more of what we call buyers’ markets rather than sellers’ markets," said Mark Fleming, chief economist at First American Financial. After dropping below 6% in February, average 30-year mortgage rates climbed to 7.4% this month—their highest point in nearly three years. This upward trajectory in interest rates has stifled housing sales nationwide.

Buyers frequently request and receive financial contributions from sellers to cover closing costs. Rising mortgage rates have also allowed buyers to negotiate for seller contributions toward rate buy-downs, reducing monthly borrowing expenses.

"It’s sort of like an indicator of where the market is right now, with buyers gaining leverage at the expense of sellers, and sellers doing more now to meet buyers halfway," said Jake Krimmel, a senior economist at Realtor.com.

It's the hot-button topic at the Frankfurt Book Fair: How profoundly will artificial intelligence (AI) transform the book industry, and how can this transformation be regulated?

In reality, however, they concern very concrete issues — copyright protection, the power of tech giants and the future of creativity. Many agree that the political measures taken don't go far enough.

This year, however, another aspect has entered the debate: Can we actually recognize when a text has been written with the help of artificial intelligence, and if so, can this be proven?

These are some of the questions raised by the current scandal surrounding Haitian-Canadian author Thelyson Orelien and his debut novel, "C'etait ca ou mourir," (This title means "It Was Either That or Die" in English but an English translation has not yet been published), which was published in Canada in the spring.

The book about global migration was universally acclaimed, with translation rights sold in more than 20 languages. Soon after the book was published in France, it was long-listed for the prestigious Prix Goncourt.

But then, all of a sudden, allegations emerged that the book had been written almost entirely using AI. Orelien, a rising literary star, found himself branded a fraud despite denying all the allegations. His nomination for the Goncourt was withdrawn.

So, who should we believe? An author or an AI that claims to have identified AI-generated writing?

"We will have to learn to live with doubt," said Hannes Bajohr, a professor of German literature at the University of California, Berkeley.

Bajohr has spent years examining how AI is changing both writing and reading. His prediction: If the origins of texts can no longer be reliably established, we will have to evaluate them differently. What matters will be their quality alone, rather than whether AI was used in the writing process.

Nina George, a bestselling author and the honorary president of the European Writers' Council, is unwilling to accept this.

At the Frankfurt Book Fair, she was awarded the Friedrich Perthes Prize in recognition of her commitment to issues such as AI and copyright reform.

George has addressed the European Parliament and the lower house of the German parliament, speaking and writing tirelessly about the dangers of AI models. It is perverse, she said, that an author should suddenly have to prove that their writing is the creation of a human. The focus of her campaigns are the tech corporations' self-serving mentality and copyright protection.

But what if an author sees AI not as an enemy but as a tool that makes writing easier?

US author and artist Luke Stoffel deliberately used AI in his memoir, "How To Win One Million Dollars and Shit Glitter," and was open about doing so.

"I'm dyslexic," he said in a video interview. "I've always written and made up stories, but I never dared to take them any further."

It was only with the help of AI, he said, that he was able to fulfill his dream. The technology drew his attention to weaknesses in his writing and suggested a marketing strategy that actually worked.

In May 2026, he received an award from the Independent Book Publishers Association for the book.

For South African author Coral Hart, AI is actually the foundation of her business model. Publishing under various pseudonyms, each year she releases more than 200 different novels, spanning the genres of romance, thriller and mystery.

Hart sees herself as an ideas person, as well as an editor and publisher. Although none of her books has yet become a bestseller, the business is profitable, she said, adding that each book reaches more than 5,000 people.

Stoffel considers the entire debate hypocritical and points out that many publishers have been working with AI tools for some time. He's certain that "right now, a million-copy bestseller is being printed that was created with the help of AI. The author won't admit it, and then there'll be a scandal."

It is entirely possible that the scandal surrounding Thelyson Orelien is precisely that story. But we may never know for sure.

For now, the battle against the tech giants continues, some of which are themselves increasingly concerned about losing control.

At the very start of the Frankfurt fair, the German Publishers and Booksellers Association issued an appeal to policymakers in Berlin and Brussels, calling for stricter regulation of artificial intelligence, which they said is more urgent than ever. More than 140 publishers have signed it.

But for now, it remains just an appeal. And the question of what authors themselves should be permitted to do with AI will undoubtedly continue to fuel debates in the future.

We can’t let them. This is a story of corporate and medical malfeasance, which is also a story too many of us know all too well in a country where cruelty has become more than “the point.” It has become currency.

#1350. Scientists Trapped an Antimatter Particle for Months to Study Its Secrets

Antimatter is not known for being easy to get ahold of. That’s part of why these particles remain so cryptic. But recently, a team of physicists at Northwestern University managed to trap a single antimatter particle for months. Cooling it to temperatures near absolute zero, they could control its quantum states with great precision without even touching it. The experiment, published in Physical Review Letters, represents a breakthrough in our understanding of one of the great mysteries of physics.

In theory, antimatter should be everywhere. Mathematics predicts that every particle of matter has an equivalent antiparticle, a fact that scientists have verified experimentally. The universe should therefore contain similar amounts of both, but almost everything we observe is made up of ordinary matter, and we still don’t know why.

It is possible to produce antimatter particles in laboratories, but studying them is still incredibly difficult. When an antiparticle comes into contact with ordinary matter, the two annihilate each other and release energy. To prevent research facilities from exploding, scientists use vacuum chambers and electromagnetic fields that suspend the antiparticles in isolation. But this only partially solves the problem. Studying these particles, especially experimenting with their behavior on a quantum scale, remains challenging.

For this experiment, researchers at Northwestern trapped a positron, the antimatter counterpart of electrons. The team, led by Gerald Gabrielse, developed a device known as a single-positron quantum cyclotron, which used electric and magnetic fields to hold the positron in a vacuum at a temperature close to absolute zero. This means the antimatter particle was at its lowest energy state, allowing the researchers to induce controlled changes in its quantum behavior. Unlike other methods in particle physics, this approach didn’t destroy the particle. The researchers were therefore able to keep the single antiparticle trapped for months, allowing them to repeat measurements.

So what can taming antimatter particles actually teach us? Scientists know that matter and antimatter have very similar properties. For example, an electron and a positron have exactly the same mass, although their electric charges are opposite. However, questions still remain about exactly how similar these two forms of matter are and whether there are small differences that have gone unnoticed in experiments conducted to date.

To this end, researchers want to compare the magnetic properties of both particles. Physicists have already made very precise measurements with electrons, but achieving the same level of precision with positrons has proven more difficult. According to the American Physical Society, the new device could enable measurements of antimatter particles about 30 times more precise than scientists were capable of before.

So far, the experiment has not revealed any unexpected differences between the two particles. What the researchers have achieved is an improvement in their ability to detect these differences in antimatter particles. If future measurements find that a positron responds to magnetic fields differently than an electron, scientists would have to revise some of the laws they use to explain the behavior of matter and antimatter.
