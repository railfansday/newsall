#2601. First Day of School STEM Ice Breakers

First days can be tricky for some children, and there’s nothing more stressful than trying to think of a fun fact about yourself on the spot. So I’ve created easy icebreakers to reduce stress and get kids working together in groups on a fun task.

Each idea uses only inexpensive materials and requires almost zero prep. They encourage teamwork, critical thinking and curiosity in a fun, hands-on way.

All you need for this STEM challenge is a piece of paper or an index card. Cut the card the right way, and you’ll find it turns into a big loop you can stand inside!

You can make this engaging by giving the gummy worm a name and saying he’s stranded on top of the cup and needs his lifejacket.

Place everyday objects like marbles, toilet roll, paperclips, coins, sponges, etc into individual sealed boxes.

All you need to test your reaction time is a ruler and a friend. It’s a simple, fun challenge that requires no prep. For older children, you could set them a question to investigate, such as “Can reaction time be improved with practice?”

#2604. Your blood could reveal inflammatory bowel disease 10 years before diagnosis

Scientists have identified hidden changes in the immune system that can appear up to 10 years before a person is diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). The findings, presented at UEG Week 2026, suggest that certain antibody patterns in the blood could eventually help doctors identify people at risk of developing the condition and even predict how severe their disease might become.

Inflammatory bowel disease refers to a group of long-term disorders, including Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), that cause persistent inflammation in the digestive tract. These conditions develop when the immune system responds abnormally, contributing to inflammation and damage in the intestines.

Scientists increasingly believe that IBD begins developing years before it is officially diagnosed. However, the immune system changes that take place during this early, largely hidden period have remained poorly understood.

Tracking Immune Changes a Decade Before Diagnosis

To investigate what happens before IBD is diagnosed, researchers examined 2,000 blood serum samples collected from 500 people. The participants included 200 who eventually developed Crohn's disease, 200 who developed ulcerative colitis, and 100 healthy individuals of comparable age and sex.

Blood samples were available from the time of diagnosis and from approximately two, four, and 10 years earlier. This allowed the researchers to look backward through a decade of immune activity and identify changes that emerged before the diseases were recognized.

The team analyzed antibody reactions to approximately 357,000 antigens associated with bacteria, viruses, food, and the immune system itself. Antigens are substances that the immune system can recognize and respond to, while antibodies are proteins produced by the immune system that help identify specific targets.

Across all 2,000 samples, the researchers detected 123,980 unique antibodies.

A striking pattern emerged. People who later developed Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis had significantly less antibody diversity than healthy participants, meaning their immune systems showed a narrower range of antibody responses. Distinctive changes could be detected in both diseases as far back as 10 years before diagnosis.

Different Blood Signals Reveal Crohn's Disease and Ulcerative Colitis

The researchers also found that Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis were associated with different immune signatures.

Among people who later developed Crohn's disease, antibody responses to herpesviruses and bacterial flagellin were elevated. Flagellin is a protein that forms part of the tail-like structures some bacteria use to move.

These elevated responses were especially noticeable in individuals who eventually developed complicated Crohn's disease or disease affecting the ileum, the final section of the small intestine.

At the same time, antibody responses to encapsulated bacteria gradually decreased as diagnosis approached. These bacteria are surrounded by protective outer coatings that can help them evade immune defenses.

People who later developed ulcerative colitis showed a somewhat different pattern. Their antibody responses to herpesviruses and autoantigens increased, while responses to encapsulated pathogens declined. Autoantigens are components of the body's own tissues that can mistakenly become targets of an immune response.

Together, these findings suggest that the immune system undergoes measurable changes many years before inflammatory bowel disease is diagnosed.

AI Identifies Hidden Disease Risk Years in Advance

The researchers then used machine learning, a form of artificial intelligence that identifies patterns in complex data, to determine whether these antibody signatures could distinguish people who would eventually develop IBD from healthy individuals.

Using blood antibody patterns from up to 10 years before diagnosis, the models distinguished future Crohn's disease cases from healthy controls with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.90. For ulcerative colitis, the corresponding value was 0.84.

AUC is a measure of how well a model distinguishes between two groups. A score of 0.5 indicates performance equivalent to chance, while 1.0 represents perfect discrimination.

The researchers also discovered that antibody patterns could help distinguish people who would develop complicated Crohn's disease from those whose disease would remain less complicated. This distinction was possible approximately 10 years before diagnosis, with an AUC of 0.85.

These findings raise the possibility that blood antibody testing could eventually help identify not only who is at increased risk of IBD, but also which patients may need more intensive treatment.

Commenting on the findings, lead author Dr. Arno Bourgonje said: "It is fascinating to see that complicated CD at diagnosis is associated with a distinct antibody profile years before the first clinical symptoms and is not simply the consequence of disease progression after diagnosis. Finding these patterns so early points to the potential of antibody profiling to support earlier risk detection, distinguish between disease subtypes and help identify patients who may benefit from more intensive therapy as early as possible."

Could a Blood Test Detect Gut Disease 10 Years Early?

Although the results offer compelling evidence that IBD has a detectable early immune signature, the researchers emphasize that the findings are not yet ready for use in routine medical screening.

The study establishes an important proof of principle, but additional research is necessary to determine whether these antibody patterns can reliably predict disease in broader populations.

Researchers must also establish whether antibody testing provides meaningful benefits beyond existing genetic information, clinical assessments, and other blood-based measurements. Any potential screening test would need to be accurate, affordable, reproducible, and ultimately shown to improve patient outcomes.

"The next priority is to validate these signatures in independent, more diverse and prospectively followed populations," concluded Dr. Bourgonje. "We also need to determine their added value alongside genetic, clinical and other blood-based markers, translate the findings into a reproducible and affordable targeted assay and, ultimately, demonstrate that using such a test actually leads to better clinical outcomes."

If future studies confirm these results, blood-based immune profiling could offer doctors a new way to recognize inflammatory bowel disease risk years before diagnosis, distinguish between disease subtypes, and identify patients who might benefit from earlier intervention.

#2608. Flying Can Use Less Energy Than Swimming, and these Birds Have Been Doing It All Along

Scientists thought swimming was the most energy-efficient way for animals to travel.

An Andean condor and a trumpeter swan weigh roughly the same: about 11 kilograms. Both can fly, and both have to overcome gravity to stay in the air. So you might be tempted to think that they use up comparable amounts of energy.

The swan spends around 99% of its flight time flapping its wings. The condor, just 0.1%. And according to new research, the condor uses 79% less energy to cover the same distance.

While the swan relies primarily on its muscles, the condor has learned to harness an enormous, invisible source of energy: the atmosphere itself. In fact, it’s so efficient, that under the right conditions, its flight can be more energy-efficient than swimming.

Swimming is generally the “cheapest” way to travel from an energy standpoint. It makes a lot of sense; water supports an animal’s weight, allowing fish and other swimmers to move without constantly fighting gravity.

Flying comes next. Sure, you can “ride the wind”, but you need to generate lift to stay airborne, which can require considerable energy. Running is typically the most expensive. Land animals have to support their own weight while repeatedly accelerating their limbs and bodies.

Scientists describe this relationship using a quantity called the cost of transport: the energy required to move a given distance, often calculated relative to body mass. Think of it as the animal equivalent of fuel economy.

Now, an international team of 68 scientists challenges the classic thought on the cost of transport. Much of our understanding, they say, comes from experiments conducted in laboratories, where fish swim in tanks, birds fly in wind tunnels, and mammals run on treadmills. Those controlled environments make it easier to measure energy expenditure, but nature doesn’t really work like that.

“To us, the sky might just look like background for flying animals, but it is an essential resource that can dramatically change the cost of a journey,” says first author Dr. Martina Scacco.

If you’ve ever watched an eagle or vulture circle around the sky, you’ve probably seen it: they can remain airborne for long periods of time while barely flapping their wings.

These birds are masters of exploiting physics.

When the Sun heats the ground, the ground warms the air above it. In some places, pockets or columns of warmer air rise through the atmosphere. These are called thermals.

A soaring bird can enter one of these rising columns and circle upward, gaining altitude without having to generate all that lift through muscular effort. Then, once they’re high up, it can glide toward its destination, gradually losing altitude until it finds another thermal.

It’s kind of like taking an elevator to the top of a building and then gliding down toward the next one.

This “free” energy comes from the Sun, which heats the landscape and drives atmospheric motion. But from the bird’s perspective, it’s an external energy subsidy.

So far, nothing new. But that’s where the new study comes in.

To find out how much energy birds actually spend travelling, the researchers assembled an unusually large dataset.

They combined 17 years of GPS and accelerometer records from approximately 850 individual birds and bats belonging to 38 species. They also analysed four years of radar observations of smaller flying birds, divided into 12 size categories.

Altogether, the analysis covered nearly 200,000 GPS-based flight segments and thousands of additional radar observations. The animals ranged from tiny birds weighing little more than 12 grams to enormous soaring birds weighing over 11 kilograms.

GPS tracking showed where and how fast animals travelled. Accelerometers, which measure movement, helped researchers distinguish between energetically expensive wingbeats and more economical gliding or soaring.

The team then matched the animals’ movements to information about wind and rising air currents, allowing them to estimate the energetic cost of travelling through real atmospheric conditions.

Across seven species of vultures, the researchers analysed flights representing more than 4.3 million kilometres of travel. That’s enough to circle the Earth more than 100 times. Collectively, the birds accumulated approximately 128,000 days of flight.

Yet depending on the species, they spent only 0.1% to 7% of that time flapping their wings. The Andean condor, the heaviest vulture in the comparison, flapped least of all.

Granted, these birds are exceptional flyers to begin with; but the secret lies in how they use the energy that was already present in their surroundings.

And when the researchers translated their movements into estimated energy costs, some birds proved more economical travellers than swimming animals.

The study uncovered another seemingly surprising pattern. In general, larger species tended to spend less time flapping and were more efficient flyers.

That sounds counterintuitive. Surely a heavier bird needs more energy to stay airborne?

Well, in absolute terms, yes, larger animals generally require more energy. But the question here is how much energy they spend moving a given distance relative to their mass.

Large soaring birds have evolved wings and flight behaviours that let them extract considerable benefit from rising air. Under favourable conditions, they can travel enormous distances with relatively little muscular effort.

But there’s a catch: larger birds are more dependent on finding suitable atmospheric conditions.

Smaller birds are more flexible, they don’t need to rely on thermals as much, but they spend more time flapping their wings and use up more energy as a result.

Perhaps the most surprising finding is that some soaring birds travel more efficiently than swimming animals, overturning the traditional ranking of animal locomotion.

But the findings could also reshape our understanding of migration. A longer route with favourable winds and thermals might require less energy than a shorter journey through difficult conditions.

That’s particularly relevant as climate change alters atmospheric conditions. Changes in wind and rising air currents could affect how much energy birds need to migrate, and potentially where they can travel.

For now, these energy costs are modelled estimates rather than direct metabolic measurements. But the message is clear: the cost of getting somewhere depends not just on the animal, but also on the environment carrying it; and we need more complex studies to truly map that out.

#2609. New Research Reveals Cows Have a Little-Known Ability To Recognize Humans

A stranger’s face can hold a cow’s attention longer than a familiar handler’s, video tests suggest, offering clues to how cattle recognize the people around them.

When cows watched videos of people, they looked longer when the face on screen matched the voice they heard. The finding suggests that these highly social animals, which live in close contact with humans, can connect a person’s appearance with their voice.

Océane Amichaud of INRAE in Nouzilly, France, and colleagues tested these abilities in 32 Prim’ Holstein cows (Bos taurus taurus). Their study, published in the open-access journal PLOS One, examined both how the animals responded to human faces and whether they could link information from sight and hearing.

In the visual tests, the researchers played muted videos of familiar and unfamiliar male faces and measured how long the cows watched them. Unfamiliar faces held the animals’ gaze longer than a familiar handler’s face. That preference for new faces indicated that the cows could distinguish between people they knew and people they did not.

The researchers also tested cross-modal recognition, the ability to connect information received through different senses. They showed videos of familiar and unfamiliar faces while playing a recording of one of the two men speaking. Each man said the same sentence, keeping the words consistent across the recordings.

“In this study, using visual preference and cross-modal tests, we showed that cows are able to process human faces presented in 2D on videos and to associate familiar and unfamiliar faces with the corresponding voices by integrating multiple sensory modalities,” the authors said.

Heart rate measurements taken while the cows watched the videos did not reveal evidence that familiar or unfamiliar faces and voices affected their emotional responses. Differences in how long the animals looked therefore did not correspond to an emotional change detectable through this measure.

The authors caution that watching a video and hearing a recording are not equivalent to interacting with a person. Although the results suggest that cows can identify people through their faces and voices, further studies could examine how the animals interact with specific individuals. Understanding those relationships could help guide efforts to support their welfare.

#2610. Spooooooky! Alaskan man makes terrifying ‘kelp-o’-lanterns’

Published Oct 21, 2026 10:48 AM EDT

It seems like Halloween decorations are getting more elaborate every year. Instead of a viral 12-foot skeleton, a digital creator in Alaska has turned to nature to find inspiration for his spooky embellishments.

In 2025, photographer and Homer resident Tim Hatfield was walking on a beach with his girlfriend. They noticed that some strands of bull kelp (Nereocystis luetkeana) had washed ashore. Bull kelp, sometimes referred to as “mermaid’s bladder,” is a fast-growing annual seaweed that grows along much of the coast of North America.

Hatfield’s girlfriend pointed out that one of the pieces of kelp looked like a face, and said that she’d seen them used as Halloween decorations before. Hatfield decided to give it a try himself, and returned to the beach after a big storm to bring some home. He then turned the bull kelp remains into eerie faces, added lights, and even fashioned one into an eyeball.

“The coolest thing about the kelp-o’-lanterns is that as they age, and it only takes a few days, they look creepier and almost look like real shrunken heads,” Hatfield tells Popular Science. “Then this year I kind of already went for the ones that looked weathered.”

This year’s kelp-o’-lantern includes a one-eyed ghoul with a creepy spider-looking thing sticking out of its mouth and a noose around its neck, and an eerie looking head with two seemingly blood-stained tongues. Last year, Hatfield set up his kelp-o’-lanterns at his girlfriend’s house in town. For this year, the spooky decorations are staked out at his place. Hatfield’s house sits at the top of a hill, so trick-or-treaters on the hunt for candy are going to have to undertake quite the hike to witness them.

“[Last year] we had a few friends who were just like, ‘What is going on here?’ Because there’s no way your mind’s going to understand what they’re made of at first, when you first see them,” Hatfield says. “It is safe to say this is probably an annual tradition.”

As spooky season is in full swing, remember to keep your fake spiderwebs away from the typical flightpaths of birds and bats, such as on a tree.

A powerful gene-editing technique has been used to edit DNA directly in a person’s body — the first known instance of such a treatment.

The milestone treatment is part of a clinical trial of prime editing, a technique that can alter DNA with more precision than standard CRISPR–Cas9 methods can. In this case, prime editing was used to treat a condition called Wilson’s disease by rewriting DNA in liver cells, to erase a mutation that prevents them from eliminating excess copper.

Prime Medicine, a biotechnology company in Cambridge, Massachusetts, announced that it had treated the first person in its trial earlier this month. It expects to announce the first results from the study next year.

“I never dreamed that in my lifetime I would see this applied to my rare disease,” says Rhonda Rowland, president of the Wilson Disease Association in Atlanta, Georgia. “It’s going to be a really exciting year.”

In launching the trial, Prime Medicine joins a host of companies aiming to engineer cells directly in the body, rather than using the standard method of extracting a person’s cells, editing their DNA in a laboratory and then reinjecting the altered cells. Rewriting DNA in the body, or in vivo, is appealing because manufacturing and administering the treatment might be much cheaper and faster than making a bespoke therapy for each person from their own cells.

But in vivo approaches have their own challenges, says Krishanu Saha, a biomedical engineer at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. “It depends on what types of risks you’re willing to accept,” he says.

Wilson’s disease is caused by mutations in a gene called ATP7B, which encodes a protein that transports excess copper out of the liver. If left untreated, the resulting build-up of copper can cause liver and nerve damage, as well as neurological disorders.

People with Wilson’s disease are eager for a one-and-done treatment for their condition, says Rowland, rather than the current, complicated regimen of copper-clearing molecules and other medicines. And gene editing could, in theory, provide a lasting solution by correcting the faulty ATP7B gene.

So far, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved only one gene-editing therapy, called Casgevy, which is used to treat two inherited blood disorders. Casgevy relies on CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing, which is often used to disable genes rather than to rewrite their sequences.

That left prime editing, a more complex and versatile system that can insert, delete and rewrite segments of DNA. In another clinical trial, Prime Medicine used the technique to treat two people with a rare immune disorder, by rewriting DNA in each participant’s blood stem cells outside of the body and then injecting them back in.

So far, no gene-editing therapies have been approved for use in vivo. But the FDA is expected to issue a decision early next year about an in vivo CRISPR–Cas9 therapy for a condition called hereditary angioedema, which causes severe swelling.

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes in front of the Sun, casting a shadow on the Earth. The shadow only affects a relatively small part of the Earth, so not everyone can see it. The path of totality is where people are fully in the Moon’s shadow and see a total eclipse.

A solar eclipse is a rare and exciting event, but it’s really important to protect your eyes. During an eclipse, the light from the sun appears less bright than usual, which means your body doesn’t make you look away, this allows the damaging light rays to reach the retina at the back of your eye. The damage may not be apparent immediately; it can take a couple of days.

The only safe way to watch is to wear specialised eclipse glasses or use an indirect method. If you’re wearing eclipse glasses, make sure they meet the international safety standard ISO 12312-2:2015(E).

#2613. This 150-million-year-old bird had dinosaur teeth and a strange secret

A remarkably preserved 150-million-year-old bird fossil discovered in China is challenging long-standing ideas about how birds evolved. Rather than developing increasingly sophisticated flight abilities, some of the earliest birds appear to have taken a very different evolutionary path, including losing the ability to fly altogether.

The newly identified species, Yuezhuornis yangyuanensis, lived during the Late Jurassic period (150-148 million years ago). Described in a study published in Nature, the crow-sized creature provides evidence that early bird evolution was much more complex than scientists once believed. Instead of evolving primarily to become better flyers, ancient birds were adapting to a variety of environments and ways of life, including those that favored living on the ground.

Researchers from the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology (IVPP) of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) and the Fujian Institute of Geological Sciences uncovered the fossil in the recently recognized Zhenghe Fauna of Fujian Province in southeastern China. According to their findings, Yuezhuornis represents the earliest known example of a bird lineage losing its ability to fly.

An Ancient Bird With Dinosaur Teeth and Claws

One of the most remarkable features of Yuezhuornis is its unusual combination of bird and dinosaur characteristics. Although it possessed skeletal features associated with birds, it also retained anatomical traits that would have been familiar among small predatory dinosaurs.

Its jaws contained closely spaced, serrated teeth resembling those of deinonychosaurs, a group of meat-eating dinosaurs that included agile predators related to birds. Such teeth are not normally associated with early birds.

Even more striking was its second toe, which could bend backward unusually far and carried a curved, sickle-shaped claw. This specialized feature is strongly associated with deinonychosaurs, including dromaeosaurids and troodontids, two groups of predatory dinosaurs.

"When we examined Yuezhuornis, we were struck by its extraordinary mix of features," said Dr. Min Wang, corresponding author of the study. "It possesses bird-like pelvic and hindlimb structures yet retains the specialized predatory foot and teeth of a small meat-eating dinosaur. It shows us that early bird evolution was far more complicated than we previously realized."

Where Does This Strange Jurassic Bird Fit in Evolution?

To understand how Yuezhuornis was related to other prehistoric birds and dinosaurs, scientists carried out detailed analyses of its evolutionary relationships. They used two approaches, maximum parsimony and Bayesian tip-dating, to compare anatomical characteristics and reconstruct the species' position on the evolutionary tree.

Maximum parsimony identifies evolutionary relationships that require the fewest changes in characteristics, while Bayesian tip-dating also incorporates statistical estimates of evolutionary history and information about when fossils lived.

Both methods produced consistent results, placing Yuezhuornis within Avialae, the evolutionary group that includes modern birds and their closest extinct relatives.

"The results consistently placed Yuezhuornis firmly within Avialae (the group containing modern birds and their closest extinct relatives), just crownward of Archaeopteryx and as a sister taxon to Zhengheornis," said Dr. Zhonghe Zhou, a co-author of the study.

This placement is particularly important because Archaeopteryx, which also lived roughly 150 million years ago, is among the most famous early bird relatives. The findings indicate that Yuezhuornis belonged to an early branch of bird evolution rather than simply being a small predatory dinosaur with bird-like characteristics.

Evidence That Some Early Birds Lost the Ability to Fly

The researchers also examined how Yuezhuornis might have lived. Using measurements of its skeletal proportions and statistical comparisons with other animals, they investigated whether its anatomy was better suited for flying or moving along the ground.

The results showed that its limb proportions closely resembled those of modern ground-dwelling birds and certain other prehistoric dinosaurs closely related to birds. This similarity suggests that Yuezhuornis spent its life on the ground and was likely unable to fly.

The findings point to an unexpected development in the history of birds. Rather than simply progressing toward stronger wings and more efficient flight, some early bird lineages appear to have moved in the opposite direction, adapting to lifestyles in which flying was no longer necessary.

This challenges the traditional assumption that the earliest stages of bird evolution were driven primarily by the demands of flight. Instead, birds were already exploring different ecological roles and developing a surprisingly wide range of body forms.

A 150-Million-Year-Old Discovery Rewrites Bird Evolution

The discovery of Yuezhuornis suggests that the evolution of birds was never a simple, one-directional process. Even during the Late Jurassic, when the earliest bird lineages were emerging, some species were developing adaptations that favored life on land rather than in the air.

Its unusual combination of bird-like anatomy, dinosaur-style predatory features, and apparent flightlessness provides a revealing glimpse into that evolutionary experimentation. The fossil shows that early birds were more anatomically and ecologically diverse than previously recognized.

It also underscores the scientific importance of the Zhenghe Fauna, a fossil assemblage that is helping researchers investigate a poorly understood period in bird evolution. As additional discoveries emerge from this region of China, scientists may gain an even clearer picture of the surprising evolutionary paths taken by the earliest birds.

#2617. Van Gogh’s The Starry Night May Have Been Based on a Real Night Sky and Astronomers Found an Astonishing Match

Astronomers found a remarkable match between Van Gogh’s Starry Night and the actual night sky of July 22, 1889 from the artist's vantange point.

A dark cypress rises like a flame above a sleeping village. The church steeple points toward a sky in motion, where great yellow stars glow inside rings of light and blue currents sweep across the heavens. In Vincent van Gogh’s landmark painting The Starry Night, painted in 1889, the countryside seems still, but the universe above it refuses to rest.

Van Gogh painted it while staying at Saint-Paul-de-Mausole, a psychiatric hospital in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, southern France. He had voluntarily entered the institution in May 1889, hoping to recover from the mental health crises that had troubled him in the preceding months. The hospital stood amid the rolling hills and olive groves of Provence. From his room, Van Gogh could look east across the countryside, a view that would inspire some of his most celebrated paintings.

The village, with a church recalling his native Netherlands, was largely imagined. But the sky had a more tangible starting point.

In a letter to his brother Theo, written in late May or early June, van Gogh recalled: “This morning I saw the countryside from my window a long time before sunrise with nothing but the morning star, which looked very big.”

The “morning star” was Venus, the brilliant planet that can appear before sunrise. Art historians have long identified it with the large, glowing object just above the cypress in The Starry Night.

But what about the other stars? Did van Gogh paint a real and somewhat accurate arrangement of celestial bodies, or did he simply imagine them to suit his artistic vision?

Scholars have long debated how much of the sky van Gogh observed and how much he imagined. New York’s Museum of Modern Art, which owns the painting, dates it to June 1889, largely based on the painter’s correspondence with Theo. But a new astronomical study proposes that the painting’s stars and crescent moon accurately depict the night sky more than a month later, before dawn on July 22, 1889.

If so, The Starry Night may be much more astronomically accurate than perhaps anyone assumed.

For Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim, the stars have been familiar companions since childhood. He was eight when he saw Halley’s Comet in 1986, a mind-blowing experience that helped set him on a path toward astronomy. Now a telescope engineer and founder of the South Korean company SLLAB, Kim has spent more than 25 years developing large telescopes. Over that time, he has become intimately familiar with the night sky, memorizing the positions of stars through countless hours spent aligning instruments.

That experience gave him a rather unusual perspective when he looked closely at Van Gogh’s The Starry Night. Among the exaggerated, glowing stars near the center of the canvas, he recognized the Hyades, a V-shaped star cluster in the constellation Taurus.

“People like me, who guide a telescope through its first alignment with the sky, naturally memorize where the stars are,” Kim told ZME Science. “To my eyes, the star cluster in The Starry Night was the Hyades in Taurus. But earlier studies explained it as Aries.”

Something didn’t sit right. Aries contains relatively faint stars, hardly the sort of brilliant celestial spectacle that might catch a painter’s imagination. The Hyades, by contrast, form one of the most recognizable star patterns in the sky.

“At that moment, I was sure this painting was worth studying,” Kim recalled.

What began as a hunch would eventually lead Kim and his colleagues at Kyung Hee University to challenge more than a century of assumptions about one of the world’s most famous paintings.

Art historian Albert Boime and astronomer Charles Whitney had previously offered conflicting astronomical interpretations in the 1980s, both working from a June date.

The most striking problem was the moon. At the painting’s upper right, it is a slender crescent. But on June 19, 1889, it was 63 percent illuminated — more than half full.

On July 22, the moon was just 28 percent illuminated. Kim’s astronomical simulations tracked the night’s sky back in time from Saint-Paul-de-Mausole’s vantage point and placed the moon above Venus in the eastern sky, with the Hyades, a V-shaped cluster in Taurus, between them. A day earlier, the moon and Venus were farther apart; in the following days, they drew much closer.

“The Moon, the Hyades and Venus all match the painting at once,” Kim said.

Of course, van Gogh did not set out to paint an astronomical chart. His stars are enlarged, haloed and embedded in sweeping brushstrokes. How could researchers distinguish a genuine star pattern from a coincidental arrangement of yellow paint? Aren’t we running the risk of reading too much between the lines when perhaps the artist had no such intention?

The researchers still thought they had something to work with. They not only reconstructed how the sky would have appeared above Saint-Rémy on different mornings in June and July 1889 but also visited the area in person and used an earlier landscape sketch by Van Gogh to estimate how much of the eastern horizon he could have captured in his composition.

The results narrowed the possibilities around dawn on July 22, but the researchers still needed to establish whether the painted stars actually resembled the cluster.

Imagine tracing five stars from a painting onto a transparent sheet, then placing that sheet over a real star chart. You can move the tracing, rotate it or enlarge it until the patterns overlap as closely as possible. What matters is not the absolute position or size of the stars, but the distances and arrangement between them.

That’s essentially what the researchers did.

Before examining The Starry Night, they tested their method on other paintings. In Van Gogh’s Starry Night Over the Rhône, for example, the seven stars of the Big Dipper are recognizable, giving them a benchmark for how accurately the artist reproduced a real constellation. They also examined works by earlier painters, including one with a recognizable depiction of Orion and another whose celestial arrangement could not have existed in the real sky.

Armed with these comparisons, the team measured five prominent points in the central star pattern of The Starry Night. Their arrangement closely resembled the V-shaped structure of the Hyades, with a degree of distortion broadly comparable to what they found in Van Gogh’s other night paintings.

There was another intriguing result. When they repeated the comparison using a black-and-white drawing Van Gogh made after the original painting, the stars matched the Hyades even more closely. The researchers interpreted this as a possible indication that Van Gogh had a real celestial arrangement in mind, rather than merely using an arbitrary pattern of painted dots.

But resemblance alone is of course not proof.

“Procrustes analysis is by no means a cure-all; even a completely different constellation can sometimes show a high degree of fit,” Kim cautioned.

Indeed, the researchers found that other constellations could produce similarly convincing mathematical matches. The trouble was that they failed other tests. Some appeared in the wrong part of the sky; others contained stars too faint to be plausible candidates, or required treating Venus as an ordinary star.

The Hyades, by contrast, satisfied several constraints at once: their shape, position, orientation and relationship to the moon and Venus were all consistent with the July 22 reconstruction.

There remains an inconvenient piece of evidence. On June 18, van Gogh told Theo that he had “a new study of a starry sky.” That is why scholars have long assigned The Starry Night to June. Kim and his colleagues do not dispute that work had begun by then, but that doesn’t mean the letter proves the canvas was finished by then.

The Dutch painter had a debilitating episode in mid-July, partly because his correspondence fell silent after July 16. He subsequently spent weeks unable to work. If that chronology is correct, he would probably have been in no condition to observe the dawn sky on July 22, let alone incorporate it into a painting.

Kim and his colleagues therefore revisited the historical evidence to see whether the episode might have begun later than previously assumed.

One clue came from Van Gogh himself. In an August 22 letter to Theo, he recalled the circumstances of his relapse: “This new crisis, my dear brother, came upon me in the fields, and when I was in the middle of painting on a windy day”.

The researchers compared that description with weather observations from three stations in southern France (how amazing is it that we have this data from the late 19th century?). They found that a strong mistral, the northerly wind characteristic of the region, swept through the area around July 27–29. Another clue appeared in a September letter, in which Van Gogh described having been confined for six weeks. Counting backward places the beginning of his illness around July 25 or 26, broadly consistent with the weather records. Other letters, however, suggest a longer period of illness, so the record remains contradictory.

All of these clues permit a later onset, which may have allowed him to observe the night sky and paint it on July 22.

“The astronomical interpretation stands on its own, regardless of when the seizure began,” Kim said.

All of this does not settle the question of when Van Gogh finished The Starry Night, nor does it diminish the imagination that transformed an ordinary landscape into one of art history’s most recognizable images.

“Vincent found it hard to paint without a model, and he even argued with Gauguin, who believed one should paint from imagination. This background explains the realistic night sky in Starry Night over the Rhône. Why, then, have the stars in Café Terrace at Night and The Starry Night been interpreted as imaginary? Of three night skies painted by the same artist, we have long accepted that one is real and the other two are imagined. Wouldn’t it be more natural to explain all three as real, or all three as imagined?”

“In my view, Vincent always pursued artistic expression grounded in accuracy. Being based on the real sky does not deny his expression. In The Starry Night, I believe Vincent brought together on a single canvas the skies of several days before and after July 22. In fact, no two skies are the same; something always changes.” Kim told ZME Science.

Indeed, the swirls need not be either a transcription of nature or a flight from it. Van Gogh may have started with a real dawn sky and made it unmistakably his own.

Kim hopes his findings will open a conversation between astronomers and art historians.

There is also a rather charming irony to his investigation. Despite spending years studying the painting, reconstructing its stars and retracing Van Gogh’s observations, Kim has never actually stood before the original canvas. It hangs at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, and he jokingly hopes the museum will invite him to see it.

In the meantime, he suggests a different way of experiencing the masterpiece. Anyone can look up at the real night sky and search for the Hyades, whose distinctive V-shaped arrangement appears near the reddish star Aldebaran in the constellation Taurus.

“But seeing the Hyades is much easier,” Kim said. “After that, whenever you look at The Starry Night, you may see the Hyades first.”

The findings were published in the journal Scientific Reports.

#2619. These Giant-Headed Ants Are Breaking a Fundamental Rule of Biology

Bigger animals generally have bigger body parts, but new research reveals that certain ants defy this biological rule.

In the insect world, bigger bodies usually come with bigger body parts. But some ants break this rule in an unexpected way. A massive soldier ant, equipped with an oversized head and powerful jaws, can have smaller antennae than a worker that is only a fraction of its size.

Researchers at the University of Ottawa have discovered that this unusual pattern is widespread among Pheidole ants, a diverse group known for their distinct worker and soldier castes. Although antennae grow in proportion to body size within each caste, their sizes overlap between castes despite major differences in overall body size.

The findings suggest that evolution can alter how individual body parts grow, allowing ants to develop specialized features without following the usual rules of biological scaling.

“It was total serendipity. We were sitting at the microscope and Erica (Erica Vong is a graduate researcher and student at uOttawa and lead author of this study) showed me the antennae of two individuals. One was clearly smaller, yet it turned out to be from the soldier!” says Rajendhran Rajakumar, Associate Professor of Biology at the University of Ottawa.

The smaller antennae belonged to a soldier, while the larger ones came from a worker. “Much like when you are getting your favorite latte, both castes have small, medium, and large sizes of overlapping antennae.”

Using laboratory experiments, field data, and museum collections, researchers examined 565 Pheidole species and found the pattern throughout the genus. Comparisons suggest it may have originated near the common ancestor shared with Cephalotes turtle ants.

“Biologists have long assumed that traits such as antennae generally scale predictably with body size,” Professor Rajakumar says. “Our findings show that evolution can decouple a trait from body size in one context while preserving that relationship in another.”

Antennae allow ants to detect chemical signals, navigate their surroundings, and communicate with nestmates. Their size and shape also influence the arrangement of sensory structures.

“The antenna is not simply getting bigger because the ant is bigger,” Professor Rajakumar explains. “In these colonies, development preserves a flexible range of antennal forms that may help individuals compatibly respond together to changing environmental demands.”

Experiments with Pheidole dentata showed that social signals, juvenile hormone, and an organ involved in regulating body proportions influence this flexibility. By manipulating these factors, particularly the soldier inhibitory pheromone, researchers produced unusual “nanoworkers” and “nanosoldiers.” The nanosoldiers had worker-sized bodies but the enlarged heads characteristic of soldiers.

“Social signals and hormones do more than determine whether an ant becomes a worker or a soldier,” clarifies Professor Rajakumar. “They can also reshape how different traits grow and scale together (or decouple), within castes, between castes, and more broadly across species.”

The results demonstrate how colony conditions can influence individual development, supporting the concept of an ant colony as a superorganism. This flexibility may have contributed to the evolution of specialized castes and the remarkable diversity of ants.

“This work reminds us that evolution is not always a process of adding more or less of the same trait,” Professor Rajakumar concludes. “Sometimes new possibilities emerge when the expected connections between traits are broken and development is reorganized.”

#2622. Lessons Learned Speaking at Conferences

If memory serves, the first conference I ever attended was an FITC(opens in new tab) event in Toronto, back in 2014. I remember being super curious about how speaking at tech conferences worked; it felt pretty veiled and mystical to me 🔮

A couple years later, I got my first opportunity to speak: I did a brief lightning talk at React Europe in 2017. Apparently it stuck, because this is something I do a few times a year now.

In this article, I'm going to break down the process, to help offer an inside look at how the whole process works. I'll also share my perspective on what the benefits and drawbacks are, and what effect COVID-19 has.

As an example, I'll be speaking about my 2018 React Rally conference talk, “Explorable Explanations with React”(opens in new tab).

The first step to any conference talk is figuring out what you want to talk about!

There are a lot of ways to approach this. My personal strategy is to find stuff at the intersection of the following Venn diagram:

In my case, I was really passionate about explorable explanations.  An explorable explanation, sometimes just called an explorable, is a project that mixes elements of data visualization, journalism, education, and video games to create interactive learning experiences. They allow people to understand complex things in less time through playing and experimenting.

Some great examples of explorable explanations:

I decided in late 2017 that I wanted to build my own explorable about how waveforms work. It’s a subject I studied formally a decade ago, and thought could really benefit from being taught in an interactive way.

After several months of work, the project was launched: Let’s Learn About Waveforms(opens in new tab).

The response to this project was overwhelming. I got so many deeply-personal thankful emails, from audio students, to people who had struggled learning it on their own, to teachers and college professors who are using this tool to explain audio or physics concepts in class.

It made me realize that there is a strong need for this kind of content. React developers already have many of the skills needed to produce these kinds of experiences, so why aren’t more of us doing it?

A conference talk seemed like a great way to spread this message, and get folks excited about building their own explorables.

More broadly, I’m fascinated by interesting intersections between disparate things. I’d love to see creative coding(opens in new tab) represented more at programming conferences, for example!

Another category of “things we aren’t hearing more about” are things that our users need that developers tend to neglect: stuff like accessibility, localization/internationalization, performance.

If you're struggling to come up with talk ideas, it can be really helpful to focus on the things you're passionate about. We tend to assume that everybody knows everything that we do, but that's often not true for the things we're passionate about.

Once you have an idea for a talk, you’ll want to find a conference to submit it to!

A great resource for finding conferences is confs.tech(opens in new tab). CSS Tricks also has a great list(opens in new tab) of conferences.

Finally, you can find specific conferences by topic; if you work with React.js, for example, you can check the React conferences list(opens in new tab) to find suitable conferences.

Not all talks will work for all conferences, so you should spend the time to get a sense of what a conference’s “feel” is. Conferences usually share recordings of talks from previous years, so you can watch last years' talks and see if your talk is suitable.

Additionally, there may be restrictions associated with travel. Being Canadian, I’m fortunate enough that I can travel to most countries in the world without a lot of hassle, but this is not universally true.

Community-run conferences generally have a few invited speakers, but the majority of the lineup will come from a process known as CFP — “Call for Proposals”. This is an open submissions process where anybody can submit proposals for giving a talk.

While every conference has their own submission guidelines, there are some common trends:

A widely-known secret is that most of the time, conference speakers only have a vague idea about their talk when they submit their proposals. Especially for technical talks, it’s common to submit a proposal for a rough hypothesis, and to figure out the technicalities after it’s accepted.

Perhaps most famously, Dan Abramov(opens in new tab) applied to speak at React Europe 2015 by pitching a new take on Flux that supported hot-reloading and time-traveling. This solution eventually became Redux, although he hadn’t actually gotten it working when his proposal was accepted!

Be warned, though, that this isn’t always the best idea. In addition to the added stress of having a hard deadline, you might kill your enthusiasm for the subject by overworking yourself:

While there is value in having experienced speakers deliver talks, nobody wants to see the exact same faces, year after year. Because of that, there's still opportunity to break in!

Additionally, many conferences will have an initial “blind round”. This means that proposals are evaluated purely by the submission itself, and nothing is known about the proposal’s author. Prioritize conferences with this kind of process in place.

The sad reality is that conferences receive far more submissions than they have slots for. Many conferences can only accept 5% of submissions (or less).

You will get rejection emails. I think I’ve been profoundly lucky, and I still get rejected ~75% of the time.

If you have a talk you really want to give, don’t let a string of rejections stop you from giving it. Record it yourself at home, and share it on social media.

After much hard work sunk into crafting your proposal, the stars align and your talk is accepted by a conference! 🎉🎇

If you’re anything like me, you’ll experience a few moments of jubilation, before a horrifying realization settles in: shoot, I actually need to give this talk.

My first draft is almost always bad. After sinking dozens of hours into getting a coherent talk together, you might realize that the whole thing stinks. It can be really discouraging.

I've found that it's necessary to get a bit of distance. Take a couple days away from it, and then come back with a fresh perspective. You'll likely immediately see a bunch of solutions to the problems that had been nagging at you.

For example, I changed the entire structure of my React Rally talk when I realized that the order made no sense (I was starting with the most complex bit of code, instead of working up to it). It was a lot of work, done only a few days before the conference, but the talk was so much better because of it.

Feedback is also extremely important. Find someone you trust to give you honest criticism. They’ll spot problems you hadn’t noticed, and offer encouragement for stuff you’re on the fence about.

You can also give a “practice run” of the talk at a local meetup. It’s not always clear which parts of your presentation will prompt a reaction, and there’s nothing more awkward than having an audience laugh when you weren’t trying to make a joke. Get this out of the way when the audience is tiny!

For my React Europe talk earlier this year, I gave the same talk at a local meetup about a month beforehand, and then grilled everyone who came to talk to me afterwards. The feedback I heard most was that there was too much code in the middle, too much detail in side-pieces that weren’t super germane. I started inserting the "✂️️" emoji in places where it wasn’t beneficial to go through the code line-by-line, and it meant I was able to fit much-needed high-level stuff in the talk.

While I work on my talks, I take frequent breaks to watch other conference talks.

For example, in my React Europe talk, I decided to rearrange the slides so that the introduction card (“My name is Josh and today I’d like to talk about…”) only shows up 5 minutes into the talk, after seeing what a nice effect it had in Gary Bernhardt’s The Birth and Death of JavaScript(opens in new tab).

After months of work spent building your talk, whether you’re ready or not, the conference arrives. As a speaker, your conference-going experience will be different in a few ways.

Being a speaker comes with perks. For example, many conferences have a “speaker dinner” the night beforehand. This is a great opportunity to meet and chat with the other speakers.

Another speaker perk is that there is often a “speaker’s lounge”, a quiet side-room that only speakers can pop into.

Initially, this idea was a little unsettling to me; one of the great things about going to conferences is being able to chat with the speakers, and if they’re all off in their own space, that doesn’t work as easily!

After a couple conferences, though, I realized that speakers don’t tend to spend much time in the lounges. Mostly it’s just to give speakers a quiet place to work on their talk, or catch a moment of silence and solitude. As an introvert, I very much appreciated having a quiet spot to sit on my laptop from time to time.

Most people have a healthy fear of public speaking.

Remarkably, most people tell me that I don’t seem nervous in my talk! This is wild, because I’m very nervous. Maybe the trick is just learning how to not seem nervous? Admittedly, it has gotten much better over the years; I'm still nervous, but it's way more manageable now.

One trick I used for years was to rehearse enough that I could give the talk in my sleep. I never had a script, because I wanted to sound authentic. Instead I like to practice enough that I hit all the same points consistently, but the exact phrasing used varies from talk to talk.

The downside to this is that you will forget to mention things. In every talk I’ve given, I forget to say a handful of things I wanted to.

Time also passes ridiculously fast on a stage. 30 minutes can go by in an instant. Before you know it, people are clapping, and you’re done!

Conference talks are a tremendous amount of work. For my React Europe 2018 talk, I estimate that I spent over 150 hours on the talk; it consumed my mornings and weekends for a couple months.

Assuming you make the median US software engineering salary of $104,000 a year, and put 150 hours of work into the talk, you’ve spent $7500 worth of your time getting it ready.

In addition, preparing a talk is a bumpy ride. There’s a bunch of stress along the way, a bunch of nights and weekends where you really don’t feel like working on it but do anyway, moments of crisis where you think your talk won’t work or isn’t interesting.

For all of that, conference talks tend to reach a surprisingly small audience. Most conference talks will be seen by a few hundred people live, and maybe another 1000 through the recording. Most of my blog posts get more views than that, and they take way less time to produce.

So on its face, the math doesn’t look favourable. Why spend so much time and energy on a conference presentation?

In my opinion, there are a few things that balance the scales:

I originally published this blog post in mid-2018. It's 2 years later, and I thought I'd include some additional things I've learned in the interim.

Aside from those points, my overall viewpoint hasn't changed much. Conference talks are a ton of work, but they're also very rewarding. I'd encourage anybody who is interested in it to give it a shot!

This is a very stressful moment in history. I know for myself, I've found it very helpful to dive into side-projects like this blog and conference talks… I'm publishing this article for folks who, like me, welcome the distraction.

But I also want to acknowledge that it's totally fine for people who aren't motivated by these kinds of pursuits. Self-care is so much more important than career development. Screw the guys making folks feel guilty for not taking advantage of a pandemic 😬

Many things in our lives have changed because of Coronavirus/COVID-19, and conferences are no exception.

Some conferences have been postponed. Others have been canceled outright. But many have transitioned to being virtual/remote, instead of in-person.

It's been a few weeks, and we've seen a few conferences attempt the transition to online. So far, things seem to be running relatively well:

I suspect that fewer established conference speakers are willing to put in the work of preparing a conference talk without the typical perks of being able to travel.

I'd also imagine that giving a talk remotely can be less stressful than giving one in-person, on a stage in front of hundreds of people. I know that some conferences even support pre-recording your talk, so that you can engage with the audience in live Q&A.

All of these factors put together suggest to me that if you're interested in becoming a conference speaker, you don't have to suspend those goals until the current pandemic has passed. Spring-time is CFP season, and conferences have adapted to the new reality.

It's worth repeating that these are turbulent times, and many folks will not want to spend their time preparing and delivering a conference talk. That is 100% OK. But for some, this is a welcome distraction, and I'm glad to see that the option continues to exists.

#2623. Mediterranean diet may boost tiny proteins that protect the heart and brain

A new study suggests that the Mediterranean diet may protect the heart, brain, and aging cells through a surprising biological mechanism involving tiny proteins produced by mitochondria, the structures responsible for generating most of the energy our cells need.

Researchers at the USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology discovered that people who more closely followed the Mediterranean diet had higher levels of two mitochondrial microproteins called humanin and SHMOOSE. Both have previously been linked to protection against cardiovascular disease and neurodegeneration, the gradual deterioration of nerve cells that occurs in conditions such as Alzheimer's disease.

The study, led by USC Leonard Davis Instructional Associate Professor of Gerontology Roberto Vicinanza, offers a possible explanation for how everyday food choices influence cellular health and the aging process.

"These microproteins may act as molecular messengers that translate what we eat into how our cells function and age," Vicinanza said. "It's a new biological pathway that helps explain why the Mediterranean diet is so powerful."

How the Mediterranean Diet May Protect Aging Cells

The Mediterranean diet emphasizes foods such as olive oil, fish, beans, lentils, fruits, and vegetables. It has consistently been associated with a lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and cognitive decline. However, scientists are still working to understand exactly how this eating pattern produces its wide-ranging health benefits.

To investigate, the researchers examined blood samples from older adults who followed the Mediterranean diet to different degrees. Those whose eating habits most closely matched the diet had significantly higher concentrations of humanin and SHMOOSE.

They also showed lower indicators of oxidative stress, a process in which harmful molecules called reactive oxygen species overwhelm the body's protective defenses. Over time, oxidative stress can damage proteins, DNA, and other cellular components, contributing to aging and chronic disease.

Interestingly, the researchers found that certain foods were associated with different microproteins. Higher consumption of olive oil, fish, and legumes was linked to increased humanin levels. Meanwhile, olive oil consumption and eating fewer refined carbohydrates were associated with higher levels of SHMOOSE.

Refined carbohydrates include foods made with heavily processed grains and sugars, such as white bread, pastries, and many sugary snacks. These foods generally contain less fiber and fewer naturally occurring nutrients than their minimally processed counterparts.

"These findings suggest that specific components of the Mediterranean diet may directly influence mitochondrial biology," said USC Leonard Davis School Dean and USC Distinguished Professor Pinchas Cohen, the study's senior author. "Humanin and SHMOOSE could serve as biomarkers for adherence to the Mediterranean diet and have clinical significance."

Biomarkers are measurable biological indicators that can provide information about a person's health or physiological state. In this case, the researchers suggest that humanin and SHMOOSE could eventually help scientists understand how closely someone's diet aligns with healthy eating patterns and how their cells respond.

Tiny Mitochondrial Proteins With Powerful Effects

The findings extend a scientific effort spanning more than 20 years to investigate small proteins produced by mitochondria. Cohen has played a leading role in discovering and studying these molecules, which are increasingly recognized as important participants in cellular health.

Mitochondria are often described as the powerhouses of cells because they produce much of the energy required for essential biological activities. But their role extends beyond energy production. They also help regulate metabolism, cellular stress responses, and processes associated with aging.

Unlike most proteins, which are produced using instructions stored in the DNA inside the cell's nucleus, mitochondrial microproteins originate from genetic instructions within mitochondria themselves.

These instructions are found in small segments of mitochondrial DNA known as open reading frames. Some of these regions were once considered nonfunctional, but researchers have discovered that they can encode biologically active molecules.

One of the best understood is humanin, first identified by Cohen and fellow researchers in 2003. Subsequent studies have associated humanin with improved insulin sensitivity, better cardiovascular health, increased longevity, and protection of cognitive abilities.

Insulin sensitivity refers to how effectively the body's cells respond to insulin, the hormone that helps regulate blood sugar. Maintaining healthy insulin sensitivity is important for metabolic health and reducing the risk of type 2 diabetes.

Another intriguing microprotein is SHMOOSE (Small Human Mitochondrial ORF Over SErine tRNA), which was identified more recently by Cohen's research team.

SHMOOSE has attracted particular interest because of its possible role in protecting the brain. Scientists have found that a genetic variant of SHMOOSE is associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer's disease. The normal version, meanwhile, appears to help protect neurons from damage involving amyloid, a protein that can accumulate abnormally in the brains of people with Alzheimer's.

"These peptides are emerging as key regulators of aging biology," Cohen said. "They connect mitochondrial function to diseases like Alzheimer's and heart disease and now, potentially, to nutrition."

A Hidden Mechanism for Protecting the Heart

The scientists also identified a potentially important connection between humanin and an enzyme called Nox2, which helps generate reactive oxygen species.

Although these molecules have useful roles in normal cell signaling and immune defense, excessive production can contribute to oxidative stress and damage blood vessels and other tissues.

The researchers observed that people with higher humanin levels tended to have lower Nox2 activity. This relationship raises the possibility that humanin helps limit damaging oxidative processes, potentially offering an additional layer of cardiovascular protection.

Based on these findings, the researchers propose that the Mediterranean diet could support heart health in two complementary ways. The diet may help reduce oxidative stress directly while also supporting the production of mitochondrial microproteins that influence pathways involved in cellular damage.

However, the observed associations do not establish that the diet directly increases these proteins or that the proteins themselves prevent cardiovascular disease.

"This could represent a new cardioprotective mechanism of the Mediterranean diet," Vicinanza said.

Bringing the Mediterranean Diet to a Global Audience

Vicinanza's interest in the Mediterranean diet extends beyond investigating its effects inside the body. He has also worked to promote this traditional eating pattern internationally as a way to support human health, cultural heritage, and environmental sustainability.

As part of those efforts, he collaborated with the Municipality of Pollica in Italy, which is recognized as a UNESCO Mediterranean Diet emblematic community.

That collaboration helped support the establishment of the International Day of the Mediterranean Diet through the United Nations.

Observed every year on November 16, the international event is intended to draw attention to the Mediterranean diet's health benefits as well as its cultural traditions and environmental significance.

For Vicinanza, the new findings reinforce the relationship between traditional food practices and modern discoveries about how the body functions.

"We're connecting centuries-old dietary traditions with cutting-edge molecular biology," Vicinanza said. "It supports the idea that healthy eating patterns with little to no ultra-processed foods reflect how humans have eaten over long periods and may create conditions to which mitochondria -- ancient cellular organelles -- are likely adapted."

Could Tiny Proteins Help Unlock Healthier Aging?

Although the findings are promising, the researchers emphasize that the study was relatively small and observational. That means it identified relationships between dietary habits and biological measurements but could not determine whether eating a Mediterranean diet directly caused the differences in microprotein levels.

Other factors, including participants' overall health and lifestyle, could also influence the results.

Nevertheless, the discovery opens an intriguing possibility for the emerging field of precision nutrition, which seeks to develop dietary recommendations based on an individual's biological characteristics and needs.

If future research confirms that dietary changes can reliably influence humanin, SHMOOSE, and related microproteins, these molecules could potentially become useful tools for evaluating nutritional interventions and developing more personalized strategies for healthy aging.

The next step is to determine whether adopting a Mediterranean-style diet actually increases these proteins and whether those changes lead to measurable improvements in cardiovascular health, brain function, or disease risk.

"Our goal is to move from observing associations to understanding causality," Vicinanza said. "If we can harness these pathways, we may be able to design nutritional strategies that promote healthy aging at the molecular level."

#2625. Dead Star Devours a Newborn Planet That Formed From Its Own Remains

Exotic chemical elements in the atmosphere of a dead star hint at the presence of a planet that formed after the star’s death—the first such second-generation planet ever seen.

For star systems, the answer seems to be yes. Astronomers have identified the first strong hints of a newborn planet orbiting a white dwarf, which is a remnant of a star like our Sun after it has exhausted its nuclear fuel. In a cruel irony for the planet, the hints of its existence come from the white dwarf actively eating its progeny’s atmosphere. But for astronomers, it’s an exciting possibility.

On the basis of the strange chemical composition of the white dwarf atmosphere, the new exoplanet candidate seems to be a second-generation planet, formed out of the debris shed by the dying star rather than the debris shed by its birth.

“This white dwarf is unique because it has a lot of traces of copper, zinc, and niobium,” said Jamie Williams, an astronomer at the University of Warwick in the United Kingdom who was the lead author of the study, published in Nature Astronomy. “Niobium is especially interesting [because] that’s the first time it’s ever been discovered in a white dwarf.”

When astronomers see chemical elements heavier than hydrogen and helium in a white dwarf’s atmosphere, it’s likely “pollution” from a planet. The planet might have been destroyed when the star died—Earth’s possible fate when our own Sun goes kerplooie in 5 billion years or so—or might be a survivor from the original star system.

“I don’t know of any other source that comes close to being this strange,” said Juliette Becker, a planetary astronomer at the University of Wisconsin–Madison who was not involved with the study. “This [white dwarf] is very different from pollution profiles that have existed so far, which is very exciting.”

Like all stellar corpses, white dwarfs are weird objects. This particular example, which carries the memorable name HS 0209+0832, is probably about half the mass of the Sun but the size of Earth, measurements that are fairly typical for a white dwarf. However, HS 0209 is relatively newly formed and very hot: about 35,000 kelvin (~63,000°F), compared to the Sun’s 5,800 kelvin surface temperature (10,000°F). A more fitting name might be “ultraviolet dwarf” since its spectrum peaks in the UV.

When astronomers observed HS 0209 in 1999 using the Hubble Space Telescope, they identified a relative excess of helium in its atmosphere. The amount came as a surprise because a white dwarf this hot should have little atmospheric helium. The new study drew together additional observations from a wealth of other telescopes, including later Hubble data, the Far Ultraviolet Spectroscopic Explorer (FUSE), and the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS).

These additional data turned up further unexpected elements, including 3 times the niobium abundance seen in the Sun’s spectrum. When the researchers compared this spectrum to other polluted white dwarfs and solar system objects like comets and meteorites, they found no matches: HS 0209 is unique. However, because astronomers think atmospheric pollution comes from material stripped from orbiting planets, it seemed possible that was the case here. That’s where the TESS data, which measure fluctuations in the visible light output from stars, came in handy.

“We also see a variability in the brightness of the system, which we think is from a day-night phase curve of the planet around it,” Williams said. In other words, as the hypothetical planet orbits, the total light from the system varies on the basis of which side of the planet is facing Earth on a 4.4-day orbital period, a type of planet known as a “hot Jupiter.”

These data together indicate a planet whose chemistry corresponds to the very end stages of life for the progenitor star before it died and formed the white dwarf. In the scenario the researchers think is most probable, the gas the original star shed—containing zinc, copper, and niobium—provided the raw material for a second-generation planet.

“We have a white dwarf orbited by a disk of this kind of material [from the dead star],” Williams said. “This disk will fragment and then you’ll form a planet.”

Both Williams and Becker pointed out that HS 0209, pollution aside, is a very typical white dwarf, meaning it’s possible many other second-generation planets could be out there. Becker, who studies unusual scenarios for planetary formation, noted that second-generation planets were actually the first exoplanets discovered. However, those were orbiting pulsars: the remnants of stars many times more massive than our Sun.

“I also have a strong bias: Because there are [pulsar] second-generation planets, I think there must be also second-generation planets around white dwarfs,” she said.

#2627. Experts: How are climate scientists using artificial intelligence?

Artificial intelligence (AI) is developing at a rapid pace and is increasingly being used by scientists to better understand climate change.

AI is perhaps best-known for its use in “large language models”, such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini.

From weather forecasting to analysing satellite images, climate scientists are using various types of AI to develop faster and more efficient ways to study the warming planet.

However, the technology comes with no shortage of risks and challenges, including overreliance on unverified results, intensive energy use and exacerbating existing inequalities in climate science.

Carbon Brief spoke to 10 experts working across a range of climate-related fields – from developing climate forecasts to analysing changes in the energy grid – about how AI is being used.

Their answers are set out below, grouped by theme.

Dr Doug McNeall, science lead in data-driven climate modelling at the UK Met Office:

One of the most significant recent developments has been the success of AI weather-prediction models, which learn from large volumes of weather data to predict the next few hours ahead and then repeatedly feed those predictions back into themselves to generate forecasts up to two weeks into the future.

These models have demonstrated that AI can provide accurate predictions while requiring far less computing power than existing numerical models [which rely on mathematical equations to simulate the climate].

One of the main challenges is that AI weather-prediction models have been developed for relatively short timescales, where large amounts of observational data [for example, temperature, rainfall or sea ice extent data] is available and model performance can be evaluated quickly and rigorously.

Climate prediction is fundamentally different. To provide useful climate information, we need to make projections for future climates that lie outside the observational record.

One promising route forward is, therefore, to use AI to emulate or accelerate these existing models, rather than relying solely on purely data-driven approaches. This blended approach could make climate risk information far more accessible and affordable to produce, while retaining the physical understanding [based on mathematical and scientific principles] that underpins confidence in climate projections.

Prof William Collins, professor of AI for climate science at the University of East Anglia:

We’re seeing a really exciting renaissance in both weather and climate science…Machine learning [a subset of AI that uses algorithms to let computers learn from data and make predictions without being explicitly programmed] has had an enormous impact on numerical weather prediction.

So, for example, the European Center for Mid-Range Weather Forecasts is now deploying an AI forecast system as part of their official communication of weather predictions to the European community, alongside the traditional forecast system. AI systems have become good enough that they’re absolutely competitive with the world’s leading numerical weather prediction…So that’s been quite remarkable.

Much of machine learning and AI involves learning from data and climate represents a future for which we do not have data. We’re literally trying to predict the future. We don’t have observations of it. So, where does the data come from to inform the models? That is, at the moment, the central challenge that the climate community is attempting to overcome.

Dr Amirpasha Mozaffari, researcher in Earth artificial intelligence at the Barcelona Supercomputing Centre:

AI forecast models do well on typical weather, but underperform physics-based models on record-breaking and unprecedented extremes – precisely the events that matter most for adaptation [to climate change].

Solving that – whether through hybrid models [which combine AI with traditional models], physical constraints [in which rules are implemented based on the laws of physics to help the models make realistic forecasts] or new ways of evaluating models on what they have not seen – is the central open problem for the field.

Dr Stefano Materia, researcher in climate variability and change at the Barcelona Supercomputing Centre:

Besides the climate modelling and prediction framework, the proliferation of Earth-observation satellites has created a massive influx of geospatial data (for example, images showing deforestation or sea ice melt). AI is used to translate raw pixel data into actionable climate intelligence.

Methane tracking and ecosystem monitoring are two examples of this kind of application. Methane is a potent greenhouse gas, but leaks are largely invisible and hard to track. AI algorithms now automatically scan hyperspectral imagery [which uses data from across the electromagnetic spectrum] from satellites to pinpoint specific industrial methane leaks globally, alerting operators in near real-time.

At the same time, machine-learning models process thousands of satellite images daily to detect illegal logging, track land-use changes and monitor glacier retreat long before humans could manually review the footage.

Dr Clare Eayrs, polar research scientist at the Environmental Fluid Dynamics Lab at New York University:

In sea ice research, machine learning is now used across the sea ice modelling workflow…The most mature application is extracting more information from observations, for example, mapping fine-scale sea ice deformation from ship radar and classifying snow layers in micropenetrometer profiles [which measure the hardness or softness of different layers of snow].

Forecasting is the most visible application in this field. Ice Net [a sea ice forecasting system] was an early demonstration that a deep-learning system [which uses models inspired by the layers of a human brain to process information] could outperform a leading physical model in seasonal Arctic forecasts. Machine-learning systems are now used to predict [climate] variables such as sea ice concentration, thickness and drift.

Prof Cristiana Stan, vice-chair of the World Climate Research Programme (WCRP):

AI now appears across all major parts of the WCRP. [For example,]…the ”weather prediction model intercomparison project” [an initiative to create a centralised database of physics- based, machine-learning and hybrid model forecasts] includes physics-based numerical weather prediction models, hybrid [models] and AI weather prediction models. Because it is focused on operational models, the project offers a direct comparison between state-of-the art systems. [It] is intended to accelerate the deployment of the next generation of operational models and contributes to development of metrics needed to assess relative strengths and weaknesses of models.

Downscaling is another area that is harnessing AI power to refine the coarse-resolution model output into high-resolution.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) is performing its seventh assessment report (AR7)…This time, for the very first time, there’s a model intercomparison project just for models using AI [for weather and climate forecasting]. So that is a real milestone…It’s a very exciting proof of principle or proof of concept and, I think, the harbinger of many more developments of this kind to come.

[For more on how model intercomparison will inform IPCC AR7, see Carbon Brief’s recent guest post.]

A few years ago, it took two or three years for a new architecture to make its way from the machine-learning community into weather and climate work. Today it is a matter of months: diffusion models, foundation models and generative ensembles have all arrived in our field almost as soon as they appeared elsewhere. That speed opens up things that were previously out of reach.

And the cost of running a global forecast has dropped by orders of magnitude…which, in principle, changes who can produce weather and climate information at all.

Prof Priya Donti, assistant professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and co-founder of Climate Change AI:

As we introduce more solar, wind and other renewable energy into the power grid, we need to rethink how we actually manage the various sources on the power grid…

AI is used in a few different ways here. One of them is just trying to get better predictions of solar and wind power production in order to reduce that uncertainty and allow us to plan with a little more foresight.

Another big one is helping to actually optimise and control the various components we have on power grids – the power generators, the batteries, flexible loads like industrial loads or the batteries in your electric vehicles or heating and cooling systems.

Dr Carlos Rodriguez-Pardo, postdoctoral researcher at Politecnico di Milano, affiliated scientist at the European Institute on Economics and the Environment and at the Euro-Mediterranean Center on Climate Change:

AI is already useful at every stage of the climate policy cycle.

The first stage is measurement: understanding what is happening, what has been tried and who is exposed. Machine learning helps us process satellite imagery to estimate emissions, improve air quality monitoring, harmonise fragmented policy databases and map a scientific literature that has grown far beyond what any human team could read.

The second stage is modelling futures. The models we use to explore climate and economic scenarios are computationally very expensive, so AI-based emulators [simple models that can be used to represent more complex mathematical models] that reproduce their behaviour at a small fraction of the cost are transforming what we can do. Instead of a handful of scenarios, we can explore thousands and quantify uncertainties that were previously out of reach.

Reinforcement learning [a type of AI in which a computer programme learns to make decisions through a process of trial and error] is also allowing us to study how multiple actors, such as countries or electricity producers, compete and cooperate under different policy designs. This can reveal strategies that classical analysis misses.

The third stage is evaluation, where causal machine learning is extending the toolkit we use to answer whether a policy actually worked. That is a different – and much harder – question than predicting what will happen next.

The WCRP has established a task team to address this very question. The focus areas considered by the task team include modelling and projections, data and observations [and AI] impacts and services, engagement with the private sector supporting development and integration of AI and machine learning into climate research and applications and the scientific rigour and trustworthiness of AI and machine learning methods.

I think the IPCC’s eighth assessment [likely due in the late 2020s] may itself largely be written using large language models. Basically, the job of the people writing the IPCC report is to synthesise…One could certainly argue whether or not that needs to be done by people. AI should be telling us the known unknowns, not the known knowns… The thing that’s value-added [by AI] as part of that assessment process is to tell us what isn’t certain, what isn’t known well. Because then that tells you where the science should be heading.

[In September 2026, the IPCC established an “informal group” on AI to “provide timely guidance on the responsible use of AI and emerging digital technologies in IPCC processes”.]

AI will also be an integral part of the eighth Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, with machine-learning codes running alongside traditional climate models.

The inclusion of AI in climate and land-vegetation modelling represents a paradigm shift. Rather than replacing physical models entirely, hybrid modelling merges the mathematical rigour and physical consistency of traditional process-based Earth system models [advanced computer models that combine physical, chemical and biological processes to simulate the climate] with the pattern-recognition power and speed of machine learning.

This new approach will be able to overcome computational bottlenecks, replace unresolved subgrid processes [which are too small to be captured by the grid cells of a computer model], improve the terrestrial biosphere – finally narrowing down the uncertainties on the terrestrial carbon sink and accurately pinpointing emergent constraints and non-linear tipping points.

Much of what we know about Antarctic sea ice comes from the satellite era, which begins in 1979. That is a short record for a system with strong decadal variability. A great deal of information about earlier conditions is contained in ship logs, whaling records, expedition photographs and early aerial and pre-1979 satellite imagery. Machine learning is well suited to extracting it. Lengthening the observational record would improve almost everything else we do, including training and testing AI systems.

AI is frequently thought of as large generative models, like [those] developed by large tech companies. But that is not mostly what is useful in AI for tackling climate change.

People talk a lot about energy usage of AI and negative impacts on society, including on the climate, as a result of that energy usage and associated negative impacts like water usage. These are very real worries. However, it is worth noting that the massive energy and other requirements of AI systems are referring to those large-scale generative and agentic systems. They’re not mostly about the kinds of AI systems that are actually used to fight climate change.

I think the general public thinks of AI as this one specific thing…often large language models, chatbots, etc…People who are making allocation decisions for who funds scientists might have a mismatched understanding of what needs to happen, which means the right science doesn’t necessarily get funded.

Frontier models [which are at the cutting edge of AI research] are built almost exclusively in the global north, trained on data that is densest where observations are richest and validated against benchmarks that reflect the same regions.

Cheap inference [in which a trained AI model applies its learned patterns to new, unseen data] does not fix that on its own: being able to run someone else’s model is not the same as having a say in what it is trained on, how it is evaluated, or whether it serves your decisions.

Without deliberate effort to share data infrastructure, evaluation metrics that centre on people’s wellbeing and genuine co-production of knowledge, AI risks automating and amplifying the existing divide in the global climate information system rather than closing it.

If done well, [developing AI-based Earth system models] could dramatically lower barriers to entry. A small research group, or an agency in a data-poor region, could adapt a pre-trained model on modest hardware rather than building bespoke systems from scratch. That matters because [the] data is thinnest and the resolution gap is largest exactly where vulnerability to climate [change] is highest.

Dr David Rolnick, associate professor in the school of computer science at McGill University and cofounder of Climate Change AI:

Climate action should fundamentally be about democratisation of action and equitable response to climate change, both in terms of mitigation and in terms of adaptation. The consolidation of large-scale AI can often work counter to those goals by consolidating power in certain geographies and stakeholders, and by defining action based upon the goals of those stakeholders.

When you build technology in specific institutions, they do not necessarily understand the needs of the majority of the world’s population or local stakeholders from other geographies and other entities.

The biggest challenge is trust…Good performance is necessary, but it is not enough. We also need independent evaluation, physical credibility, process-based validation, robustness and some degree of interpretability.

The risk I worry about most is that the apparent success of these systems could be read as a reason to invest less on observations. AI is not a substitute for observation, physical understanding or careful evaluation. Especially for Antarctic sea ice, where observations of sea ice and snow thickness remain sparse and understanding of snow-ice processes remains limited, we need more, targeted observations, not fewer. It also makes the case for documenting them – and their uncertainties – far better than we do now.

The challenge is always making sure that you’re getting the right answer for the right reason and the risk is getting a plausible answer that is “truthy”, but not the truth…Ask a computer for an answer and it will give you one. And it’s very good. It will always obey. It will always give you an answer. The question is whether or not that answer is valid or not.

The first and foremost risk is AI diverging attention from other forms of climate action. AI is not a silver bullet. It is not an answer in itself. And where it is useful, it is only one part of the solution.

Prof Detlef van Vuuren, a senior researcher at the PBL Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency [in a 2026 Carbon Brief interview]:

There are now many more proposals being submitted than in the past, as people can use AI tools. So our science discipline has to start learning how to deal with this, both in terms of what we feel is allowed in creating [scientific] articles – what is allowed in reviewing [and] what is allowed in proposal writing. This is something that we have to learn.

The partnership between academia, the data centres and private industry is going to be integral to our success going forward. And I think the challenge is going to be navigating that relationship in a way that respects open science, uses open source code, etc…

You do not want to be in a situation where we have something like a large language model where it’s not open source…[We need] fairness, accountability, transparency and explainability.

High-resolution outputs can look exact when they are not; uncertainties compound in invisible ways and predictions that feed into policy need honest, well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, communicated in decision-relevant terms.

Like any other technology, AI should augment human judgement in climate decisions, not replace it. As researchers, it is our responsibility to ensure the field serves as a force for social progress.

#2628. Your Saliva Could Hold the Key to Detecting a Deadly Cancer

Scientists have discovered distinct bacterial patterns in the saliva of patients with a deadly form of esophageal cancer.

Bacteria living in the mouth may hold clues to a deadly form of esophageal cancer that often goes undetected until treatment options are limited. Researchers in South Africa have identified distinctive bacterial patterns in patients’ saliva, raising the possibility that a simple, inexpensive test could eventually help identify people who need further screening.

The study, published in Communications Medicine, was conducted by scientists at the Sydney Brenner Institute for Molecular Bioscience (SBIMB) at Wits University and Columbia University in New York. Combining bacterial DNA sequencing with machine learning, the team developed a model that distinguished cancer patients from unaffected individuals more effectively than one based on clinical and demographic information alone.

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is especially common in parts of eastern and southern Africa, including South Africa’s Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal provinces. High rates also occur in regions of China and Iran, forming a geographic belt that scientists have struggled to explain.

Unlike esophageal adenocarcinoma, which is strongly associated with acid reflux, obesity, and lifestyle factors, ESCC has less clearly understood causes. It also frequently affects relatively young adults.

“A feature of the cancer is the young age of many patients, with a mean age of 50, and 18% of patients presenting before the age of 40,” says Distinguished Professor Christopher Mathew of the SBIMB.

“This geographical occurrence is also puzzling, and may reflect genetic susceptibility, a specific environmental exposure, or a combination of factors that researchers have still not been able to pin down,” explains Dr. Wenlong Carl Chen, a researcher at SBIMB.

Analysis of data from the Johannesburg Cancer Study identified several ESCC risk factors, including smoking, heavy alcohol consumption, rural residence, lower educational attainment, and the use of biomass or other household fuels.

“Still, they do not fully explain the number of cases, the geographic clustering, or why some patients develop the disease without these obvious exposures.”

To investigate another possible indicator, researchers examined the oral microbiome, the community of microorganisms living in the mouth. DNA sequencing revealed distinct differences between saliva samples from cancer patients and unaffected controls.

“We found clear differences between the saliva bacteria of people with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma and unaffected controls. Using genetic sequencing of bacteria and machine-learning methods, the team identified a distinctive microbial pattern associated with the cancer,” says Chen.

Several bacterial species were more abundant in ESCC patients, including Fusobacterium nucleatum, a microorganism previously implicated in other cancers. The microbiome-based model also performed better than one relying solely on clinical and demographic data.

However, the findings do not establish that these bacteria cause cancer. The microbial changes could instead result from the disease itself, particularly as a growing tumor narrows the esophagus and disrupts normal swallowing.

“But even that could be useful. A blockage in the esophagus develops gradually, often before patients arrive at the hospital, unable to swallow properly. If microbial changes in saliva can help flag that something is already changing, they could one day help identify people who need earlier referral for endoscopy.”

A saliva or cheek swab test could eventually help doctors prioritize patients for endoscopy, particularly in communities with limited access to specialized care. Such a test would not replace endoscopy, however, and the current approach has not been established as an early cancer detection method.

Researchers are also investigating whether inherited genetic differences increase susceptibility to ESCC and whether tumors contain evidence of earlier environmental exposures.

One approach involves studying mutational signatures, distinctive patterns of DNA damage preserved in cancer cells. Certain chemicals, pollutants, smoke exposures, or water contaminants may leave recognizable genetic traces, potentially helping scientists identify contributors to the disease.

“This is critical research which has generated important foundational knowledge that will take us to the next step, and hopefully to the clinic in the long run,” says Professor Michèle Ramsay, Director of the SBIMB.

The saliva model has undergone internal validation but still requires testing in independent populations. Oral bacterial communities vary with geography, diet, and environmental conditions, meaning results from one population may not translate directly to another.

The next research phase will include patients with confirmed ESCC, healthy individuals, and people with benign esophageal conditions that can also cause swallowing difficulties.

These comparisons will help establish whether the bacterial patterns are specific to cancer or reflect esophageal obstruction more generally. The answer will determine whether saliva testing could eventually become a practical tool for identifying patients who need further investigation.

#2631. Accessible Animations in React

Long-time readers of this site have probably picked up that I am a big fan of animations. When used properly, I believe they add a ton of value to the user experience.

That said, it's important to be mindful of the fact that not everybody experiences them the way I do. For some folks, motion can trigger physical symptoms like nausea, dizziness, and malaise. I very much don't want to cause any of those symptoms!

Operating systems offer a remedy for this: users can opt out of animations. The setting is meant primarily for the OS, but websites and web applications can read that value. It doesn't disable animations automatically, so the onus is on us—the developers—to take advantage of it.

In this tutorial, we'll see how to use the prefers-reduced-motion media query to disable animations in React.

Already familiar with the vestibular system, and the prefers-reduced-motion media query? Jump straight to the solution for a copy-and-pasteable React hook.

The human body is comprised of many different systems responsible for regulating and managing this whole being-alive thing. One of those systems is called the vestibular system. It includes the inner ear and parts of the brain, and it manages our sense of balance.

You know when you spin really fast and it makes you dizzy? By spinning, you're sloshing fluid around in your inner ear, and the brain uses that fluid to help it figure out which direction is “up”. This is similar to how a level works:

When you spin around in circles, your brain receives incompatible information from different sources: your ear-fluid is claiming one thing, while your eyes are claiming another. This dissonance is super disorienting and unpleasant.

For most people, unless you're intentionally trying to confuse this system, it works properly, and you can move through your life trusting that it will keep you upright. But for others, this system is not always trustworthy—a vestibular disorder causes the vestibular system to feed the brain bad information.

The most commonly-known symptom of this is vertigo; all of a sudden, someone will feel like gravity is pulling them in the wrong direction. This is just one way that vestibular disorders can manifest.

Troublingly, animations can be a trigger for some folks, leading to a whole range of unpleasant side-effects: dizziness, nausea, headaches, malaise. It can feel as if our website is reaching out and spinning the person in their office chair 😬

It is estimated that up to 35% of adults 40+ in the US have experienced some form of vestibular dysfunction, with 5% reporting chronic problems ( source(opens in new tab)).

We shouldn't forget about these people when developing animations.

When it comes to motion accessibility, most of the discussion focuses on vestibular disorders, but this is really just the tip of the iceberg. There are all sorts of conditions which can cause motion sensitivities, including dyslexia, ADHD, autism, epilepsy, traumatic brain injuries, and many more.

#2633. One of Earth’s most powerful supervolcanoes is refilling with fresh magma

One of Earth's most powerful supervolcanoes is slowly refilling its enormous underground magma reservoir, thousands of years after producing one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recent geological history. Scientists at Kobe University have discovered evidence that fresh magma is accumulating beneath Japan's Kikai caldera, which unleashed a catastrophic eruption about 7,300 years ago.

The discovery offers a rare look at how giant volcanic systems rebuild their magma supplies after massive eruptions. It could also provide important clues about the behavior of other supervolcanoes, including Yellowstone in the United States and Toba in Indonesia, potentially helping scientists improve their ability to recognize warning signs of future eruptions.

How Supervolcanoes Store Enormous Amounts of Magma

Some volcanic eruptions are so immense that they dramatically reshape the landscape. The amount of magma released can be staggering, enough to bury all of New York City's Central Park beneath a layer roughly 12 kilometers (7.5 miles) thick.

When such enormous volumes of magma escape from underground, the rock above the emptied reservoir can collapse, leaving behind a broad, relatively shallow depression known as a caldera. These features are associated with some of the most powerful eruptions in Earth's history.

Famous examples include Yellowstone, Toba, and Japan's Kikai caldera, most of which lies beneath the ocean. Kikai last experienced a giant eruption approximately 7,300 years ago, producing the largest known volcanic eruption of the Holocene, the geological epoch that began roughly 11,700 years ago and continues today.

Although scientists know that giant caldera volcanoes can erupt more than once, they still have an incomplete understanding of how the enormous quantities of magma required for these events accumulate underground. This makes it extremely difficult to anticipate when another major eruption might occur.

"We must understand how such large quantities of magma can accumulate to understand how giant caldera eruptions occur," says Kobe University geophysicist Nobukazu Seama.

Scientists Discover a Massive Magma Reservoir Beneath Japan

Kikai's underwater location might seem like an obstacle to studying the volcano, but it actually gives researchers an important advantage. The surrounding seafloor allows scientists to carry out extensive geological surveys across the volcanic structure.

"The underwater location allows us to implement systematic, large-scale surveys," Seama explains.

To investigate what lies beneath Kikai, researchers from Kobe University collaborated with the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC). They used powerful airgun arrays to generate seismic pulses that traveled through the crust beneath the ocean floor.

Instruments called ocean bottom seismometers recorded how these waves moved through the underground rock. Because seismic waves travel differently through solid rock and regions containing molten material, scientists can analyze their behavior to investigate structures that cannot be observed directly.

The findings, published in Communications Earth & Environment, revealed a substantial region containing a high proportion of magma directly beneath the site of Kikai's massive eruption 7,300 years ago.

By examining the seismic measurements, the team was able to determine the approximate size and shape of the underground reservoir. Its position and extent suggest that it occupies the same magma storage system involved in the ancient eruption.

"Due to its extent and location it is clear that this is in fact the same magma reservoir as in the previous eruption," Seama says.

Fresh Magma Is Recharging the Ancient Supervolcano

Perhaps the most intriguing finding is that the magma currently beneath Kikai does not appear to be simply leftover material from its catastrophic eruption thousands of years ago.

Previous investigations revealed that a new lava dome has been developing near the center of the caldera over approximately the past 3,900 years. A lava dome forms when relatively thick, viscous lava accumulates around a volcanic vent rather than flowing freely across the surrounding landscape.

Chemical analyses also showed that volcanic material from this dome and other more recent activity differs in composition from the material released during Kikai's giant eruption.

That difference provides an important clue. It suggests that the magma now stored beneath the volcano was supplied by new injections of molten rock from deeper within Earth's crust or mantle, rather than remaining unchanged since the previous eruption.

"This means that the magma that is now present in the magma reservoir under the lava dome is likely newly injected magma," summarizes Seama.

Together, the seismic observations and chemical evidence help researchers develop a clearer picture of how a giant volcanic system can gradually rebuild its underground magma supply following a massive eruption.

This replenishment process does not mean that another catastrophic eruption is imminent. Instead, it reveals how a magma reservoir can be recharged over thousands of years, an important process that scientists need to understand before they can reliably assess the long-term behavior of supervolcanoes.

What the Discovery Could Reveal About Yellowstone

The findings could have implications far beyond Japan.

Researchers believe the process identified beneath Kikai may help explain why other enormous caldera systems, including Yellowstone and Toba, also contain large magma reservoirs relatively close to Earth's surface.

"This magma re-injection model is consistent with the existence of large shallow magma reservoirs beneath other giant calderas like Yellowstone and Toba," says Seama.

The proposed model suggests that, following a giant eruption, new magma can gradually enter and replenish an existing underground reservoir. Understanding how this happens could reveal important patterns in the long-term cycles of magma accumulation, volcanic activity, and major eruptions.

Researchers now hope to improve the seismic techniques used at Kikai so they can investigate the movement and storage of magma in greater detail.

"We want to refine the methods that have proved to be so useful in this study to more deeply understand the re-injection processes. Our ultimate goal is to become better able to monitor the crucial indicators of future giant eruptions."

By revealing how fresh magma accumulates beneath a volcano responsible for one of Earth's most extraordinary eruptions, the research brings scientists closer to understanding the processes that eventually make such colossal events possible.

#2637. Scientists Discover a Hidden Cholesterol Switch That Could Change How We Treat Heart Disease

Researchers uncovered how a high-cholesterol diet weakens the liver’s defenses and lowered LDL in mice by blocking the pathway, pointing to a potential new use for a shelved heart failure drug.

A diet high in cholesterol can gradually weaken the liver’s ability to remove harmful cholesterol from the blood. Cholesterol-related heart disease remains the leading cause of death worldwide, even as doctors have gained more medications to treat it.

Researchers at the University of California San Diego School of Medicine have identified a previously unknown biological pathway that helps explain why the liver’s cholesterol-clearing capacity declines. Their study, published in Nature, combined experiments in mice and human cells and identified a potential treatment target. An experimental drug that acts on that target has already undergone initial safety testing in humans, potentially shortening the route to a clinical trial for high cholesterol.

The liver normally removes cholesterol from the blood so it can be broken down and used elsewhere. Proteins called LDL receptors sit on the surface of liver cells, where they capture harmful low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and pull it inside for processing. More receptors mean more cholesterol can be cleared. Most cholesterol-lowering drugs, including statins and PCSK9 inhibitors, work by increasing or preserving the number of these receptors.

Some patients, however, cannot tolerate the side effects of available medications.

“There’s still a real need for new cholesterol-lowering options, since some people can’t get to safe levels even with the drugs we have now,” said senior author Alan Saltiel, PhD, professor of medicine at UC San Diego School of Medicine and director of the UC San Diego/UCLA Diabetes Research Center. “This new pathway we discovered is completely separate from anything that existing drugs target, so it gives us a new opportunity to fill that gap.”

The newly identified pathway works against cholesterol removal by gradually depleting LDL receptors. It begins when high dietary cholesterol activates Ral, a protein Saltiel previously studied in fat cells. As Ral activity increases, fewer LDL receptors remain available to pull cholesterol from the bloodstream, contributing to higher blood cholesterol.

That receptor loss ultimately depends on an enzyme called cathepsin A, or CTSA. When the researchers blocked CTSA with a small molecule inhibitor, they stabilized LDL receptors and sharply lowered circulating LDL cholesterol in mice.

“We’ve known for a long time that a high-cholesterol diet reduces the liver’s ability to clear cholesterol from the blood, but we didn’t fully understand why,” said Saltiel. “This new discovery explains a critical piece of that puzzle.”

Turning a biological discovery into a treatment usually requires substantial additional research to find a suitable drug. In this case, a CTSA inhibitor had already been developed as a potential treatment for heart failure. It reached a Phase 1 clinical trial, where it was successfully tested for safety, before being shelved for strategic reasons.

That earlier work could allow the investigational drug to move into a Phase 2 trial for high cholesterol. The reduction in LDL cholesterol demonstrated in mice has yet to be established in people.

“Luckily, there’s an experimental drug sitting on the shelf that’s already been shown to be safe in humans,” said Saltiel. “We hope to test whether this might be effective by conducting a clinical trial — which could potentially bring a new treatment option to patients much sooner than would have been expected.”

#2641. Scientists discover a hidden switch that burns fat and blocks new fat cells

Scientists have identified a remarkable protein switch that influences whether cells burn fat or store it. Disabling this protein increases fat consumption and interferes with the formation of new fat cells, raising the possibility of a different approach to treating obesity. Subsequent research has strengthened the discovery, revealing that the same protein helps regulate how much energy the body burns.

What if one of the keys to fighting obesity lies not in suppressing appetite, but in changing how the body's cells use energy?

Researchers at the Weizmann Institute of Science have identified a protein called MTCH2, nicknamed "Mitch," that plays an important role in controlling metabolism. When scientists disabled this protein in laboratory experiments, cells began consuming more fuel, including fats and carbohydrates. They also found that removing Mitch could interfere with the process through which immature cells develop into fat cells.

The discovery builds on earlier experiments in mice, in which switching off Mitch in muscle tissue increased energy expenditure, improved endurance, and protected the animals from diet-related obesity.

The findings, published in The EMBO Journal in January 2025, suggest that manipulating this protein could eventually offer a way to influence both fat burning and fat accumulation. Additional research published later that year has provided further evidence of its importance in regulating body fat and metabolism.

Why Scientists Are Searching for Better Obesity Treatments

Over the past decade, medications designed to promote weight loss have transformed obesity treatment. Drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide have helped many patients achieve substantial reductions in body weight, largely by influencing appetite and the body's responses to food.

But losing weight does not necessarily mean losing only fat.

Weight reduction can also involve a decline in lean body mass, which includes muscle and other tissues. Preserving muscle is especially important because it supports strength, mobility, and metabolic health.

A 2026 analysis of 20 clinical trials involving 15,782 participants found that lean mass accounted for approximately 25% to 39% of the weight lost with several widely used weight-loss medications. Importantly, the researchers found that comparable proportional losses also occurred with lifestyle interventions, while adding resistance training helped preserve more lean tissue.

These concerns have encouraged scientists to investigate ways to reduce body fat while protecting the muscles needed for long-term health.

Professor Atan Gross and his colleagues at the Weizmann Institute arrived at a possible avenue through an unexpected discovery involving Mitch.

Switching Off One Protein Protected Mice From Obesity

In earlier experiments, Gross's team reduced the activity of MTCH2 in the muscles of mice. The effects were striking.

The genetically modified animals showed increased metabolic activity and were resistant to obesity caused by their diet. Their muscles also developed more fibers with a high capacity for using oxygen, helping improve physical endurance.

The mice performed better in exercise and stress tests and showed improvements in heart function. In addition to resisting excess fat accumulation, they experienced favorable changes in their overall body composition.

The results suggested that Mitch was doing much more than controlling a single metabolic reaction. Somehow, this protein appeared to influence the body's ability to use energy, store fat, and sustain physical activity.

That raised an intriguing question: How could disabling one protein produce such widespread effects?

To investigate, the researchers turned their attention to mitochondria, the microscopic structures responsible for supplying much of the energy that cells need to function.

The Mitochondria Connection Behind Fat Burning

Mitochondria are frequently described as the powerhouses of cells because they convert energy from food into a usable form called ATP, or adenosine triphosphate.

ATP acts as a cellular energy currency. Muscles use it to contract, cells need it to grow and repair themselves, and countless biological processes depend on a steady supply.

However, mitochondria are not static structures. They constantly change shape, joining together into larger networks or separating into smaller units.

When mitochondria fuse, they can form interconnected systems that help coordinate energy production. When they divide, the resulting structures may operate differently, altering how efficiently cells produce and consume energy.

Gross's team had previously established that Mitch plays a major role in mitochondrial fusion, the process that allows separate mitochondria to merge.

Without this protein, the mitochondrial network becomes fragmented. Although the cells can still generate energy, they experience changes in energy efficiency and demand that can force them to consume more fuel.

This offered a possible explanation for the unusual metabolism observed in the mice. Instead of conserving energy efficiently, their cells appeared to be operating in a more energy-intensive state.

But would disabling Mitch have a similar effect in human cells?

Human Cells Begin Burning More Fat Without Mitch

To answer that question, researchers led by doctoral student Sabita Chourasia used genetic engineering to remove MTCH2 from human cells grown in the laboratory.

The change disrupted the normal structure of their mitochondrial networks. Individual mitochondria became more separated, and the balance between energy production and energy demand shifted.

The cells developed a persistent shortage of readily available energy relative to their needs, even as their mitochondria increased their oxygen-consuming activity.

To compensate, they began consuming more nutrients.

"After deleting Mitch, we examined, every few hours, the effect that had on more than 100 substances taking part in metabolism in human cells," Chourasia explains. "We saw an increase in cellular respiration, the process in which the cell produces energy from nutrients, such as carbohydrates and fats, using oxygen. This explains the increase in muscular endurance in previous experiments using mice."

Cellular respiration is the series of chemical reactions that allows cells to extract usable energy from nutrients. Increasing this activity generally requires a greater supply of fuel.

The researchers found that cells lacking Mitch consumed more fats, carbohydrates, and amino acids, the building blocks of proteins.

Fat metabolism was particularly revealing.

Rather than relying on their usual mix of energy sources, the altered cells increased their use of fatty acids for fuel. This indicated that MTCH2 influences not only how much energy cells consume, but also which nutrients they use to meet that demand.

"We discovered that deleting Mitch led to a major drop in fats in membranes," Gross explains. "At the same time, we saw an increase in fatty substances used to produce energy, and we realized that the fat was being broken down from the membrane to be used as fuel. In other words, we showed that Mitch determines the fate of fat in human cells."

Cell membranes are thin, flexible barriers made largely from fatty molecules called lipids. They protect cells and organize many of their essential functions.

The findings suggested that cells deprived of Mitch were changing how they distributed and used these lipids, drawing on some of their available fatty resources to support increased energy consumption.

Detailed analysis also revealed changes in stored lipids, demonstrating that the effect was more complex than simply eliminating cellular fat. Overall, the results showed that MTCH2 is a powerful regulator of how cells allocate nutrients between energy production and other biological needs.

The researchers then investigated whether Mitch also influences how fat cells develop.

This question was particularly interesting because previous studies had found elevated MTCH2 levels in fat tissue from women with obesity.

Fat cells, also known as adipocytes, do not appear fully formed. They develop from immature precursor cells through a process called adipogenesis, or fat cell differentiation.

As these precursor cells mature, they must build new cellular structures, produce fats, and activate genes that allow them to specialize in storing energy.

All of this requires considerable energy and raw materials.

Gross's team suspected that removing Mitch might disrupt the process by creating an environment in which cells were too busy consuming fuel to build the components needed for fat storage.

Experiments using mouse precursor fat cells supported this idea. When the researchers deleted MTCH2, far fewer cells successfully developed into mature fat cells.

In the published experiments, approximately 80% to 90% of normal precursor cells differentiated within six days, compared with only 5% to 10% of cells lacking MTCH2. The altered cells also accumulated substantially fewer lipid droplets, the structures used to store fat.

"When we deleted Mitch from the progenitor cells, we discovered that the environment created in these cells was not conducive to the synthesis of new fats," Gross explains. "Reducing the ability to synthesize membranes prevents the cells from growing, developing and reaching the point where differentiation is possible. The process of fat accumulation requires a large amount of available energy, but in cells without Mitch, there is a shortage of energy. In addition, the expression of genes necessary for differentiation is suppressed, and there is a shortage of the substances vital for this process to occur. As a result, differentiation of new fat cells is reduced, along with fat accumulation."

In other words, disabling Mitch appeared to affect fat metabolism in two distinct ways. It increased the consumption of energy-rich nutrients and interfered with the cellular machinery needed to develop new fat-storing cells.

Importantly, the fat cell differentiation experiments used a mouse-derived cell line, while the detailed analysis of nutrient consumption was conducted in human cells. The combined results suggest a broader role for the protein, but they do not establish that blocking MTCH2 would prevent new fat cells from forming in people.

Follow-Up Research Reveals Another Fat-Burning Mechanism

The importance of MTCH2 became even clearer in research published on October 3, 2025, in Nature Communications.

An international research team, which included Gross and Chourasia, investigated how MTCH2 influences metabolism in fat tissue. By analyzing human and mouse fat samples, studying cultured fat cells, and conducting experiments in genetically modified mice, the scientists identified another mechanism linking this protein to energy expenditure.

They found that MTCH2 helps regulate the movement of fatty acids into mitochondria, where these molecules can be broken down to produce energy.

The process involves an enzyme called carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, or CPT1. This enzyme acts as an important checkpoint, helping determine how much fat can enter mitochondria for use as fuel.

Normally, CPT1 activity is regulated by a molecule called malonyl-CoA, which signals when cells should limit fatty acid consumption.

The researchers discovered that MTCH2 physically interacts with CPT1 and influences how strongly this regulatory signal affects the enzyme.

Removing MTCH2 altered that control system, allowing mitochondria to increase fatty acid utilization and energy expenditure.

This provided a more detailed molecular explanation for why disabling the protein could encourage cells to burn additional fat.

Mice Burned More Energy Without Eating Less

The follow-up study also produced an especially promising result.

When researchers removed MTCH2 specifically from the fat cells of mice, the animals burned more energy without increasing their physical activity or changing how much food they consumed.

Under a regular diet, the genetically modified mice gained weight at a rate similar to that of normal mice.

However, when the animals were fed a high-fat diet, a difference emerged.

Mice lacking MTCH2 in their fat tissue gained less weight and accumulated less body fat than untreated mice, despite consuming similar amounts of food.

Their lean body mass remained comparable to that of the control animals.

The genetically modified mice also demonstrated improved glucose tolerance, indicating that their bodies were better able to manage blood sugar after receiving glucose. In addition, their cholesterol levels were lower.

These findings strengthened the evidence that MTCH2 influences whole-body metabolism, rather than simply changing the behavior of isolated cells.

The scientists also found that its metabolic effects could occur independently of UCP1, a protein commonly associated with heat production in brown fat. Brown fat is a specialized form of body fat that helps generate heat by burning energy, whereas white fat primarily stores excess energy.

Discovering an additional pathway for increasing energy expenditure could be important because it offers researchers another potential target for treating obesity and related metabolic disorders.

Could This Protein Lead to Better Weight-Loss Treatments?

Together, the findings suggest that MTCH2 may be an important molecular switch linking fat storage, energy consumption, and the development of fat cells.

The original experiments showed that removing the protein could increase nutrient consumption in human cells and interfere with fat cell formation in mouse cells. The later research demonstrated that selectively removing MTCH2 from fat tissue could increase energy expenditure and protect mice against excessive weight gain on a high-fat diet.

That combination raises the possibility of developing treatments that change how the body handles energy, potentially complementing medications that primarily work by reducing appetite.

However, several major questions remain unanswered.

Scientists have not yet demonstrated that inhibiting MTCH2 produces safe, sustained weight loss in humans. The protein also participates in other important biological activities, including mitochondrial regulation and pathways involved in cell survival. Blocking it throughout the body could therefore produce unwanted consequences.

Preventing fat cells from forming is not necessarily beneficial in every situation, either. Healthy fat tissue plays an essential role in storing excess energy, maintaining blood sugar balance, and producing hormones. Disrupting its function could potentially redirect fats into organs where they may cause harm.

Researchers will need to determine whether MTCH2 can be targeted safely, which tissues would benefit most, and whether reducing its activity would preserve muscle health while promoting fat loss.

For now, established strategies such as resistance exercise and adequate protein intake remain important for protecting muscle during weight reduction. A 2026 review of clinical trials found that combining lifestyle changes with resistance training produced a more favorable pattern of lean mass preservation than weight-loss interventions without such exercise.

The research nevertheless points toward a compelling possibility: Rather than focusing exclusively on how much food the body takes in, future obesity treatments might also influence how cells decide whether to burn energy or store it.

And one protein, Mitch, appears to be deeply involved in that decision.

#2642. What’s a Heatwave?

A heatwave is defined as an area being unusually hot for 3 or more consecutive days, but what causes hot weather?

Hot weather is caused by high pressure. When an area of high pressure pushes down on the ground, the air compresses and becomes hotter and drier. The high-pressure system pushes clouds out of the way, allowing the sun to beat down on the ground and stopping cooler air from moving in.

A heatwave ends when the high-pressure system weakens. This is usually due to a cold front moving in. Dense, cool air slides beneath the lighter, warmer air, pushing it higher into the atmosphere. As the warm air rises, it cools.

Less commonly, the jet stream shifts, allowing cooler air (such as ocean breezes) to flow back over land. Or the intense heat leads to extreme humidity buildup, making the air unstable and triggering widespread thunderstorms that break the heat cycle.

During a heatwave, the air temperature rises, causing it to expand and become less dense. This warm air rises upwards, creating a convection current.

While warm air wants to rise, heatwaves are most often caused by a high pressure system ( heat dome ) sitting over a region. High pressure systems force air to sink down to the ground; as it sinks and becomes compressed, it heats up even more. The high pressure traps warm air, stopping it from rising and escaping into the atmosphere.

So while air close to the surface does rise and create small convection currents, the bigger weather system in a heatwave is caused by trapped hot air that can’t escape.

You can demonstrate convection currents with an underwater volcano, a candle carousel or a convection snake!

High temperatures can lead to higher than normal ozone levels when sunlight drives reactions between pollutants from cars and other sources. This can reduce the overall air quality.

#2644. UNEP: Opportunity has ‘been lost’ to meet 1.5C warming limit without ‘overshoot’

A temporary breach of the 1.5C global-warming limit is now “inevitable” following “decades of insufficient climate action”, says the UN Environment Programme (UNEP).

In its “sobering” annual assessment of the global “emissions gap”, UNEP says that exceeding the Paris Agreement’s 1.5C limit is “imminent and unavoidable”.

This year’s report, titled “The point of return”, says the “task now” is to minimise the size and scale of a temperature “overshoot” and to “establish a credible return path to 1.5C by the end of the century”.

Even the “lowest possible” overshoot scenario for 1.5C would first see global warming peak at around 1.7-1.8C in the middle of the century before declining, says the report.

This scenario – which is “still aligned with the long-term temperature goal of the Paris Agreement” – would require global emissions cuts of 18% by 2030 and 45% by 2035, while ramping up “removals” of carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere, says UNEP.

In contrast, it notes that the current climate pledges by countries around the world – even if fully implemented – would only reduce emissions by 5-8% by 2030 and 11-15 % by 2035, relative to 2025 levels.

Nonetheless, the report identifies “ample opportunity to accelerate action”, concluding that “countries can still limit peak warming, shorten the duration of overshoot and keep the goals of the Paris Agreement within reach”.

(See Carbon Brief’s detailed coverage of previous reports in 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025.)

The report says that emissions in 2025 were “comparable” to those of 2024.

Global greenhouse gas emissions in 2025 totalled 58.4bn tonnes of CO2 equivalent (GtCO2e), according to the report. This is around 0.16GtCO2e lower than 2024 levels – albeit, with high levels of uncertainty around the numbers.

In addition, because the figures presented in the 2025 report are “provisional”, the report says, it is “not possible to conclude on the overall direction of emissions in 2025”.

The emissions growth rate has been slowing down in recent decades: the growth rate over 1990-2015 was, on average, 1.7% per year, while emissions have increased by an average of 0.8% per year since 2016.

The chart below shows the total human-driven emissions between 1990 and 2025, separated by greenhouse gas: CO2 from fossil-fuel combustion (dark grey), methane (light grey), nitrous oxide (blue), fluorinated gases (orange) and CO2 from land-use change (green).

Compared to 2024, emissions from fossil fuels increased slightly, by between 0.4-1.1%, says the report. Emissions of both nitrous oxide and fluorinated gases increased by even higher rates, but since these comprise such a small part of total greenhouse gas emissions, “their contribution to the overall trend is relatively less significant”, it adds. Methane emissions remained steady over that time.

A reduction in the CO2 emissions from land use, land-use change and forestry (LULUCF) “slightly overcompensates” for the rise in emissions of fossil-fuel CO2 and other gases, according to the report. (For more on falling land-use emissions, see Carbon Brief’s explainer.)

The decrease in LULUCF emissions was “partially driven” by a decline in global wildfire emissions, particularly in Africa, South America and parts of south-east Asia. The report notes that these decreases were “likely connected to the ceasing of El Niño conditions”. The ongoing “super” El Niño is likely to cause LULUCF emissions to increase in 2026.

The report also examines countries’ contributions to the increase in greenhouse gas emissions – although here, it excludes LULUCF due to a lag in national-level emissions inventories for the sector.

Among the top six emitters, only the US saw a notable increase in 2025, says the report, whereas emissions in China, the EU, India, Russia and Indonesia were relatively flat. Emissions in the US grew by 133m tonnes of CO2e (MtCO2e) in 2025, due to increased electricity demand and a “rebound” of coal power. This growth in US emissions is likely due to a combination of market factors, including increased heating demand due to a colder winter, higher gas prices and overall increased electricity demand.

The changes for the six largest emitters, the rest of the G20 countries, international transport and the rest of the world can be seen in the chart below.

Across most sectors, progress towards decarbonisation has been “modest”, says the report. The most notable decreases have occurred in the power sector and the transport sector, due to the growth of solar power and electric vehicles. The report notes:

“There are emerging signs of transport sector electrification and decarbonisation, but at rates and levels that are not yet sufficient to drive emissions reductions at the global level.”

However, emissions from the transport sector have continued to grow, due to increased demand for international aviation and shipping.

The report notes that while electricity demand from data centres is “projected to double” by 2030, data centres at present account for less than 1% of total emissions.

All countries were meant to submit new UN climate pledges for 2035, known as the third nationally determined contributions (NDCs) to the Paris Agreement, by February 2025. However, 95% of nations missed this deadline.

Many countries came forward with their third NDCs at a UN climate summit in New York in September 2025 and at the COP30 climate summit in Brazil in November 2025.

By the end of COP30, 122 – or 63% – of countries had produced their third NDCs.

The report says that figure had risen to 124 by 1 September 2026, with pledges now covering 75% of global emissions.

This is more than double the number of third NDCs available at the time of the 2025 emissions gap report – allowing a “more robust basis for assessing collective progress and ambition toward 2035”, says the report.

Although the first global stocktake of action towards the goals of the Paris Agreement called for countries to submit targets “covering all greenhouse gases”, there has only been a “modest” increase in the number of nations to do so, according to the report.

It says that eight countries included greenhouse gas targets for the first time in their third NDCs, bringing the total number parties with such targets up to 155.

The third NDCs “increasingly use more robust and verifiable target formulations”, such as standardised baselines, says the report.

However, they have not “meaningfully broadened the scope of sectors and gases covered by the targets”, it adds.

If the third NDCs were implemented in full, it would lower global emissions in 2035 by 3.5GtCO2e, compared to 2030 targets, says the report.

It says this is “far from the global reductions needed to get on track to achieving the long-term temperature goal of the Paris Agreement”.

Compared to the 2025 report, the projections for global emissions in 2035 as a result the third NDCs are about 3GtCO2e higher.

This is down to the formal withdrawal of the US from the Paris Agreement, as well as from “harmonising” the projections with the latest historical emissions data, says the report.

The global “implementation gap” for 2030 – defined as the difference between countries’ projected emissions under current policies and the emissions expected if they implemented their NDCs in full – has narrowed compared to last year, says the report.

However, this is mainly due to the withdrawal of the US from the Paris Agreement. In other words, the narrowing of the gap is due to “policy changes, not policy progress”.

The implementation gap for both 2030 and 2035 are of a similar size – suggesting “enhanced collective ambition of 2035 NDCs is weak”, continues the report.

Some 14 of the 17 G20 nations, counting the EU as one, have submitted new NDCs with emissions targets for 2035, says the report.

However, the emissions trajectories of the majority of G20 nations remain “misaligned” with their net-zero targets, says the report.

The chart below shows the gap between nations’ current emission trajectories and their NDC targets for 2030 and 2035 (diamonds), along with their net-zero targets (circles).

All G20 members except Japan, the EU and UK would need to “accelerate their emission reduction rates based on current policies” to achieve their net-zero targets, adds the report.

The report goes on to estimate the “emissions gap” – the difference between projected global emissions if all NDCs are met in full and what would be needed to achieve the long-term temperature goal of the Paris Agreement.

For example, the authors explain, emissions are currently around 80% above the level that would have been needed by 2030 to limit warming to 1.5C with no or “low” overshoot.

Therefore, as a result of “decades of insufficient emission reductions”, the possibility of limiting warming to 1.5C with limited or no overshoot “has now been lost”, say the authors.

The overshoot scenario “minimise[s] both the magnitude and duration of overshoot above 1.5C”, say the authors, while meeting a defined set of “feasibility and sustainability guardrails”. This acknowledged – for the first time in a COP text – that there was likely to be an overshoot of 1.5C and urged that both its extent and duration needed to be “limit[ed]”.

The lowest 1.5C overshoot scenario requires “immediate, deep, rapid and sustained reductions”, says the report. Global greenhouse gas emissions would need to fall by 18% by 2030, 45% by 2035 and 69% by 2040, relative to 2025 levels. Global net-zero CO2 emissions would be reached around 2050.

These benchmarks for 1.5C effectively replace those in the IPCC’s AR6, which specified reductions of 43% by 2030 and 60% by 2035, relative to 2019 levels. These have been frequently cited in COP decisions and other declarations.

With the ongoing uncertainty around the timeline for the IPCC’s seventh assessment reports, the benchmarks in the UNEP report provide an important update on the emissions cuts required to limit overshoot.

For the 2C scenario, global greenhouse gas emissions would need to drop by 11% by 2030, 24% by 2035 and 37% by 2040, relative to 2025 levels, the report says.

These emissions gaps are “still large”, say the authors, with current NDCs and net-zero targets not being “enough to minimise overshoot”.

(The report also notes that because the 1.5C and 2C scenarios have changed, the emissions gaps in 2026 are not directly comparable with previous reports.)

Putting the world on track for the lowest 1.5C scenario would require global emissions to fall by 5.7% per year over the next decade, it says.

This is “even more rapid” than the 5% annual drop that would have been required to meet the 1.5C limit without overshoot, had emissions cuts started in 2019, adds the report.

To explore different future emission pathways and their impact on global temperatures, the report uses a simple climate model, or “emulator”, called FaIR.

The report estimates that current national policies would cause 2.8C of global warming over the century, with a 66% chance. Under this scenario, there is also a 10% chance that warming would exceed 3.4C.

If unconditional NDCs are implemented, warming this century could reach 2.7C, with a 66% chance, it says.

If all NDCs are fully implemented, warming could reach 2.6C by 2100, with a 66% chance.

In the “most optimistic” scenario, where countries fully implement their NDCs and reach their net-zero targets, there is a 66% chance that warming could be limited to 2.1C by the end of this century.

Compared to last year, the central warming estimates in 2100 for the NDC scenarios are about 0.2-0.3C higher, says the report.

This is because the projected global emissions as a result of the 2035 NDCs have increased. Again, this is down to the US withdrawal from the Paris Agreement and higher estimates of historical emissions.

While fully implementing all NDCs and net-zero pledges could cut warming by 0.6C, this would still be 0.25C higher than the lowest 1.5C overshoot pathways, says the report.

It adds that this “highlights the need both to close the implementation gap between current policies and existing NDCs and net-zero pledges, and to strengthen NDCs or exceed existing commitments”.

As noted above, the report continues that, “with immediate, deep, rapid and sustained emission cuts”, it would still be possible to limit peak warming to 1.7-1.8C, with a 66% chance.

If these emission cuts are “combined with an ambitious and sustainable scale-up of CO2 removal”, then warming could be returned to 1.5C by the end of the century, it continues.

However, “continued delay” in reducing global emissions would “further worsen the climate outcomes still achievable”, it says, adding:

“For every five years of delay in starting to cut emissions, around another 0.1C of warming will be locked in.

“Continued insufficient emission reductions over the next decades could make warming of 2C inevitable, putting the Paris Agreement at risk of failure.”

The report also looks at how the world is measuring up against the mitigation actions set out in the first “global stocktake”, which was agreed at the COP28 climate summit in Dubai, in 2023.

The global stocktake is a key part of the Paris regime that assesses progress towards meeting the goals of the agreement. After each stocktake is completed, countries are supposed to increase their ambition as needed to get on track with their collective targets.

The report looks at nine “mitigation action areas” agreed in the outcome of the first global stocktake. Overall, the report says, progress in those areas is “uneven and insufficient”.

However, it does note that there are “several remarkable examples of progress that exceeded expectations”.

In particular, the deployment of renewable energy has increased substantially more than was expected by future projections made in 2015 using then-current policies, notes the report. The increase in overall renewable capacity was three times higher than expected under the previous projections. As a result, CO2 emissions related to energy generation increased by 40% less than was expected.

The world is “lagging particularly far behind” where it needs to be to limit warming to the lowest possible overshoot of the 1.5C target in two areas: transitioning away from fossil fuels and halting deforestation, says the report. (Deforestation is the largest contributor to LULUCF emissions.)

The report notes that the amount of energy sourced from fossil fuels “continues to rise globally, albeit at a slower rate than was projected in 2015”. Under current policies, it says, global coal usage may peak by 2030, but oil and gas use is “likely to keep increasing”.

Furthermore, it says, global efforts to stop deforestation by 2030 “remain significantly off track”. Achieving the end-of-decade target would require accelerating the slowdown in forest loss by “more than a factor of 10”.

#2646. The Quest for the Perfect Dark Mode

Maybe the hardest / most complicated part of building my statically-generated blog was adding Dark Mode.

Not the live-embedded code snippets, not the unified GraphQL layer that manages and aggregates all content and data, not the custom analytics system, not the myriad bits of whimsy. Freaking Dark Mode.

It's a good reminder that when it comes to software development, a simple feature can have a complex implementation.

The reason that this problem is so dastardly has to do with how frameworks like Next.js work; the HTML is generated ahead of time. If you're not careful, you'll wind up with that telltale flicker, where the user sees the wrong colors for a brief moment.

I like to think of this as a "chasm" in space and time. There's the moment where we build our code, on our computer or in the cloud. And then there's the moment the client runs our code, on their device, at a very different place and time.

We want to write some code that will be injected at compile-time, but only executed at run-time. We need to pass that function as if it was a piece of data, to be run on the user's device.

We have to do it this way because we don't have the right information yet; we don't know what's in the user's localStorage, or whether their operating system is running in dark mode. We won't know that until we're running code on the user's device.

The opposite is also true! When this code eventually runs, it won't have access to any of our bundled JS code; it will run before the bundle has even been downloaded! That means it won't automatically know what our design tokens are.
