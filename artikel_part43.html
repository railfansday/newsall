#2104. This Nike race medal is meant to be worn as jewelry

For its 2026 After Dark Tour, a nighttime race series of 10Ks and half-marathons for women, Nike designed a multipurpose medal that’s meant to be worn long after race day as a necklace, bracelet, keychain loop, or belt chain.

It features a Nike Swoosh and chain adorned with miniature laser-engraved Swooshes that can fit in the palm of your hand. Each comes with a city-specific carabiner for the places where the races take place: London; Shanghai; Los Angeles; Mexico City; Sydney; Manila, Philippines; and Mumbai, India. Most finisher medals end up in a drawer, the thinking goes, but this medal was designed to leave the house.

“The Nike After Dark Tour isn’t a traditional race, so the medal didn’t need to be traditional either,” Elizabeth Brock-Jones, Nike’s footwear development director, tells Fast Company.

The new medal was designed by an all-woman team, which helped get the details just right.

“Because women led every stage of the process, we designed for things we’d experience ourselves: how it hangs when you move, whether it catches in your hair, whether you can open the clasp with longer nails,” Brock-Jones says. “We wanted something women would actually reach for, not just something that looked good on paper.”

The carabiner took about seven rounds to perfect and the chain system at least five. To cut down on the design timeline, the team traveled to Italy to work directly with its manufacturing partner to make decisions in real time instead of waiting for samples.

For Nike, the new medal is the latest example of the sportswear brand’s outreach to women, a demographic group it’s courting through partnerships like NikeSkims, advertising, and events, like competitions. Nike hosted a series of women’s races in major cities from 2005 to 2015. Then last year it launched the After Dark Tour, a race series it said was “designed to be the ultimate girls’ weekend.”

The 2025 medal was unconventional, too. It featured a shoelace-inspired chain that worked like a reflective, sweat-proof bolo tie on one side with a magnetic closure on the other and a textured Nike Swoosh. After seeing how runners actually wore it, the design team was inspired to make this year’s medals intentionally as something that could be worn as jewelry.

“We saw women styling the 2025 medal in ways we never intended,” Brock-Jones says. “They put it on bags, layered it with their own jewelry, and made it part of their everyday look.”

Nike also designed a small, matching accessories collection including hoop and stud earrings featuring swooshes. The products are available to After Dark runners as a gift with purchase at select race locations.

All race medals are mementos, but by turning this medal into a multipurpose jewelry piece, Nike hopes it will keep the race—not to mention everything that led up to it—much more front of mind.

“Every time she sees it, we hope it brings her back to the early mornings, the long training runs, and the setbacks she pushed through to get there, while reminding her what she’s capable of next,” Brock-Jones says.

#2107. Amgen says drug combo cuts risk of death by 58% in patients with aggressive lung cancer

A drug from Amgen could give patients with a notoriously aggressive form of lung cancer significantly more time to live when added to an immunotherapy from AstraZeneca, a study found.

Full results from a late-stage trial released Saturday show that adding Amgen's immunotherapy Imdelltra to AstraZeneca's Imfinzi drug slashed the risk of death by 58% in patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer, a fast-growing disease that has spread beyond the lung where it began.

The drug combination extended their median overall survival to 21.1 months compared with 12.7 months with AstraZeneca's drug alone, the trial found.

That means half of patients in the group that took the combination regimen were alive for at least roughly 21 months. The trial tested the combo as a maintenance therapy in patients who received initial treatment with Imfinzi and chemotherapy, and whose cancer had not progressed.

"I think there are moments in medicine when you see a piece of data that is so profound that it's going to transform the standard of care for patients, and I think, honestly, we're at that moment," said Dr. Paul Burton, Amgen's chief medical officer, in an interview.

The combination "has the potential to take on one of the most aggressive cancers that we deal with today, and almost convert it into a chronic disease," he said, adding that "these data say that's a possibility."

The results also suggest a greater commercial opportunity for Imdelltra, which posted $288 million in sales during the second quarter, up 115% year over year. The drug already generated $627 million in global sales in 2025 and appears to be on its way to becoming a blockbuster medicine, or hitting the $1 billion sales threshold.

Small cell lung cancer remains difficult to treat because, even if chemotherapy initially works well for most patients, the disease often returns or progresses within months, said lead trial investigator Dr. Jacob Sands, who is the associate chief of the Lowe Center for Thoracic Oncology at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute.

He said immunotherapy has helped a small subset of patients achieve years of disease control, but most have not experienced similarly durable benefits, highlighting the need for better treatments. There are roughly 31,000 new small cell lung cancer cases in the U.S. each year, and an estimated 60% to 70% of those people are diagnosed with extensive-stage disease.

Sands said the benefits in the trial were seen across subgroups of patients, including those whose cancer spread to the brain or liver. Disease that has spread to those organs is associated with poorer treatment outcomes.

He added that with longer-term follow-up data on the patients, the 21-month figure "may evolve further and hopefully will substantially improve," similar to what Amgen saw in a Phase 1 trial on the immunotherapy combination. In that early-stage trial on a smaller number of patients, a long-term follow-up showed the benefit was sustained for years.

Amgen said the findings also suggest that using Imdelltra earlier in a patient's treatment could meaningfully alter the trajectory of the disease. Imdelltra is currently approved for patients whose small cell lung cancer has progressed while they are on, or after, platinum-based chemotherapy.

Researchers specifically tested whether adding Imdelltra could help patients live longer than continuing Imfinzi alone, which is already approved as a first treatment for this type of lung cancer. Amgen said nearly 80% of patients who started taking both drugs were alive at one year compared with 53% receiving Imfinzi alone.

Imdelltra's "big impact" on controlling the disease and ultimately how long people live highlights the importance of including it in first-line maintenance treatment, Sands said. That refers to ongoing therapy given after an initial heavy treatment, such as chemotherapy or surgery.

Burton added that it positions Amgen's drug to be an "indispensable backbone therapy" for small cell lung cancer, regardless of the other immunotherapy that can be paired with it.

Imdelltra binds to DLL3, a protein on cancer cells, and CD3, a protein on T cells, which are a type of immune cell that can kill cancer. By binding to both, Imdelltra brings T cells into close contact with tumor cells, helping the immune system destroy the cancer.

Imfinzi, made by AstraZeneca, is an immunotherapy that blocks a protein called PD-L1, which cancer cells use to evade the immune system. The drugs work together in complementary ways: Imfinzi helps remove the brakes on the immune response, while Imdelltra brings T cells directly to cancer cells and helps activate them to attack the tumor.

The study, which was presented at a major cancer meeting called the European Society for Medical Oncology Congress, did not test whether the combination was better than other first-line treatment approaches.

Still, the findings suggest Amgen's drug could be another option offered to small cell lung cancer patients earlier in their treatment. Kate Brenan, a 44-year-old mother of two from Denver, sees that as a win on its own.

She was diagnosed with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer in early January 2024. Brenan underwent six rounds of a common combination chemotherapy, followed by chest radiation and later whole-brain radiation after the cancer spread.

In September 2024, she started Imdelltra and has had no evidence of disease ever since.

"I think that it's remarkable how quickly things are changing in the world of small cancer treatment options," Brenan told CNBC, adding that Imdelltra potentially becoming an earlier treatment option "can only be a good thing for the patients moving forward, and I'm really excited about that."

If you know you are going to be traveling by plane this Thanksgiving—and you haven’t purchased your tickets yet—you shouldn’t wait much longer. As a matter of fact, the ideal time to purchase your Thanksgiving flights may have just passed.

According to a blog post from the travel experts at Google Flights, the search giant’s flight aggregator and comparison tool, there is a period before the holiday when flights are generally at their lowest. This period is generally 21 days to 57 days before departure.

So, if your Thanksgiving flight departure date is actually on Thanksgiving—November 26 this year—that means airfares will be at their lowest between September 30 and November 5 (adjust those dates accordingly to your actual Thanksgiving departure plans).

The prices during this period, of course, are not fixed. They’ll change by the day based on demand and other metrics. On some days, the same flight could be more expensive or cheaper. 

But Google Flights notes that there is one day on which its data shows that Thanksgiving flights are generally at their lowest. That day, for 2026, is 34 days before departure.

Using flying on Thanksgiving morning as an example, the 34-day prior mark means airfare was most likely lowest on Friday, October 23. If you’re planning on flying on the Wednesday or Tuesday before Thanksgiving, the cheapest fares for you were likely on October 22 or October 21.

Does that mean you’re out of luck if you haven’t already purchased your Thanksgiving flights? Not necessarily. Remember, Google says flights should remain at their lowest until about 21 days before departure this year. That gives you until the first few days of November to purchase your airfare—but the sooner, the better.

When is the best time to purchase airfare for Christmas 2026?

If you haven’t purchased your Christmas airfare yet, you should do it soon. That’s because, according to Google, the lowest prices for Christmas flights occur much farther out than they do for Thanksgiving flights.

According to Google, the lowest prices for Christmas flights are 33 to 67 days before departure. Given Christmas is on December 25, and assuming you would be flying that morning, that means that the lowest prices for Christmas flights would generally be found between October 19 and November 22. Of course, since most people travel before Christmas Day, those dates shift even earlier.

If you’re looking for the day with the statistically lowest Christmas airfare prices this year, Google says the “lowest prices hit 56 days before departure” in 2026. If you are traveling on Christmas Day, that means the day you are likely to find the cheapest flights will be this coming Friday, October 30.

Can popular airfare hacks save me even more money on Thanksgiving and Christmas flights?

When it comes to airfare, the internet is full of advice that purportedly shows you how to save even more—from using VPNs to buying tickets on a Tuesday to clearing your browser’s cookies.

But could these so-called hacks actually save you money on your Thanksgiving or Christmas flights?

It’s unlikely, no matter what time of year you are flying. That’s according to Google Flight’s James Byers, who is a group product manager at Google Search. Last year, I asked Byers if Google Flights data showed whether any of the three most common bits of conventional wisdom about buying airfare held up—at least when searching for flights on Google’s aggregator.

Byers revealed that clearing cookies or using Incognito mode didn’t impact airfares on Google Flights. When it comes to using VPNs to try to trick airlines into thinking you’re in a country where prices may be lower, Byers said it may work—until you have to turn over your billing address for your payment method. At that point the VPN-adjusted local fare will likely no longer be available to you. 

As for buying flights on a Tuesday, supposedly the day of the week airfare is cheapest, Byers said there was some truth to that. But airfares were generally only about 1.3% lower than on the most expensive day, Sunday.

As for this holiday season, Google Flights data show that the best time to buy, if you want the lowest airfare possible for your Thanksgiving or Christmas trip, is quickly approaching its end.

#2115. Data Binding in React

So you have a bit of state in React, and you want to sync it with a form field. How do you do it?

Well, it depends on the type of form control: text inputs, selects, checkboxes, and radio buttons all work a little bit differently.

The good news is that while the details vary, they all share the same fundamental mechanism. There's a consistent philosophy in React when it comes to data binding.

In this tutorial, we'll first learn how React approaches data binding, and then I'll show you how each form field works, one by one. We'll look at complete, real-world examples. I'll also share some tips I've learned over the years, and some “gotchas” to watch out for!

This article is intended for beginner-to-intermediate React developers. You don't need a ton of React experience, but I do assume you're already familiar with state, event handlers, things like that.

So let's start with the core mechanism React uses for data binding.

By default, React takes a very “hands off” approach. It creates the DOM node for us and then leaves it alone. This is known as an uncontrolled element, since React isn't actively managing it.
