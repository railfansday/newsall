#2703. Scientists Are Teaching Birds to Sing Lost Songs

This article was originally published by The Guardian and is reproduced here as part of the Climate Desk collaboration.

A rufous-collared sparrow. Charles J. Sharp via Wikipedia

When bird populations decline, fledglings may struggle to learn the songs they need to attract mates. But new research suggests it is possible to teach birds lost songs.

Listening to birdsong is one of life’s great joys, but when researchers heard a particular tune emerging from a tree in a Buenos Aires park, they were especially jubilant. This arrangement was among those chirped by the local rufous-collared sparrows in the 1960s, but had since disappeared from the area.

That was until scientists created a computer-generated rendition, curious to find out if birds might consider it realistic enough to imitate. Less than three months later, a local avian resident began to copy parts of the song.

There is a growing recognition of birdsong’s importance to conservation, and of the need to better understand how birds learn to sing. If an individual sings well, and in a familiar “dialect,” they are better placed to find a mate, defend their territory, and ultimately survive.

If a species’ population declines, however, there are fewer opportunities for fledglings to become good singers. Birds can begin to tweet abnormally or mistakenly learn the songs of other species, jeopardizing their chances of breeding.

Male birds are in a better position to attract a mate if they can sing well — which, broadly speaking, means their song is complex, sung consistently, and well performed. “If your song requires a lot of energy, you might have to be in quite a good condition to sing it,” says Rebecca Lewis, a conservation scientist at Chester Zoo.

“When people talk about biodiversity, we’re always talking about genes, about genetic material,” says Gabriel B. Mindlin, professor of physics at the University of Buenos Aires, who coauthored the study on rufous-collared sparrows, and whose work is featured in a Guardian-produced documentary, Dream of Birds.

But animals’ learned behavior or culture, such as birdsong, is also an essential part of their population, says Mindlin.

The rufous-collared sparrow is not considered vulnerable globally, but researchers hope that synthetic recordings could help teach birdsong to other species whose populations are dwindling, allowing conservationists to tutor individuals where real-life recordings do not exist.

About 4,000 of the world’s 10,000 bird species are songbirds (or oscine passerines), such as finches, sparrows, and blackbirds, which generally have complex songs that are at least partly learned from other individuals. Their songs are hugely diverse, though separate research on the wider passerine group of birds, which incorporates songbirds, has identified common “motifs” or building blocks: three types of trill, three types of whistle, chaotic notes, and harmonic notes.

The way that birds use these motifs reflects the environment in which the birds evolved. Flat whistles are more likely to be sung by birds in tropical rainforests where sound must travel through dense vegetation. Complex motifs, such as ultrafast trills, are more common in temperate regions where birds tend to communicate at closer range.

The song studied by Mindlin was recreated based on hand-drawn annotations of rufous-collared sparrow songs that were made in the 1960s. Each song consists of a trill, which is consistent among individuals in the same region, and a theme, which varies. Some of the themes recorded decades ago are still sung by the local birds, but others have died out.

It is not clear why the song studied by Mindlin had disappeared, though he notes the population of birds in the park had declined.

Birds’ songs can change naturally over time — for example, birds might make errors when learning songs, which then can become imprinted in future renditions. Human encroachment and population declines can also cause dramatic changes.

Songbirds learn from other members of their species — such as a father or neighbors — which results in local groups developing their own dialects. The window for learning varies from a few weeks to a lifetime. Some species also have an innate level of knowledge.

“There’s such a diversity in songbirds in how they learn,” says Sarah Collins, associate professor of animal behavior at the University of Plymouth, who studied a group of cirl buntings that were translocated from Devon to Cornwall as part of a conservation project.

Initially, the birds produced abnormal songs that were squeaky and slow, and far less diverse than those of the source populations in Devon. “They didn’t have the richness of sound,” says Collins. Eight years later, however, their songs had returned to normal.

A few of the chicks were played recordings of cirl sounds, though not all. The study suggests that recovery of song is possible, though it is unclear if other species would bounce back in the same way. Cirl buntings are born with knowledge of the elements that make up the “notes” used in their songs, says Collins. “What they learn is how to put those elements together,” she says.

Some songbird species have a similar genetic underpinning and so may also be able to recover their songs, but others do not. Either way, Collins recommends a tutor to help other birds that are being translocated.

A separate study in Australia tracked regent honeyeaters that were bred in zoos to boost a critically endangered wild population that had fallen to about 250 individuals. Interacting with just two wild-born male “tutors” helped the zoo-bred birds to learn to sing the wild songs. Thereafter, zoo-bred males that had learned the traditional songs went on to tutor the next generation.

“Sometimes birds don’t learn very well from CDs or just playbacks. They need some sort of interaction,” says Collins.

Bringing wild birds into captivity to teach hand-reared chicks could cause huge distress and bring disease, however, risking the health of individuals from an already endangered population. It is possible that A.I. could help provide live tutoring in the future.

Dialect is also important. Past translocation projects have shown that birds are slower to match if they do not sing in the same dialect. “Birds with mismatched songs don’t pair quite as quickly or do quite as well once they’re paired,” adds Lewis. Familiarizing birds with songs of their destination can help to mitigate this.

A huge volume of birdsong is now being recorded and uploaded into databases, including by a growing number of hobbyists interested in birdwatching, says Mindlin. “One day we’ll be grateful to have all those records,” he says. “I don’t think we still have an idea that really we have to register dialects and communication systematically.”

In Buenos Aires, the song from the 1960s can still be heard. “It’s still being sung by just a few individuals, pretty close to where we inserted it,” he says, though only time will tell how long it persists.

Do you know that adding carbon dioxide to water makes it more acidic? We used red cabbage indicator to test the pH of tap water and carbonated water from a Sodastream. The tap water was slightly alkaline, and the fizzy water was acidic!

#2705. Analysis: Wind and solar save UK from gas imports worth £5.9bn during Hormuz crisis

The UK has avoided the need for gas imports worth £5.9bn since the start of the Hormuz crisis as a result of record generation from wind and solar, reveals Carbon Brief analysis.

While gas prices are surging to levels not seen since the 2022 energy crisis, the UK has been generating record amounts of power from wind and solar, up 14% year-on-year.

This unprecedented clean-power generation is directly cutting the need for gas-fired electricity, which is down by nearly 10% year-on-year in 2026 to date.

In total, wind and solar have generated a record 41% share of the UK’s electricity needs in 2026 to date, compared with 25% from gas, according to Carbon Brief’s analysis.

The figure below shows that wind and solar has avoided the need for UK gas imports worth £5.9bn, since the outbreak of war between the US and Iran in February 2026.

These avoided gas imports would have required the UK to secure the equivalent of more than 100 additional tanker deliveries of liquefied natural gas (LNG).

The £1.3bn import saving in September 2026 to date is the result of record wind and solar output, at nearly 10 terawatt hours (TWh), combined with surging gas prices.

The latest analysis updates figures published by Carbon Brief in April and May.

Wholesale gas prices in the UK have remained elevated ever since Russia cut off supplies to Europe in the wake of its invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

Gas averaged 90p per therm from 2023 until the start of this year, roughly three times above 2019 prices, before the Covid and Ukraine crises.

Since the outbreak of war in the Middle East in March, gas prices have climbed higher still, averaging 134p per therm or nearly four times the level seen in 2019.

In September 2026 to date, gas prices have averaged 189p per therm, reaching their highest level since the global energy crisis in 2022, as shown in the figure below.

UK gas prices are spiking again because winter is approaching – meaning higher demand for heating – and there is no end in sight for the Hormuz crisis.

At the same time, European gas stocks are low. This means Europe will have to compete with Asia to secure the cargoes of LNG needed to keep warm.

In the UK, high wholesale gas prices are hitting household gas bills under the price cap set by energy regulator Ofgem, whereas electricity bills have barely increased.

From this Thursday, 1 October, typical household gas bills will be 33% higher than they were in April, some £200 per year, according to thinktank Nesta.

In contrast, household electricity bills will only have risen 4%, according to Nesta’s analysis, as a result of growing generation from clean-energy sources.

Andrew Sissons, director for sustainable future at Nesta, explained in a social media post that “the link between electricity and gas prices has already begun to break”.

The UK and other fossil-fuel importing nations are being hit not only by high gas prices, but also by high prices for oil, diesel and other refined fuels.

The EU has reportedly had to pay an extra €100bn for fossil-fuel imports since the start of the crisis. Higher import bills have fallen particularly hard on low and middle-income countries, according to separate analysis.

In the UK, diesel prices have hit record levels of around £2 per litre. In contrast, recent Carbon Brief analysis shows that electric cars are up to nine times cheaper to drive.

In her speech to the Labour party conference, energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh said that energy bills were high because the UK is “exposed to global fossil-fuel markets”.

In his own conference speech, prime minister Andy Burnham said the expansion of clean energy was easing the impact of high gas prices on electricity bills. He said:

“We are already taking more control of our electricity prices with a massive expansion of home-grown renewables and nuclear. I have asked Miatta to speed up the breaking of the link between what we pay for power at home and the international gas market, to get bills down.”

The ability to create protein scaffolds that impart enantioselectivity to simple, readily available metal catalysts could redefine the landscape of asymmetric catalysis. Artificial metalloenzymes (ArMs), hybrid catalysts that combine a synthetic metal cofactor with a protein scaffold, offer a potential means to achieve this goal, but scaffold discovery has remained constrained by trial-and-error approaches and the functional limits of extant proteins.1–5 This challenge is acute for metal polypyridine photocatalysts, which are widely used in visible-light chemistry but have not enabled highly enantioselective photocatalysis within protein scaffolds. In this study, we show that generative protein design6–8 can create de novo scaffolds that selectively bind these photocatalysts and that directed evolution converts the resulting artificial metalloenzymes into efficient, highly enantioselective photocatalysts9. The evolved proteins support diverse light-driven reactions, high catalyst turnover and catalyst recycling, and can function with commercially available racemic metal complexes. Spectroscopic studies show that the protein environment extends the photocatalyst excited-state lifetime and organizes substrate near the cofactor, altering energy transfer and controlling stereoselectivity. These findings establish de novo protein design as a route to functional scaffolds for synthetic metal complexes that lack natural protein partners and provide a strategy for combining protein design and evolution to control non-natural catalytic chemistry.

#2708. Effective Collaboration with Product and Design

If you had 15 minutes of a developer's time, and your goal was to help them become more effective/productive at their job by teaching them something you've learned, what would you pick?*Framed another way, how would you fulfill the Geico promise: “15 minutes could save you 15% or more on development efforts”?

This blog post is my answer to that question. It's roughly a 15 minute read, and I believe it could potentially save you hundreds of hours of development effort throughout your career (among other benefits). I believe it's the highest-ROI tip I have, and I'm excited to talk about it. 😄

It's not about JavaScript, though, or even about programming more broadly. As a community, we've done an incredible job optimizing along the engineering axis; we have brilliant frameworks, tools, and services. This problem space has been well-harvested, and there's not as much low-hanging fruit left. But there are many other areas we can improve our skills in!

Most of the work we do, especially on the front-end, is intertwined with product and design. It's likely that you spend a good chunk of your time working on implementing a vision that other team members provide. Communication and trust play a huge role in how effectively we work together, and it can make an enormous difference in our velocity.

I used to work for Khan Academy(opens in new tab), a non-profit organization on a mission to provide a free, world-class education to anyone, anywhere. I worked on the classroom team, building tools to be used by teachers and their students.

We discovered pretty quickly that teachers really dislike having product updates shipped mid-semester; it disrupts their workflow, and eats up extremely limited time. A teacher might only have 5 minutes before a class starts to check in on how their students are doing. Product releases disrupt that.

For this and many other reasons, Khan Academy structured most of their releases around the “back-to-school” calendar: we aimed to have new stuff launched by July/August, in time for the new school year (in the northern hemisphere). Especially on the classroom team, we couldn't ship new features after school started, so we had very firm deadlines.

In 2018, our squad worked on a project code-named rainbow road, centered around revamping the tools teachers use outside of class to understand what their students' needs are. Our plan was super aggressive: we were revamping 5 key product areas, and launching a bunch of new functionality. For a while, it was very uncertain whether we would actually finish in time!

In the end, we met all of our core commitments! Like in many real-world projects, there was a bit of creative scope-cutting needed, but we delivered a working product in time for back-to-school, and teachers (and their students!) really benefitted from the work we did.

I believe a huge part of how we were able to pull it off was the tight collaboration we had between product/design and engineering.

A product manager once told me that he had three levers he could pull on, in order to alleviate pressure when work is behind schedule:

The first option, adding people, feels precarious to me. It can backfire; adding people doesn't always mean the work will get done faster. New developers need to gather a bunch of context, and sometimes that context exists only within the current developers' heads. Instead of speeding things up, it can slow things down.

The second option, time, might not be an option at all, especially if there are external, immovable deadlines. This is an underutilized lever in our industry, in my opinion, but sometimes you really can't push back a deadline.

Scope cuts are generally decided by product managers, but they don't make that decision alone. It often starts with a conversation between the designer and the developer. If these two people trust each other, the process is collaborative, cordial, and productive. They figure out the best solution under the current constraints, taking development time and user experience into account, and then pitch it to the PM. If the PM trusts the designer and developer, and their proposed solution works from a product perspective, it's typically approved without any fuss.

This workflow is awesome, and I believe it was critical in our success in the Rainbow Road project: if we weren't able to course-correct with so little friction, there's no way we would have been able to ship in time.

This workflow only works if all parties trust each other, though.

So here's a fun exercise. Imagine you're at a party, and someone asks what you do. You mention that you're a developer for Company X. The person pulls out their phone and navigates to Company X's homepage, which is a page you built.

For many devs, there's a certain amount of pride with this kind of thing. The code that you wrote is running on this person's phone! Even though many other people worked very hard on it, you were the one who translated those efforts into a tangible, interactive thing. You were the final step in the process, and your work is on full display.

Now imagine that you're in the same situation, except you're the UI/UX designer who worked on Company X's homepage. The mockup you designed a few months ago is noticeably different from what you're seeing on this person's phone. The fonts are all wrong, the spacing is off, there's a weird glitch on scroll. It's not really your work. Not without a lot of asterisks and caveats, at least.

Designers work hard to create a design that looks good and works well for the end user, but their work is funneled through an implementation process, one which invariably leaves a mark. The end result will never exactly match their design. Sometimes, it's worlds apart. And yet, their professional reputation is often all about which real-world products they've delivered, not how many beautiful mockups they've designed.

This is a situation that can very easily lead to resentment and conflict. I've worked on teams where there was a pervasive icy tension between designers and developers. And, frankly, I think developers are to blame most of the time. It's usually our fault.

It's worth recognizing that we haven't always been perfect teammates. The first step in building trust with our design and product friends is to show them that we take their concerns seriously.

Generally what happens in these situations is that the developer tells the designer to create a ticket for these nits, where it'll likely be assigned a P4 and lost in a sea of other low-priority tickets.

Another thing: designers usually aren't familiar enough with the web platform to be able to tell what's easy and what's hard (and they shouldn't need to be, since this is our job, not theirs). Multi-line ellipsis sounds like it should be easy, but we're saying it isn't. In effect, we're asking them to trust us.

Imagine if I had said something like this:

Hi [designer name] - some of your requested changes are non-trivial, so I won't be able to get to them. Sorry.

The designer might wonder, “is this actually a hard thing, or are they making excuses because they don't want to deal with it?”. They might think we're giving them a canned line, so that we can get back to other work that interests us more.

This is how trust is made or broken. Designers typically work with many different developers; they can tell which developers are making an earnest effort to implement their feedback, and which ones aren't interested.

It's important that we establish a trusting relationship, because these are the same people we'll be talking with when we're a week from a deadline and we need to make hard choices about scope.

Scope discussions involve a negotiation between what's best for the user and what's feasible given the current development constraints. If the designer knows that you're earnestly trying to find the best solution, they'll likely be more receptive to alternative suggestions, and much more willing to collaborate on a solution. If the designer feels like you're just trying to do the minimum possible, though, they'll likely stand firm and insist that the design is followed precisely.

Coming up with suitable alternatives is a skill on its own. You may find yourself in a predicament, where you recognize that the design will be very hard to implement as-specified, but you also don't have any other ideas.

You shouldn't let that stop you from starting a conversation. I would say something like this:

Hi there - I saw that the design features [seemingly-simple thing]. Unfortunately, because of [technical constraint], implementing this would probably take 3-4 days of development time. I wanted to check and see how critical this element is before I invest that kind of time, and see if maybe you had alternative ideas we could try.

The person who designed the mockup has spent a lot of time thinking about that screen, and they likely already have multiple alternative ideas. They might not even think the element in question is that important, and maybe you can omit it altogether.

What you don't want to do is assume that everything on the design is written in stone. You might spend a week implementing a multi-line ellipsis only to learn that the work was totally unnecessary!

In the above hypothetical scenario, I took the liberty of taking half an hour to implement the easier parts of the designer's feedback. I didn't check with my manager, or the team lead, I just did it.

Depending on where you work, you may or may not have this kind of autonomy. At some organizations, it may be considered unusual for developers to take on work that wasn't explicitly assigned by a PM.

If you work in an environment like this, designers probably don't have any expectation that you'll address these concerns anyway, so it's a bit of a different situation. Good communication is the most important part; you can still have that discussion around workarounds and optimal solutions, even if you're not authorized to implement the fixes yet.

I also have a little trick I really like; if I finish a task early and have some extra time to spare, I'll DM a team member and ask if they have any lingering tickets they'd like me to look at. It could be a designer, or a member of the support team, or someone in sales.

Often, folks in these roles will have a couple of tickets that are very important to them, and yet those tickets never seem to find their way into the active sprint. Typically, the reply I get will look like:

OMG YES I've been trying to get this ticket solved for 3 months, it's affecting a lot of users and I don't understand why it isn't being taken more seriously. Thank you thank you thank you.

In these cases, I like to pretend that I'm on an undercover mission - I'll do things "off the record" when it comes to Jira tickets, and I won't broadcast that I'm working on it (though of course I'll still be honest if anyone asks, and I'll probably bring it up in the next standup, after the fact).

It's worth pointing out that as a cis white guy, I am privileged enough that I can get away with stuff like this without worrying about any repercussions. Unfortunately, your experience might vary, especially if you're an underrepresented minority in tech. A safer bet might be to reach out to your PM and ask for permission first, instead of forgiveness later.

Every few months, this question is asked in design communities, and everyone jumps in with hot takes. I am not a designer, and am woefully out of my depth when it comes to answering this question, but I do have one strong opinion: if designers want to code, we should do whatever we can to enable them.

I've worked on teams where designers knew CSS, and wanted to be able to make small tweaks, stuff like adjusting font sizes and positions. Sometimes, developers are hesitant—there's a concern that it will eat up a bunch of their time. Especially if the codebase is complex and has a lot of quirks (which is every production codebase), the concern goes like this: “It would take me longer to teach you how to fix it than it would for me to just fix it myself”.

The problem is that we often say this while ignoring a pile of tickets that designers have opened. We can't have it both ways; either we agree to promptly address any design feedback we receive, or we find a way to empower designers to make those changes themselves (if they want to). While it might take a bit more developer time upfront, it's an investment in the future.

It's a bit of a sad statement that designers are sometimes willing to learn an entirely different trade because they see it as the easiest way to make sure that their designs are correctly implemented 😬

Also, we shouldn't underestimate designers' ability to make sense of our code. Yes, coding is hard, but it's not as mystical as we like to believe.*On a flight, I once sat next to someone who figured that software developers were "like in The Matrix", working with 0s and 1s. She was very underwhelmed when I showed her what our job actually looked like.

The conventional way to build software, "waterfall", was a multi-step process: first, design did all the user research and sketching and prototyping and creating high fidelity mockups. Once they were confident their work was finished, they'd toss it over the wall to the development team, and wouldn't hear anything about it until it was just about ready to go live.

As I'm sure many people have experienced, this is not an ideal way to work. As developers, we have critical information needed for a successful design process. We know what data is and isn't available in our database. We understand the web platform and its limitations. In short, we know which ideas will be easy to implement, and which will be next-to-impossible.

#2710. Scientists discover a hidden messaging system deep inside the brain

Deep within the brainstem is a tiny group of nerve cells that plays an outsized role in how the brain functions. This region, called the locus coeruleus (LC), gets its name from the Latin words for "blue place." Although it contains only a small fraction of the brain's neurons, it produces norepinephrine (NE), a chemical messenger that helps regulate attention, learning, heart rate, and the body's reactions to stress and excitement.

Scientists have long assumed that the LC operates like a loudspeaker, sending essentially the same norepinephrine signal across the brain. However, a new study published in Nature challenges that understanding. Using brain imaging, recordings of neural activity, genetic techniques, and behavioral experiments in mice, researchers at the Allen Institute discovered that the LC communicates through several distinct pathways. Rather than broadcasting one general message, it delivers signals to specific destinations, with different groups of neurons responding to different experiences.

A Hidden Brain Network That Sends Precise Messages

By tracing the connections of LC neurons, researchers found that different groups of cells communicate with specific regions of the nervous system. These pathways determine where signals trigger the release of norepinephrine, creating a much more organized communication network than previously recognized.

Neurons in the upper (dorsal) portion of the LC primarily connect to the cerebral cortex and forebrain. Meanwhile, cells in the lower (ventral) portion send their signals mainly to the brainstem and spinal cord. This arrangement allows different parts of the LC to influence separate brain functions.

"The findings suggest the brain's norepinephrine system is far more like a targeted postal network than a foghorn," said Karel Svoboda, director of Neural Dynamics at the Allen Institute and study co-author.

Why the Discovery Could Matter for Brain Disorders

Understanding how this signaling network is organized could eventually change how certain neurological and mental health conditions are treated.

Norepinephrine plays a central role in regulating attention and mood, making it an important target for medications used to treat depression, anxiety, and ADHD. Neurons in the LC are also among the earliest brain cells to deteriorate in Alzheimer's disease, suggesting that the new findings could have implications for understanding and treating these conditions.

Current treatments that influence norepinephrine can affect multiple brain circuits at once, sometimes producing beneficial effects in one region while causing unwanted effects elsewhere. A clearer map of the LC's specialized pathways could help scientists develop therapies that act more selectively on the circuits involved in particular symptoms.

The research received support from the National Institutes of Health's Brain Research Through Advancing Innovative Neurotechnologies® Initiative, also known as The BRAIN Initiative®.

Scientists Identify Brain Cells Involved in Learning

The researchers also uncovered an important distinction between the functions of different LC neurons.

Cells in the dorsal LC that connect to the cortex were particularly active during learning. When mice performed decision-making tasks and adjusted their choices after an unfavorable result, these neurons increased their activity.

Neurons in the ventral LC behaved differently. Their activity rose shortly before mice ignored signals indicating that a reward might be available. This suggests that these cells help regulate whether animals respond to opportunities in their surroundings.

"What emerged was a clear map: neurons in the dorsal LC that send signals upward to the cortex are involved in learning, whereas neurons in the ventral region projecting downward to the brainstem and spinal cord govern whether animals engage with their environment at all," said Svoboda. "We also showed that these anatomical differences are mirrored by distinct gene expression patterns."

These findings indicate that the differences between the two groups extend beyond their locations and connections. They also have distinct patterns of gene activity that reflect their specialized roles.

Researchers Discover a Record-Breaking Neuron

The study involved an extensive examination of the mouse brain. Scientists used whole-brain imaging to study nearly 35,000 neurons, analyzed the genetic profiles of almost 400,000 cells, and reconstructed the complete structures of several exceptionally complex nerve cells.

During this work, they encountered another remarkable finding: LC neurons possess extraordinarily long axons. These projections carry signals from nerve cells to other cells, allowing information to travel across the nervous system.

The researchers found that LC axons measured approximately 35 centimeters long on average. One particularly extraordinary axon had a total reconstructed length of 70.32 centimeters, making it part of the longest single neuron ever measured in a mouse.

"This neuron, like many others that we studied, supplies NE to a very large volume of the cerebral cortex. For the brain, this is highly unusual. Most neurons are more specific in their targets," said Jeremiah Cohen, scientist at the Allen Institute and study co-author. "But this neuron doesn't release NE everywhere. It ignores the cerebellum, brainstem, and spinal cord."

Despite its remarkable length and extensive reach, the neuron does not distribute norepinephrine throughout the entire brain. Instead, its connections remain selective, reinforcing the discovery that the LC operates through carefully organized signaling pathways.

A New Understanding of How the Brain Learns

The researchers also identified intriguing similarities between the norepinephrine system and the brain's dopamine signaling system.

Dopamine is another major chemical messenger that sends signals to the basal ganglia, a brain region involved in forming habits. Scientists suspect that dopamine and norepinephrine work together to support learning, potentially allowing the brain to acquire several complex and abstract concepts at the same time.

By revealing the intricate connections, specialized functions, and unusual physical structure of neurons in the locus coeruleus, the research provides a more detailed picture of how the animal brain communicates and learns.

Although the findings come from experiments in mice, they could eventually help scientists better understand human brain function and design treatments that target specific neural circuits rather than affecting entire signaling systems.

#2715. Do fish feel thirsty?

Fish live in water, but do they feel thirsty, and how do they drink water safely if they live in the sea?

Of course, we don’t know what a fish actually feels, but they do need water to stay alive.

Fish take on water either by drinking or absorbing water through the skin and gills by osmosis.

Fish regulate the amount of water in their body by a process called osmoregulation. They use their kidneys and gills to manage salt and water levels.

To understand how fish stay hydrated, you need to understand a concept called osmosis. Osmosis is the movement of water from an area of high concentration ( of water ) to an area of low concentration. This means the needs of fish living in salt water and fish living in freshwater are very different.

Fish living in freshwater don’t actively drink. Their blood and tissues have a higher salt concentration than the freshwater they live in, so they need to prevent themselves from taking on too much water.

The kidneys of a freshwater fish constantly remove water from the blood to produce dilute urine, which is almost constantly expelled, while the gills have specialised salt cells that pump salt back into the fish’s body.

Saltwater fish have a different challenge. The ocean is saltier than their insides, so they need to keep too much salt out of their body. Unlike freshwater fish, saltwater fish drink constantly. to cope with all the salt, their gills contain cells that pump excess salt back into the sea, and their kidneys produce only small amounts of concentrated urine, so they conserve as much freshwater as possible.

To help explain omoregulation, you can use the classic gummy bear osmosis experiment.

The gummy bear represents a fish. The outside represents the semi-permeable skin of a fish, and the inside represents the inside of the fish.

Several gummy bears or other gummy sweets.

Half fill both containers with plain water.

Add salt to the second container until it’s hard to dissolve any more.

Place a gummy bear in the plain water container for a few hours. It should increase in size.

The concentration of water in the container is higher than the concentration of water inside the gummy bear, so water moves from the container into the bear by osmosis.

This is why fish living in freshwater don’t drink and have to excrete urine almost constantly. If they didn’t, they would swell up and maybe even burst!

Carefully place the swollen gummy bear in the salty water and leave for a few hours. This time, you should find the gummy bear shrinks.

If you made the water salty enough, the concentration of water inside the bear should be higher than that of the salty water, and so water moves from the bear into the water, making the bear shrink.

Saltwater fish have to drink constantly, as water is continually moving out of their body into the salty ocean.

#2719. This snake cartwheels to escape danger

Published Oct 19, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

Snakes will bite, rattle their tails, and even spit venom to escape danger. But of all the world’s species, southeast Asia’s dwarf reed snake (Pseudorabdion longiceps) relies on an especially fascinating escape method. When threatened, the diminutive serpent rolls away like a speeding wheel.

An adult dwarf reed snake typically measures less than 12 inches long, but its unique locomotive maneuvers allow it to retreat as fast one foot per second—about four times faster than it travels by simply slithering on the ground. It’s not a flailing getaway, either. The snake also easily rolls along a straight path while somehow keeping its head both stable and facing forward.

Now, researchers finally have a better understanding of how the dwarf reed snake achieves such dizzying acrobatics. In a study published today in the journal Current Biology, a team at Japan’s Toho University detailed the specific physiology that enables its locomotion after filming the snakes with high-speed cameras. Essentially, the snake doesn’t rotate its entire body in a singular, forward motion—it whips one side of its body forward towards the ground while an arched portion swings down by its head in what the study’s authors call a “stroke.” The snake then alternates between its left and right side coming into contact with the ground for each successive stroke. Essentially, it’s like twirling a rope in a lateral circle while holding one end in your hand—except that end is the snake’s head.

The cumulative efforts bypass the traditional idea of a wheel requiring something like a central axle. Instead, this ingenious, highly controlled movement allows the animal to keep its head and sensory organs upright and facing ahead while traveling at such high speed. This is compounded by the dwarf reed snake directing regions of its body to engage in large, alternating rotations while stabilizing its head.

By gaining better insight into these biological mechanisms, researchers also highlight the difficulty in trying to predict an animal’s capabilities based solely on its physiology. A snake may have primarily evolved to slither—but that doesn’t mean it can’t get creative when necessary.

#2720. A tiny defect in a crystal reveals a big quantum surprise

When light strikes a tiny imperfection inside a material such as diamond or silicon carbide, it can transfer energy to electrons and push them into an excited state. These imperfections, known as spin defects, could become valuable building blocks for future quantum technologies, including quantum sensors. Because they can function as stable qubits whose states can be read, scientists are eager to understand exactly how they behave. However, predicting how their energy changes over time has remained a major challenge.

Researchers at the University of Chicago Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering (UChicago PME) have now developed a computational method that provides a more complete picture of what happens when these defects interact with light. Their approach can simulate the entire optical cycle, including a particularly difficult process known as internal conversion.

The findings, published in Physical Review Letters, could help scientists design better materials for quantum technologies. The method may also prove useful for studying materials involved in renewable energy conversion and storage.

"This internal conversion process is not only relevant to spin defects -- it's also related to processes of interest to energy technologies," said Giulia Galli, the Liew Family Professor of Electronic Structure and Simulations in UChicago PME and the Department of Chemistry and the senior author of the study. "That's one of the reasons this work is so exciting."

When a laser excites a spin defect, the defect can return to its original, lower-energy state through several different processes. It might release a photon of light, transition between states with different spins, or transfer its energy into vibrations of the surrounding atoms.

That final pathway, called internal conversion, has been especially difficult for scientists to model accurately. Although the fundamental equations of quantum mechanics can describe the other two processes, calculating how energy is lost through atomic vibrations presents substantial computational obstacles.

"Although the equations are known, it's hard to describe the process accurately because of computational difficulties," said Stefano Villani, a UChicago PME postdoctoral researcher and the study's first author. "You need to account for complex interactions between the electrons and surrounding atoms, and that's challenging in realistic solids represented by models with hundreds or thousands of atoms"

To overcome this problem, Galli, Villani, and Yu Jin combined several computational techniques that their research group had developed over many years. Jin, a former graduate student in Galli's group, is now a postdoctoral scholar at the Flatiron Institute in New York.

The researchers used mathematical descriptions known as many-body wavefunctions to represent the excited states of spin defects. Through exact mathematical derivations, they determined how strongly each excited state interacts with the movements of nearby atoms.

This allowed them to calculate how individual electronic states connect with all the possible vibrational configurations of hundreds of surrounding atoms, rather than having to analyze those configurations individually.

"In the past, a clear mathematical derivation and efficient algorithms were not available," explained Yu Jin. "The new derivation came together after years of work on the optical cycle of spin defects."

The researchers incorporated their new framework into WEST, an open-source simulation program optimized for graphics processing units (GPUs). Galli's group has spent the past decade developing WEST through the Midwest Integrated Center for Computational Materials (MICCoM), which has received funding from the U.S. Department of Energy's Office of Basic Energy Sciences since 2015.

A Surprising Discovery Inside Silicon Carbide

To test their approach, the researchers first examined a nitrogen-vacancy center in diamond, a type of crystal defect widely studied for quantum applications.

Their calculations closely matched recent spectroscopy measurements. For triplet states, internal conversion contributed relatively little to energy loss. In singlet states, however, it was the primary mechanism responsible for releasing energy. Both findings agreed with what scientists had previously expected.

The results changed when the team investigated a divacancy defect in silicon carbide.

Their calculations revealed that internal conversion has a much greater influence on the lifetime of the defect's excited triplet state than earlier models had accounted for. This previously underestimated contribution helped explain a long-standing disagreement between theoretical predictions and experimental observations.

"We were expecting to see an effect, but of a smaller magnitude," Galli said. "What we found is that this internal conversion process turns out to be more important than we thought."

Understanding this unexpected energy-loss pathway could help scientists more accurately predict the behavior of silicon carbide defects and improve their potential use as qubits in future quantum devices.

A New Tool for Quantum Computing and Energy Research

With the framework now available through WEST, researchers at other institutions can begin applying it to their own studies. However, Galli noted that the simulations still demand considerable computing power.

The team is exploring ways to reduce those requirements, including the use of artificial intelligence.

"We are working on ways to integrate machine learning into excited states calculations to further improve the efficiency of our method," Villani said.

Meanwhile, the scientists are expanding their approach to investigate spin defects in silicon, as well as materials used in energy technologies where internal conversion influences how energy is transferred or lost.

By addressing one of the most difficult parts of modeling how spin defects respond to light, the researchers have made it possible to predict their behavior more completely and accurately.

"We provided the missing piece of the puzzle needed to completely describe the optical cycle of spin defects," Galli said. "Our work can now be used to make more accurate predictions about many materials of interest for quantum technologies," added Yu Jin.

#2724. Ocean Acidification Investigation

The ocean covers about 71% of the Earth’s surface and holds about 97% of our planet’s total water. Despite the vastness of our oceans, they are being massively impacted by human activity. Microplastics have been found at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, fish populations are declining due to overfishing, and the oceans are warming and rising.

Burning coal for energy, driving cars, and other activities that use fossil fuels release carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. The oceans absorb about a quarter of the carbon dioxide released by human activity, which might sound like a good thing, but even though the oceans are vast, the huge amount of carbon dioxide they’ve absorbed has caused their pH to drop, a process called acidification.

Our oceans have become about 30% more acidic over the last 200 years, a change faster than anything known over the last 50 million years!

Increasing ocean acidity is expected to have huge impacts on marine life. As with any change, some organisms will survive and maybe even thrive, some struggle to adapt, and some will not survive. The effects are wide ranging, and it’s not just lost biodiversity, but a potential threat to food supplies for people.

As seawater gets more acidic, several things happen.

You can use an egg and vinegar to demonstrate visually how sea creatures can lose their protective shells in increasingly acidic waters.

One simple way to demonstrate the effect of ocean acidification on the shells of marine life is to place an egg in vinegar and watch as the vinegar dissolves the shell.

Eggshells are made from calcium carbonate ( CaCO3 ), which is the same substance corals, clams, oysters, and other marine animals use to build their skeletons and shells.

Acidic ocean water contains carbonic acid, but we’re going to use another weak acid, which you probably have in your kitchen, acetic acid ( vinegar ).

When you place an egg in vinegar, the acid breaks down the calcium carbonate, releasing carbon dioxide gas ( you’ll see little bubbles being released around the shell ). The same corrosion happens to the shells of marine organisms, but much more slowly. Vinegar has a pH of around 3, and the ocean around 8.1. The acidic ocean weakens shells over time, making them thinner and more fragile.

You might be thinking that if the ocean has a pH of 8.1, it’s not acidic, but even though pH 7 is neutral and anything above that is basic, the ocean is more acidic than it was in previous years. Acidification doesn’t mean the ocean is turning into acid, but rather that it is becoming more acidic, and even a very small change in pH can substantially affect marine life.

Vinegar – White vinegar is good, as you can see the egg better, but malt vinegar also works.

Carefully place the egg in the container.

Pour vinegar into the container until the egg is completely covered.

Leave for about 24 hours, then remove the egg and rinse it carefully under running water. You should be able to rub away some of the shell.

To remove the shell completely, place the egg back in the vinegar for another 24 hours.

Once you’ve removed all the eggshell, you should be able to gently bounce the shell-less egg.

Vinegar contains acetic acid, which reacts with the calcium carbonate in the egg shell to form calcium ions, water and carbon dioxide.

The dissolving of the eggshell shows how much impact ocean acidification can have on marine life.

In my experience, it's a bit of a yellow flag if remote workers aren't spread across the org chart, including management and senior leadership.

If the company is not fully distributed, it's important to figure out whether remote work is treated as a first-class concern, or whether it's an afterthought. When designing company policies, are remote workers top-of-mind? Is remote work a core part of the culture?

A common misconception around remote work is that it's a solo activity. In my experience, it involves a ton of collaboration! This felt like a fair policy to me.

If the company hires globally, this gets a lot more challenging; there aren't any hours of overlap in the workday between folks in California and folks in Berlin, for example. It's all well and good to put more emphasis on asynchronous communication, but it's harder to work effectively without ever being able to speak to someone over a video call.

#2728. Glioblastoma breakthrough reveals how brain cancer cells become more aggressive

Glioblastoma is among the most lethal forms of brain cancer, and scientists have uncovered a surprising factor that may help its cells become more invasive. The fluid surrounding the outer edges of these tumors is much thicker than the fluid near their centers, forcing cancer cells to encounter increasing resistance as they move into healthy brain tissue. Rather than simply slowing their movement, however, this environment appears to help the cells adapt in ways that make invasion easier.

To investigate this unexpected behavior, researchers created an innovative microfluidic chip that recreates the physical conditions encountered by glioblastoma cells. They found that spending extended periods in thick fluid caused the cells to become smaller, more flexible, and better able to squeeze through narrow openings. The discovery suggests that fluid viscosity does more than obstruct cancer cells. It can actively influence their behavior and potentially make them more aggressive.

How Thick Fluid Influences Brain Cancer Invasion

Cancer research has traditionally concentrated on chemical signals and the stiffness of the tissues surrounding tumors. Far less attention has been given to the viscosity of the fluid through which cancer cells travel.

In glioblastoma, this difference is particularly striking. Fluid at the tumor's advancing edge is approximately eight times more viscous than fluid in its dead tissue at the center. As cancer cells move outward, they must push through an increasingly resistant environment.

Studying this process has been difficult because conventional closed microfluidic devices cannot accurately reproduce these conditions. Their enclosed channels can limit access to oxygen and nutrients, while friction against the walls introduces additional forces that influence cell behavior. These limitations also make it challenging to observe how cancer cells respond to prolonged exposure to viscous fluid.

Researchers therefore needed a way to isolate the effects of fluid viscosity from other mechanical influences and determine whether extended exposure produces lasting changes in glioblastoma cells.

A New Device Reveals How Cancer Cells Adapt

Scientists from Chongqing General Hospital and Chongqing University in China developed a specialized open microfluidic platform to address these challenges. Their findings were published in Microsystems & Nanoengineering.

The device consists of a two-layer open microfluidic membrane equipped with a removable cap and an array of tiny pillars. This arrangement allows researchers to control precisely when cancer cells begin migrating while observing how their nuclei change shape as they move through confined spaces.

Unlike traditional enclosed systems, the platform also supports continuous cell culture for up to one month. This makes it possible to examine how prolonged mechanical stress changes the movement and invasive behavior of glioblastoma cells.

The researchers tested two human glioblastoma cell lines, U-251 and LN-229. Both were grown for a month in a thick fluid designed to reproduce conditions at the tumor's invasive outer edge (7.1 cP).

When the cells were subsequently examined using the chip, those that had adapted to the viscous environment traveled faster and covered greater distances than untreated control cells. This result was especially surprising because thicker fluid would ordinarily be expected to impede movement.

Brain Cancer Cells Become Smaller and More Flexible

Microscopic observations revealed how the adapted cells overcame this resistance. After prolonged exposure to the viscous fluid, they had become smaller and easier to deform, allowing them to maneuver through the narrow gaps between the device's microscopic pillars.

As the cells squeezed through these restricted passages, their nuclei underwent visible deformation. Researchers also observed an accumulation of YAP, a protein involved in sensing mechanical forces, inside the nuclei. This is a recognized sign that cells have activated pathways involved in responding to physical stress.

Although both glioblastoma cell lines developed similar changes in shape and movement, their underlying molecular responses were unexpectedly different.

U-251 cells underwent a form of mesenchymal-like reprogramming, activating genes associated with cancer invasion, including CD44, FN1, and MMP9. LN-229 cells, by contrast, became more flexible and mobile without showing substantial lasting changes in gene expression.

Further analysis using Western blotting showed that protein changes remained detectable even after cells were transferred back into fluid with normal viscosity. This indicated that at least some of the adaptations were persistent rather than simply short-lived responses to their surroundings.

Scientists Discover a Strange Kind of Cellular Memory

One of the most unexpected findings was that exposure to thick fluid appeared to leave a lasting imprint on glioblastoma cells.

The researchers had anticipated that viscosity would primarily function as a physical obstacle. Instead, their experiments showed that prolonged exposure could alter how cells behaved even after the original mechanical conditions were removed.

This phenomenon points to a form of mechanical memory, in which cells retain changes produced by earlier physical stress.

The open design of the new chip was essential for identifying this effect. By separating resistance from the fluid itself from the confinement imposed by channel walls, the researchers could investigate viscosity without the additional mechanical interference that complicates conventional experiments.

Observing changes in nuclear shape and migration patterns after weeks of exposure provided evidence that glioblastoma cells can adapt to their mechanical environment in lasting ways.

The contrasting molecular responses of the two cell lines were particularly revealing. Although both appeared to undergo similar physical transformations, only one showed extensive changes in its gene activity. This suggests that glioblastoma cells may acquire comparable invasive characteristics through different underlying biological processes.

A Potential New Approach to Studying Brain Cancer Treatments

Beyond uncovering an overlooked influence on glioblastoma invasion, the researchers believe their device could provide a valuable tool for investigating potential cancer therapies.

The open microfluidic platform fits into standard multi-well plates, allowing it to be incorporated into existing laboratory procedures for cell culture and imaging. Its accessible design also enables researchers to stain cells directly and observe them over long periods without the clogging problems that can occur in closed systems.

These features could make it useful for testing drugs that interfere with the cellular pathways responsible for detecting and responding to mechanical forces.

For glioblastoma, the results raise the possibility that highly viscous environments may favor cancer cells that are better adapted to invasion. Treatments targeting YAP signaling or the remodeling of the cytoskeleton, the internal framework that helps cells maintain and change their shape, could therefore be investigated under conditions that more closely resemble those inside tumors.

The technology may also prove useful for studying other cancers in which fluid viscosity varies across different regions. By examining how tumor cells respond to these mechanical conditions, researchers could gain a better understanding of why some cancers are particularly invasive and potentially identify patients whose tumors depend on mechanical adaptation to spread.

Although further research is needed to determine how these laboratory findings translate to tumors in patients, the discovery highlights an important and previously underexplored aspect of cancer biology: the physical environment surrounding tumor cells may actively shape their ability to invade healthy tissue.

#2735. My experience as a remote worker

I had an interesting realization recently: I was three years into my career when I got my first remote job, and it's been about three years since then. I've now spent half my career working in a traditional in-office software development job, and the other half working remotely!

Increasingly, remote work is becoming a common, mainstream thing. Social media pundits have been quick to form and share opinions, and I've seen a lot of misconceptions and misinformation about what "remote work" is, and how it works.

This is part 1 of a two-part blog post series. Today, I'll share what my experience has been like—it'll be an honest look at the ups and downs of remote life. Hopefully, this context will be helpful if you've been considering remote work for yourself!

Let's start by getting this out of the way—most people talking about remote work are describing something very different from my experience.

"Remote work sounds great because you can work from home in your pyjamas, but it's not all it's cracked up to be, because soon you'll discover how isolating it is to spend all day alone"

This discussion is predicated on a false idea, that "remote work" is synonymous with "working from home", and that it means you work alone.

I think it would surprise people just how much my "remote work" lifestyle looks like their "normal office" lifestyle:

This schedule is not universal to remote workers, and that's kind of the point—the idea with remote work is that it's flexible. It allows you to construct whatever working situation works for you. Plenty of remote workers do work from home (though none that I've met have been the unshaven recluse so often depicted in remote-work discussions), but they do it because that's the situation that works best for them.

I realized pretty quickly that working from home wasn't going to work for me.

When I started working remotely for Khan Academy, I rented an office at WeWork. It ticked a bunch of boxes, but ultimately it wasn't the right fit. It was rowdy at times. It was also kinda dreary—they keep their lights dim, so there's a perpetual 7pm vibe. Despite having all-glass walls, my office wasn't anywhere near natural light. The office I was renting was small enough that if I stood in the center, I could just about touch all 4 walls without moving.

About a year ago, I switched to a new office.

I'm subletting it from a local Montreal company, located in Old Montreal. It's not really a coworking space; it feels less like a trendy startup incubator and more like a traditional small business. It grants me the perfect level of in-person social interaction; there are always people around I can talk about hockey or the weather with.

My office is big and bright, featuring tall ceilings and a large window. It overlooks a public square, and I've set up a digital piano in front of it, so I can take frequent breaks and watch tourists meander while I noodle on the keyboard. It's pretty great.

I am very aware that this office is a privilege that many cannot afford—it costs $700 CAD / $525 USD a month. In many cases, though, employers are willing to contribute towards these costs, either in part or in full. And this additional expense is often smaller than the bump in salary folks receive when transitioning to remote work.

For the most part, I've been surprised to see how similar remote work is from in-office work. There are some things that are different, though. Let's look at them in depth.

I live in Montréal, Québec. According to Payscale(opens in new tab), the average Montreal software developer makes about $65,000 CAD, about $49,000 USD. This is dramatically less than in the Bay Area, where Payscale(opens in new tab) estimates the average salary at around $118,000 USD. That's a 2.4x multiple!

Remote-friendly companies don't always pay Bay Area salaries, but in my experience, they tend to pay quite a bit better than local ones. For example, Buffer pays based on San Francisco rates, and deducts up to 25% for cost-of-living(opens in new tab). This feels pretty harsh to me, and yet it still comes out to a much more generous salary than is common in Montreal.

If you already live in a high-pay, high-cost-of-living area like San Francisco or New York City, working remotely can grant you the freedom to move wherever you'd like. While not everyone can uproot their family and move, it's still better to have the option than not.

Recently, I’ve seen several companies moving away from cost-of-living multipliers. Personally, I believe that employees should be paid based on the value they provide, not where they happen to live. The truth is that all kinds of things affect how much of our income is discretionary, such as having kids or being in debt, and employers have never adjusted salaries for those things. If you're providing Bay Area value, you deserve Bay Area dollars!

One of the myths of remote work is that it's "lone wolf" territory, meant for contractors who need little guidance and have little oversight. The truth is that all of my years as a remote worker have been fairly collaborative, working within tight-knit teams to develop and ship features.

As a remote worker, I have roughly the same number of meetings as I did when I worked in-office.

Most remote-friendly companies will have a policy that every meeting room is equipped with video-conferencing software, and every meeting has a Google Hangout or Zoom session created for it. This way, remote folks can join any meeting added to the calendar without meeting organizers having to do anything special.

It can be hard to fully participate, though. It's human nature to interact primarily with the people in the same space as you. As a remote employee, your disembodied, televised head is often floating out of eyeline, off in the background. As conversation picks up, it can be hard to get a word in edge-wise. If you wait for the perfect opportunity, you might never have a chance to speak. And if you decide to interject haphazardly, you'll be the booming voice of God, a gigantic face stretched across a 72" screen, causing heads to swivel around like a mob of startled meerkats. I don't like drawing that much attention to myself.

If you work for a fully-distributed company, this isn't a problem, since everyone's on their own laptop. And many companies are moving to a model where all meeting participants join on their own computer, even those in the same room together. This ensures a level playing field, and makes it much easier for remote participants to participate.

A concern many folks have about remote work is that inevitably, no matter how much Slack is stressed, two employees will bump into each other in the office and share information or make decisions. Folks in other timezones and countries will miss out on that information, and sometimes this can make it harder to be effective at your job.

I can definitely think of times where, say, I spent a day working on something only to learn that the feature isn't needed anymore... but the thing is, that's happened to me both as a remote worker and as an in-office worker. Communication challenges are not unique to remote work. And the solutions are pretty much the same:

People assume that it's harder to stay in touch with people when you don't work out of the same space, but I'd argue it's the opposite. Tools like Slack let you instantly fire off a message, without worrying if you're interrupting someone deep in concentration. When I worked in-office, I was always hesitant to walk over to someone hard at work. Slack gives me license to talk to people more often, not less.

Of the articles and social media threads I've read from people who have actually tried remote work, this is the most common issue. For folks who are used to working in an office, it can be hard to transition to a model where you only speak with your co-workers over screens, where there are no Thursday-evening game nights, mini birthday celebrations, or group lunches. In an office, it's easy to spend twenty minutes by the watercooler, just hanging out with your peers. Those serendipitous interactions don't occur remotely, every communication is intentional.

In my case, I've solved for this by renting an office. The people I share this space with technically aren't coworkers—we don't share an employer—but that doesn't stop us from gossiping about the Habs(opens in new tab), currently on their worst losing streak since 1940(opens in new tab), or sharing vacation pictures with each other.

It's also self-evident to me that it's possible to form close personal relationships with remote coworkers—I'm still friends with many folks I worked with at my first remote job! It may not be ideal, but it works. If hit TLC television show 90 Day Fiancée(opens in new tab) teaches us anything, it's that humans are remarkably good at overcoming the artificial nature of long-distance communication.

Another important consideration: depending on where you live, and the size of the organization, you may not be alone in your city! I live in Montreal, a city not really known for being a tech hub, but when I worked at Khan Academy, I had two coworkers that lived here. Once every month or so we'd grab coffee and co-work together, either at a co-working space or at a coffee shop. Many orgs will rent out a small office for cities that reach a certain threshold, blurring the line between "remote work" and "flexible in-office work".

Many folks I've spoken to have expressed concern around mentorship; for folks early in their careers, are they at a disadvantage by not being in the same room as the senior devs?

I remember when I was about a year into my career, I was working at a local startup and I had gotten myself into a big mess with Git. I was worried I'd accidentally erase all the progress I'd made on the task that day. The senior dev sat right near me—I could walk over and tap him on the shoulder, but what if he was in the middle of something? I imagined the frustration he might feel, watching his mental house-of-cards collapse as his focus evaporated, all because some sheepish junior dev doesn't understand source control.

Now that I'm a senior developer myself, I recognize how silly this thought was—I'm always happy to help when someone needs it! But that fact is irrelevant; junior devs often worry about stuff like that, and that worry can lead to bad outcomes, like not asking for help when it's needed.

There's something to be said for Slack's asynchronous model. It's so much easier to send someone a message, knowing they probably won't see it right away if they're deep in concentration.

What about the actual getting-help itself? Surely it's better for two devs to sit side-by-side when explaining a problem? I'm not so sure. Tools like Screen Hero (RIP) and Slack's video conferencing let two people share a screen, each with their own connected mouse and keyboard. Pair programming works really well when the "passenger" can use their cursor to indicate a specific line, or draw on the screen using a pencil tool. I expect the tooling will only improve as remote work becomes more common!

Also, I feel like text chat is underrated for stuff like this. It lets the explainer choose their words carefully, and make sure the explanation makes sense. And it provides a permanent transcript that the person being helped can reference in the future, if the explanation doesn't stick.

One more anecdote: every summer at Khan Academy, we'd hire a class of college interns. Every intern would be assigned a mentor, a point person to provide technical guidance, assign and review work, and generally make sure that the intern was having a happy, healthy, educational summer.

Mentors could either be remote or in-office, and as far as we were able to tell, it made no difference; interns were as likely to succeed regardless of whether their mentor was sitting next to then in Mountain View, or located in a different state or country.

I was an intern mentor myself, and it was incredibly fulfilling! I don't believe being remote was a hindrance at all.

Despite all of this, I still recommend proceeding cautiously as a junior developer seeking remote work. I've had the privilege of working for stand-out organizations that have put a lot of resources into making the remote culture succeed. If certain conventions aren't in place, it can be much harder to receive mentorship as a remote employee. I'll talk more about this in the next post in this series.

I've had the privilege of working remotely for several different organizations now, and I've seen how different structures can lead to different cultures.

At one role in particular, about 50% of the engineering department was remote, while 100% of the executive team (and most managers) were in-office. A good chunk of the executive team was new to the org, and they had come from organizations that were not remote-friendly. This meant that in-office "individual contributors" (ICs) were able to form relationships with senior leadership in a way that remote workers weren't.

I remember during a particularly contentious period, hearing from an in-office friend that a member of the senior leadership team had come up to them, bewildered to learn that remote workers were unhappy with recent policy shifts. This member of the exec team went to an in-office IC for clarification about how remote folks were feeling, because that's who they knew.

This problematic dynamic was highlighted during performance reviews. As in many organizations, promotions were determined by senior leadership, and the process was not super transparent. When a remote worker is denied a promotion, it's hard not to wonder if personal relationships played a part; after all, wouldn't anyone have a subconscious bias towards the folks they have lunch with every day vs. someone they barely know?

These kinds of "office politics" are not unique to remote workers—at any organization, there's the risk of executives biasing promotions towards folks they like. Even if everyone works in the same space, there's no guarantee that relationships will form equally! And I suspect it's human nature to reach for external factors when trying to understand why we weren't selected for a promotion.

Despite these caveats, career progression can definitely be harder for remote workers if the company isn't fully distributed. In the next part of this blog post series, we'll dig into this a bit more as we learn more about how to find a great remote job.

The goal of this blog post was to share my experiences as a remote worker, with the hope that it provides a bit of clarity around whether or not remote work could be a good fit for you. And it's a hard question to answer, since "remote work" can mean so many different things. Very few of the experiences I've had—both good and bad—are universally true for remote work.

Ultimately, looking back, there have definitely been some bumps on my remote journey, but the overall experience has been very positive. I don't expect I'll go back to traditional in-office employment any time soon.

I do think I'm a particularly well-suited person for remote work, though. When I worked in-office, I was on a perpetual mission to find a way to eat lunch alone, without offending anyone or arousing concern. I had wonderful co-workers, but my idea of a perfect lunch is sitting alone in a park, listening to a podcast. In that sense, remote work is a perfect fit for me.

While I do think the concern about isolation as a remote worker is overblown—after all, I'm around people constantly—it is true that it can be harder to form close personal relationships with folks you're working around, rather than working with. Making friends as an adult is tough! If your current social network is comprised primarily of people you work with, and you thrive on that sort of camaraderie, remote work might be more of an uphill climb for you.

Aside from this, I believe the other concerns folks have about remote work can be mitigated, either by customizing your work environment, or finding a company with the right kinds of remote policies for you.

If remote work sounds good to you, my next blog post in this series is all about how to find your first remote job. It covers everything I’ve learned as someone who has been involved in hiring for several remote-friendly orgs, and how to evaluate the “remote-friendliness” of companies during the interview process.

#2736. How Pollution Climbs the Food Chain

A food chain is a diagram that shows how energy is transferred from one living thing to another. Energy is transferred from the sun to plants, which use the light to produce energy through a process called photosynthesis. An animal then eats the plant for food, is eaten by another animal, and so on until you reach the top of the food chain.

Plants are called producers because they make their own food and are the starting point of the food chain. Animals are called consumers as they eat plants and other animals and are further up the food chain. If the animal at the top of the food chain has no natural predators, it is called an apex predator.

If one part of a food chain is reduced or altered, the entire food chain is affected. For example, if there were a drought and fewer plants available to eat, the animals that depended on them would survive less well, reducing the food supply for animals further up the food chain.

Food chains are broken down into trophic levels.

Producers are usually plants, algae or phytoplankton. They use sunlight to create food by photosynthesis.

These are usually herbivores that eat producers.

These eat the primary consumers. They are carnivores or omnivores.

These are bigger animals that eat the secondary consumers.

#2737. GTA cities are missing climate targets. Could the election change things?

Rajpreet Sahota has been reporting on provincial environmental policy in Ontario with a focus on how it impacts racialized communities. She is based in Toronto.

In 2019, municipalities across Ontario declared a climate emergency. The backdrop was a groundswell of attention to global warming, including tens of thousands of people filling the streets around Ontario’s legislature in September, demanding urgent action on climate change.

The demonstration was part of the Global Week for Future, inspired by Swedish climate activist Greta Thunberg’s Fridays for Future movement. Young people marched alongside families, seniors and environmental advocates. The crowd outside Queen’s Park in Toronto was so large that some demonstrators couldn’t hear the speakers addressing the rally.

Lyn Adamson, co-chair of the environmental advocacy group ClimateFast, spent months helping organize the demonstration. “It was something I’d never seen,” she said. “I’d never seen that kind of mobilization.”

Mississauga and Brampton had declared an emergency in June, while Toronto City Council joined the list the week after the march. In the country’s largest city, council called for accelerated action to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and asked city staff to examine how Toronto could reach net-zero emissions by 2040.

Seven years later, municipalities across the Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area have introduced new climate policies, invested in energy-efficient buildings and expanded programs aimed at reducing emissions. But despite that progress, emissions are not falling quickly enough to meet many of their targets.

With Ontario’s municipal election approaching on Oct. 26, environmental advocates say the next four years will be critical in determining whether cities can turn their commitments into measurable results. And the term will take municipalities to 2030, a deadline attached to several major greenhouse gas reduction targets.

In Toronto, decisions about building emissions standards and retrofits, and transportation investments remain outstanding. Mississauga’s next council is expected to consider an updated emissions reduction plan in early 2027 and Brampton faces questions about how much progress it is actually making on its climate commitments.

And progress across the province has been slowed by the Ontario government stepping in to prevent cities and towns from instituting ambitious strategies it said could impede development.

Here’s a look at some of the commitments and progress made by municipalities, as they welcome the next municipal council to take the helm.

Greenhouse gas emissions across the Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area represent 46 per cent of Ontario’s emissions. They reached approximately 54.7 million tonnes in 2024, a modest increase of 1.1 per cent from the previous year, according to the latest carbon emissions inventory from the Toronto Atmospheric Fund.

Buildings accounted for 45 per cent of regional emissions, largely from natural gas used for heating and electricity generation. Transportation made up another 37 per cent.

Overall emissions remain far from where they need to be to reach net-zero targets that were developed in line with the United Nations and global scientists to stave off some of the worst effects of climate change.

The Atmospheric Fund, an agency of the City of Toronto, estimates the region would need reductions of approximately 11 per cent every year to meet its collective 2030 climate targets.

Gabriella Kalapos, executive director of Climate Action Partnership, said municipalities have made progress since 2019, particularly in understanding where emissions come from and identifying opportunities to reduce them. “It’s nowhere near the level of action that’s actually needed to respond to this climate emergency,” she said.

For Adamson, Toronto’s climate commitments aren’t just about reducing greenhouse gas emissions. They’re about making everyday life easier for residents, whether that’s because cycling is safe, public transit is reliable or utility bills are lower because homes use less energy.

Under its TransformTO climate strategy, the city has set a target of reducing community-wide emissions by 65 per cent below 1990 levels by 2030, and ultimately reach net-zero emissions by 2040.

Toronto acknowledges it is not on track to meet that goal. In 2023, the city released its most recent inventory of greenhouse gas emissions, finding community-wide emissions were just 35 per cent below 1990 levels. Buildings accounted for 55 per cent of emissions, followed by transportation at 36 per cent.

In an email to The Narwhal, James Nowlan, executive director of City of Toronto’s environment, climate and forestry division, wrote that buildings and transportation remain among the most challenging sources of emissions to address because many of the necessary changes extend beyond the city’s direct control.

Take the city’s multi-year battle with the provincial government to maintain some existing bike lanes and build more on main arteries of the city. Right now, the province is winning, having dispatched crews on a $48-million job to rip lanes out of major streets in the middle of the election campaign.

Premier Doug Ford’s government has also clamped down on how municipalities can implement green building standards. It restricted municipalities from mandating any green building features that go beyond provincial requirements. This put the Toronto Green Standard, a framework that has shaped development in Canada’s largest city for more than 15 years, in the crosshairs.

The standard required developers to incorporate features — such as bicycle parking, electric-vehicle charging stations and green roofs — to reduce emissions and improve resilience to climate change. The province has argued that standardized development requirements can help speed up construction during a housing crisis.

Environmental advocates, however, say the changes undermine municipal authority, including the ability to address local climate priorities. Kalapos said municipal progress on energy-efficient development policies has been complicated by provincial intervention.

“If you don’t have a supportive framework for climate action at the provincial and federal level, municipalities are really hamstrung to be able to move forward on their targets,” she said. “We are not going to meet this climate change imperative off the property tax base.”

The city is still developing emissions standards that would see building owners gradually reduce emissions through upgrades and retrofits. But provincial legislation will prevent Toronto from making the changes mandatory.

“It’s all voluntary. There is no deadline for anything,” Adamson said, arguing that relying on voluntary measures makes it difficult to achieve the scale of change needed.

Still, cities are trying. In Toronto, a new TransformTO action plan outlines further measures to reduce emissions between now and 2030, including support for community-led initiatives.

The city budgeted more than $2 billion in 2026 for emissions-reduction initiatives, including ongoing support for a bike share program and household composting.

In neighbouring Mississauga, the city reports progress on 59 of the 89 actions outlined in its 2019 climate change action plan, a 10-year municipal roadmap aimed at reducing emissions by 40 per cent by 2030 and 80 per cent by 2050. The plan was adopted months after council declared a climate emergency in June 2019.

Only 13 of the actions are considered fully complete, standalone actions, such as developing a program that diverts dog waste from landfills and converts it to energy and a strategy on increasing urban agriculture. Another 46 items, like gradually replacing municipal vehicles with electric alternatives, are mostly complete, with work ongoing and possibly evolving.

Carrah Bullock, Mississauga’s supervisor of climate policy, said the original plan was designed to lay the groundwork for future climate action, but did not include ways to measure its direct impact on emissions. The city is now updating its climate plan to better track the reductions associated with individual initiatives.

Despite this progress, Mississauga acknowledges, like its neighbouring cities, that it is not on track to meet its 2030 emissions targets. In fact, preliminary 2025 data suggest that emissions may have been higher than 2024, due largely to issues that, once again, are in the provincial government’s hands.

Ontario’s power grid has become less clean since 2021, when it was 94 per cent emissions-free. That number is now below 90 per cent, due largely to nuclear plants being taken offline for refurbishment. Having cancelled hundreds of renewable energy projects upon taking office in 2018, the Ford government has relied mainly on natural gas to replace them. The Atmospheric Fund reported that electricity-related emissions increased 28 per cent across the Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area in 2024 because of greater reliance on natural gas-fired generation.

The city has made progress on emissions reductions within its own buildings and vehicle fleet. Dianne Zimmerman, Mississauga’s environment manager, pointed to the introduction of a green standard for city-owned buildings, including a new fire station built to a net-zero standard. The city has also electrified municipal vehicles and equipment.

But reducing emissions from privately owned homes and commercial buildings remains a significant challenge. Bullock said a proposed community building retrofit program has been progressing more slowly than the city would like.

The city’s updated emissions reduction plan is expected to go before council in early 2027, followed by a separate climate resilience strategy focused on coping with extreme heat, storms and other effects of a warming planet.

David Laing, president and co-founder of the Brampton Environmental Alliance, regularly uses a bicycle for transportation. When Brampton began introducing cycling infrastructure in the years after declaring a climate emergency in 2019, he noticed the difference.

The improvements, he said, “made my life an awful lot easier and safer and more comfortable.”

But now, the city is reversing course, removing some of those bike lanes to make way for cars — such as on Howden Boulevard in the city’s Westgate neighbourhood — leaving him frustrated with what he sees as inconsistent decisions about transportation and climate action.

According to The Atmospheric Fund, transportation produced approximately 1.81 million tonnes of greenhouse gas emissions in Brampton in 2024, accounting for nearly half the city’s total.

It’s one example of why the city’s progress on community-wide emissions has been in question.

In 2019, Brampton released its community energy and emissions reduction plan, aiming to cut community-wide emissions by 50 per cent below 2016 levels by 2041, and at least 80 per cent by 2050.

Since then, it has taken steps to electrify public transit, including a $4-billion partnership in 2025 with U.K.-based company Zenobē to transition to as many as 1,000 electric buses over the next decade. And the city has developed an updated environmental master plan, subsidized building retrofits and launched a program to help local businesses reduce their energy use.

But the results of those efforts haven’t been published. Laing said the city needs to make it easier for residents to understand which climate commitments have been completed, how much money has been spent and whether emissions are actually declining.

Without that information, there’s little to keep city administration — and incoming mayors and councillors — accountable.

#2738. Birds are more afraid of women than men. Scientists have no idea why

Birds living in cities appear to react differently to men and women, and scientists are struggling to explain why. An international research team discovered that urban birds, including great tits, house sparrows, and blackbirds, tend to fly away sooner when approached by women than by men. The surprising behavior raises new questions about how birds perceive the people around them.

The study took place in five European countries, where male and female participants (matched for height and clothing) walked directly toward birds in city parks and other green spaces.

On average, men were able to approach about one meter closer than women before the birds flew away. The researchers measured this response by recording how close a person could get before a bird took flight (termed flight initiation distance).

Published in the British Ecological Society journal People and Nature, the findings showed the same pattern in Czechia, France, Germany, Poland, and Spain. The difference also appeared across all 37 bird species examined, including easily startled magpies and pigeons that typically allow people to approach much more closely.

The researchers believe their findings indicate that city birds can distinguish between male and female humans. However, they have yet to determine which characteristics the birds recognize or why they appear more cautious around women.

Professor Daniel Blumstein of the University of California, Los Angeles, who helped conduct the research, said the results were convincing despite the lack of an explanation.

"I fully believe our results, that urban birds react differently based on the sex of the person approaching them, but I can't explain them right now. We used bleeding-edge comparative analysis techniques that showed our findings were consistent across cities and species, but we simply don't have a conclusive explanation yet."

Dr. Yanina Benedetti of the Czech University of Life Sciences Prague, another member of the research team, said the discovery challenged her expectations about how birds respond to human observers.

Scientists have proposed several possible explanations for the difference. Birds might be responding to chemical signals such as pheromones, variations in body shape, or differences in how men and women walk. For now, however, these ideas remain speculative, and additional experiments will be needed to identify the actual cause.

"This is maybe the most interesting part of our study," said Dr. Federico Morelli from the University of Turin, a co-author of the study. "We have identified a phenomenon, but we really don't know why. However, what our results do highlight is the birds' sophisticated ability to evaluate their environment."

The findings suggest that urban birds may be sensitive to human characteristics that people themselves rarely notice. Identifying those signals could help researchers better understand how wildlife assesses potential threats in environments shared with humans.

Dr. Benedetti said: "Urban birds clearly react to subtle cues that humans do not easily notice. Follow-up studies could focus on individual factors such as movement patterns, scent cues, or physical traits, testing them separately rather than grouping them under observer sex. This approach would help identify the specific cues birds detect."

More Research Is Needed to Explain the Bird Behavior

Although the investigation included 2,701 observations, the researchers emphasize that their findings are still preliminary. Further studies will be necessary to establish whether the difference between birds' responses to men and women consistently appears in other settings and populations.

For now, the discovery points to an intriguing possibility: Birds sharing our cities may be paying attention to subtle differences between people that scientists have yet to fully understand.

#2739. The U.S. Public Is Losing Its Say on Environmental Policy

A hearing room in the headquarters of the Environmental Protection Agency in Washington, D.C. Carol M. Highsmith / Buyenlarge / Getty Images

Federal agencies have long been required to seek public comment when writing new regulations or approving large projects, from highways to data centers. The Trump administration is systematically undercutting that process, stifling public input while skirting federal laws.

Public participation is a hallmark of environmental regulation, embedded in every major environmental statute enacted over the past half-century. Yet the Trump administration has systematically sought to curtail public input into the government’s environmental decision-making by shirking decades of precedent and making strained interpretations of longstanding federal law. Looked at collectively, the pattern of public disenfranchisement is unmistakable.

There are good reasons to speed up the process of writing regulations and issuing permits, but truncating public input has serious costs. It can result in flawed policy that is harder to implement, full of avoidable error, and more vulnerable to being struck down by courts. Input from industry, public interest groups, academics, states, and other agencies gives regulators critical feedback on the potential impact of their proposed rules, helping them to anticipate and avoid unforeseen problems. Stakeholders possess information, experience, and expertise the government lacks, and they can suggest alternatives to make rules more effective or less costly. By testing agency assumptions and pushing officials to explain their rationale, public comments perform a crucial vetting function. The process also helps to make government more accountable by alerting Congress to potential pitfalls and building a record in the event of judicial review.

In 2025, the administration began stifling public input on the rigorous environmental reviews required by the 1969 National Environmental Policy Act. Under this law, federal agencies must evaluate and disclose the foreseeable environmental impacts of the major projects they build, fund, or approve, from highways to dams to data centers. The point of NEPA disclosure is to avoid unnecessary harm by having agencies look before they leap.

The process can delay projects, but it can also improve them substantially. Studies show that public-comment requirements and stakeholder engagement have repeatedly identified environmental risks that proposing agencies had missed. Often, significant harms can be reduced by taking sensible steps, like rerouting roads around sensitive areas, as was done with Utah’s Legacy Parkway, which was redesigned to largely avoid the Great Salt Lake wetlands. In another example, the Energy Department amended its permitting plan for the Los Alamos National Laboratory to include a comprehensive wildfire-mitigation program after commenters focused attention on the risks of wildfire. These steps significantly reduced impacts when a fire later burned the region.

For this process to work, however, the government must share its draft impact statements with the public, which past Republican and Democratic administrations have done. The Trump administration lifted that obligation, though, and agencies including Interior, Agriculture, Energy, and Transportation announced last year that they will no longer share drafts, making informed public comment practically impossible. Several agencies have also jettisoned the requirement to analyze proposed projects’ indirect and cumulative impacts, like climate change. The agencies made these changes without taking comment first, thus narrowing public involvement without public involvement.

A 2011 fire near the Los Alamos National Laboratory in New Mexico. Public commenters drew attention to the risk of wildfires when the lab was undergoing environmental review. Jae C. Hong / Associated Press

The Trump administration’s efforts to suppress public input are part of a larger backlash in which critics on both the right and left claim that procedural red tape strangles worthy projects. Some of this criticism is fair, but it can be overdrawn. Infrastructure projects falter for many reasons, including high costs, supply chain problems, and opposition from rival developers. Environmental reviews may be part of the problem, but project timelines would be shorter if agencies followed NEPA rules more carefully from a project’s inception. Congress already limited the scope and length of environmental reviews in 2023, and while more steps are needed to streamline federal permitting, that can be done without jettisoning valuable public consultation.

The permitting reform legislation proposed in the Senate in September would, among other things, drastically limit legal remedies when agencies violate NEPA, barring courts from delaying projects even when the agencies make blatant errors in environmental reviews. If projects proceed no matter what, there is little incentive to engage the public. The changes might speed up projects, but they will also result in many avoidable and unnecessary harms.

In addition to thwarting comments on new projects, the administration is also marginalizing comments on new regulations. The president has directed agencies to issue rules without taking input first, citing the “good cause” exception to the requirement in the Administrative Procedure Act that agencies publish their proposed rules in the Federal Register, triggering a 30- or 60-day comment period. Decades of legal precedent holds that agencies may finalize rules before taking comment only in exceptional cases where they can justify the urgency — for example, where the rule is necessary to foil terrorism, respond to a public health emergency, or prevent investors from gaming the market in anticipation of a regulatory change.

Finalizing rules more quickly has obvious appeal — the current system is slow and cumbersome and blocks both Republican and Democratic administrations from getting things done. But shunting public input to the end of the process will not solve that problem if rules are less sound, prone to error, and more vulnerable to legal challenge as a result.

In another example of limiting public participation, the EPA is proposing to drop a longstanding federal requirement that state and local air agencies allow the public to comment on air pollution permits for new “minor” sources that emit pollution below a certain threshold. This classification could include data centers that divide up their power generation units to make each source appear smaller. These permits are highly controversial because data centers powered by gas or diesel generators emit substantial air pollution. But the rule change could allow these permits to be fast-tracked without any opportunity for the public to weigh in.

Gas turbines at an xAI data center in Memphis, Tennessee. The Trump administration is trying to dismiss a citizen suit against the data center. Brandon Dill for The Washington Post via Getty Images

Besides undercutting notice and comment, the administration has stymied public participation in a broad range of environmental decisions by declaring emergencies and citing national security, among other tactics. For example, in March, the Interior Department invoked a never-before-used national security exemption to relieve the oil and natural gas industry of Endangered Species Act requirements in the Gulf of Mexico on the rationale that court rulings enforcing the law might hamper energy production, which the administration linked to national security. In 2025, President Trump granted two-year exemptions from Clean Air Act hazardous air pollution standards to coal plants, coke ovens, and chemical manufacturers, invoking for the first time a national security-based provision in the Clean Air Act.

A hard-won rule to cut chemical plant pollution is being unraveled. Read more.

In another gambit in June, the EPA found a creative way to revoke three waivers that allowed California to set its own clean car standards: by relabeling them as rules and submitting them to Congress for fast-track “disapproval” under the Congressional Review Act. Both the Government Accountability Office and the Senate Parliamentarian objected to this maneuver but were ignored. Normally, waiver rescissions would require public notice and comment. (A district court has blocked EPA from relabeling a second batch of waivers.)

Separately, the administration is trying to frustrate the citizen suit provisions that Congress included in virtually every major environmental law passed in the 1970s and 1980s. Under these provisions, individuals or groups may file a lawsuit against regulated entities for statutory or regulatory violations (unless the federal government is “diligently prosecuting” the same violations). Citizen suits may also be brought against federal agencies for failing to perform congressionally mandated duties, like setting pollution standards by certain deadlines. This summer, in response to a citizen suit by a coalition of states and nonprofit groups, a federal court held that the EPA had failed to identify areas with unhealthy levels of soot pollution — a first step toward applying stricter control measures — by the legal deadline and ordered it to do so by early next year. (The administration is appealing.)

While it’s true that private enforcement actions can be inconvenient for an administration, there is no doubt that citizen suits have profoundly improved conditions for communities affected by harmful pollution. Both Republican and Democratic administrations have sometimes objected to these suits, but no administration has sought to eliminate them.

Until this summer, that is, when the Trump administration intervened in a citizen suit against xAI for violating the Clean Air Act when generating power for its data centers. The administration claims that the president may demand that a court dismiss a citizen suit for any reason, whether the administration is diligently prosecuting violations or not.

An EPA researcher details the agency’s assault on science. Read more.

Public participation does take time, but it is valuable to governance. It gives regulated industries, professional associations, public interest groups, experts, other agencies, states, and individuals a chance to be heard. It also helps to build trust in government. As quaint as that claim may sound, it is backed up by evidence. Research shows that when people participate in decisions that affect them, they are more likely to view those decisions as legitimate, even when they don’t go their way. Having a voice matters. There are good reasons to reform our regulatory and permitting system, but not by dispensing with public participation.

#2743. Clever Code Considered Harmful

There is something undeniably satisfying about coming up with clever solutions to hard problems. There is a joy when you challenge yourself to use recursion instead of iteration, for example, or when you create elegant, cascading layers of abstraction that ensure code is never duplicated.

My favourite outlet for this kind of programming is Project Euler(opens in new tab).

This is not "production-ready" code, though. This is recreational code. This is code that you write to feel clever, to impress fellow math nerds, to exercise your brain. When you solve a Project Euler problem, you never have to look at that code again. It's disposable. We aren't handing it off to someone else to maintain it.

This is a different universe from the environments we write code in day-to-day.

When it comes to day-to-day production code, here's the barometer I like to use: will a junior developer, someone at the very start of their career, struggle to understand this code?

In the context of a shared codebase, good code is simple code. Code that doesn’t do anything fancy. Code that makes minimal use of abstractions. Code that you’d use to explain fundamental concepts to novices.

And yet, the original version is way harder to understand. I had to burn a ton of calories trying to work out what it was doing. I suspect many junior developers would be completely stumped trying to decipher it.

In my opinion, the readability cost of the functional version is too high. It's not worth it.

To understand why I think readability is such a crucial attribute of good code, let’s look at a popular open-source library, lodash(opens in new tab).

lodash is an immensely popular tool. It’s downloaded more than 26,000,000 times a week on NPM alone, and has over 42,000 Github stars. There is something absolutely curious about it, though; it consistently has less than 10 open issues.

"Inbox zero" is a thing with email, but it never happens with issues on popular projects. And yet, lodash often sits at zero open issues. It's been like this for years.

Well, one reason is that the library’s primary author, John-David Dalton, is a passionate maintainer who spends a lot of his time triaging issues as they come in. But I don’t believe anyone, no matter how superhuman, can get a library this popular to 0 issues alone.

Years ago, I heard JDD on a podcast(opens in new tab) talk about how the key to managing a project like this is to encourage lots of folks to contribute to it. One of the ways he’s done this is by keeping the code at a pretty fundamental level; by using simple, basic constructs, it ensures that aspiring contributors can understand and contribute to the code, regardless of how much experience they have. I believe JDD mentions that they prefer if/else to ternaries simply because less people have experience with ternaries. When the goal is to keep the code simple, the expressive power of the ternary operator is detrimental.

This is important to keep in mind if you’re building an open-source tool, but it’s even more important if you’re working in a production codebase with other humans. Especially ones that have less experience than you.

As I write this update in 2023, Lodash has a couple hundred open issues. This is likely because its maintainer, JDD, has moved onto other projects.

Less code means less space for bugs to hide

This argument makes the case that short code is better, because it'll be easier to spot bugs. With every additional character you type, you're increasing the likelihood of a mistake.

This is true when it comes to typos, sure. But typos tend to be easy to catch and fix. The really troublesome bugs — the ones that tend to break user experiences for weeks as developers pass the support ticket around like a hot potato — are often caused by too much complexity, not too many characters.

In order to debug an issue, you have to wrap your mind around what the code is doing. When you create an abstraction to reduce duplication (“Make it DRY”), you add a layer of indirection that your mind has to unpack. The harder it is for your mental model to account for every edge-case and possible state, the more likely it is that you’ll have trouble diagnosing what’s gone wrong.

“Everyone knows that debugging is twice as hard as writing a program in the first place. So if you’re as clever as you can be when you write it, how will you ever debug it?”

If the goal is to reduce complexity, and abstractions add complexity, should we abolish abstractions altogether?

Well, no. Abstractions are everywhere. Loops are abstractions. Functions are abstractions. Programming languages themselves are abstractions over machine code, which itself is an abstraction over transistors flickering off and on really fast. It’s abstractions all the way down.

The key is to weigh the cost of an abstraction against its benefit. Say we’re building a React app, and we have a list of 100 things to render. We could copy/paste the same JSX 100 times, or we could map over an array and write the JSX once. The “complex” solution in this case is totally worth it, because the underlying complexity is commonly known, and the alternative would be burdensome to maintain.

As we build stuff, we make trade-off decisions like this all the time. If I have a point, it’s that we should consider these tradeoffs with our most junior teammates in mind; how much complexity are we adding for them? Is it worth it?

Code sometimes has to be complex, because the real world is complex and our software has to model it! We won’t always be able to write code that a junior engineer can easily parse and contribute to. Sometimes the business logic is genuinely really tricky, sometimes we have to use an API with an inscrutable interface, and so on.

I think the best way to deal with this is to try and sequester complexity. Set up clear boundaries between the simple stuff and the complex stuff. Don’t let the complexity seep into the surrounding areas.

John-David Dalton did this with lodash. According to his interview in that same podcast(opens in new tab), the vast majority of lodash code is simple and easy-to-follow, and they’ve pushed the complex bits to a sophisticated core that handles the hard problems. This means that most contributors are spared from having to deal with that complexity, since it isn’t sprinkled across the application.

If your app is architected so that the most complex concerns are all dealt with in the same place, you can keep the overwhelming majority of your app’s surface area simple.

What if the junior engineer needs to work on that complex core? Well, good news! They have a more-senior person (you) to help guide them through it. Mentorship and education is a huge part of being a senior developer.

One of my favourite talks from React Rally last year was Chantastic’s “Hot Garbage; Clean Code is Dead”(opens in new tab). I won’t spoil the talk (seriously, go watch it!), but one of the takeaways I took from it is that everyone suffers from impostor syndrome, and as a result, we're always trying to prove to each other that we know our stuff. If we write a function that is super clever and indecipherable, our co-workers will know that we're smart, that we belong here!

What I've come to realize, though, is that anyone can write code that seems complicated. The hard thing is solving complex problems with simple code. If you can develop that skill, nobody will ever doubt your abilities. ✨

#2745. Oxford scientists discover how viruses build themselves without a blueprint

Researchers at the University of Oxford have observed virus-like particles assembling themselves one molecule at a time, uncovering the surprisingly simple rules that allow complex biological structures to form with remarkable reliability. The findings, published in Nature, offer a detailed look at a process that scientists have struggled to observe directly for decades.

Understanding how viruses construct their protective protein shells, and how that construction might be interrupted, could lead to new antiviral drugs, improved vaccines, and other medical treatments. The discovery helps explain how individual proteins consistently organize themselves into the correct structures despite having thousands of possible ways to connect.

The researchers found that viral assembly resembles navigating a complicated maze, where only certain paths lead to lasting progress. At first, protein components form weak connections that can easily break apart if the arrangement is unsuccessful. This flexibility allows the molecules to keep trying different combinations until they form particular closed structures. Once multiple connections hold these structures together, they become far more stable.

These stable arrangements serve as molecular checkpoints, narrowing the enormous number of possible assembly routes into a much smaller set of successful pathways. One especially important milestone occurs when five larger protein building blocks connect to form a closed pentagonal ring. This is the first highly stable structure in the assembly process. Once it forms, each subsequent stable stage requires fewer additional building blocks, allowing the particle to grow more quickly.

Co-lead author Dr. Roi Asor (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) explained: "A virus has to solve an extraordinary construction problem. Its components somehow have to find the right arrangement among a huge number of possibilities, without a blueprint or machinery directing the process. We can now watch what happens molecule by molecule and see the physical rules that make it possible."

Watching Virus Particles Assemble Molecule by Molecule

Viruses store and protect their genetic material within organized protein shells known as capsids. Depending on the virus, these shells may consist of tens or even thousands of individual protein components. Remarkably, in many cases, the proteins can arrange themselves into the correct structure without any external machinery directing their assembly.

Although scientists have investigated viral self-assembly for decades, capturing the process as it unfolds has been a major challenge. Many of the intermediate structures exist only briefly and appear in very small quantities. As a result, traditional methods have generally allowed researchers to examine the starting materials and finished particles without clearly showing how one transforms into the other.

To overcome this limitation, the Oxford researchers studied an engineered virus-like particle composed of 60 protein units. They used mass photometry, a technique developed at Oxford that determines the mass of individual molecules by measuring the light they scatter. By combining this technology with a new approach for keeping individual molecules confined and continuously observable, the team could repeatedly measure the mass of a growing particle as additional proteins attached.

This approach allowed the scientists to follow the construction process as it happened, effectively tracking the addition of individual molecular building blocks over time.

Co-first author PhD student Dan Loewenthal (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) said: "This method lets us study the assembly process directly, we just take a video!"

Co-lead author Professor Philipp Kukura (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, Oxford University) said: "Until now, much of our understanding of how these structures assemble has had to be reconstructed from snapshots or theoretical models. Being able to both quantify the underlying interactions and follow one particle as it grows changes that. We can see the important intermediate structures appear and deduce a detailed model of the construction process."

The Surprising Trick That Makes Viral Assembly Work

The results help explain how a process involving an overwhelming number of possible molecular arrangements can consistently produce the correct structure. Rather than exploring every possible route, the proteins progress through a relatively small number of stable intermediate stages that guide assembly toward completion.

The findings also clarify why the earliest stage of construction, called nucleation, is particularly difficult and why growth becomes substantially faster after the first stable structure appears.

A key part of this efficiency comes from the ability of protein building blocks to make mistakes without permanently disrupting the process. Weak initial connections allow incorrect combinations to separate, giving the proteins another opportunity to find a better arrangement. When enough components eventually connect in the correct closed configuration, the resulting structure becomes stable enough to support further growth.

Dr. Asor added: "The weak interactions give the system room to make mistakes. Most encounters don't have to be successful: the components can separate and try again. But once enough of them come together in the right closed arrangement, the structure becomes stable, and assembly can move forward. That combination of trial and error followed by locking in successful structures is what makes the process so reliable."

New Possibilities for Antiviral Drugs and Vaccines

By identifying the molecular interactions that control viral assembly, scientists may eventually be able to develop treatments that interfere with the construction of viral protein shells. The same insights could also support efforts to engineer virus-like particles for vaccines and other therapeutic applications.

Co-author Dr. Jack Tan (MRC Weatherall Institute of Molecular Medicine and CAMS-Oxford Institute) noted: "The ability to understand these processes at the molecular level could have important applications in vaccine development and antivirals that disrupt viral assembly."

The implications may extend far beyond viruses. Self-assembly is essential throughout biology, helping create everything from the internal skeletons of cells to specialized molecular compartments and large protein complexes.

By making it possible to observe these intricate construction processes one molecule at a time, the Oxford team's approach offers researchers a powerful new way to investigate how complex biological structures emerge from relatively simple interactions.

#2747. ‘Gratifying and humbling’: First Nation leads ghost net cleanup from Interior B.C. rivers

This article has been co-published with IndigiNews, and was produced as part of our 2026 Indigenous journalism fellowship.

After starting the day with a prayer and a safety demonstration, a group of volunteers gears up in water-resistant clothing and lifejackets, ready to hit the water.

The crew is preparing to scour a stretch of the Fraser and Thompson rivers, removing any fishing gear or waste left behind. Fishing nets left underneath the water are commonly known as ghost nets, which trap and kill fish.

As the saying goes on a volunteer call out poster, “ghost nets create ghost fish.”

TLKemchEEN (Lytton First Nation) has led the river clean-up program in the area for the past 20 years, all in an effort to preserve salmon populations and aquatic life following harvest season.

Over two days in September, a group of 40 volunteers spent a total of 20 combined hours on the water.

From Lillooet to Lytton during the first day, and from Lytton to Yale during the second day, volunteers passed through white-water rapids and rugged terrain such as the famously turbulent passage of Hell’s Gate.

When the raft leader yells “hold on,” that’s exactly what participants do, reaching down for a rope attached to the raft, laughing and hollering, ripping through the Fraser Canyon rapids.

The cold river water splashes everyone — waking up the crew as countless bald and golden eagles watch from above.

“I didn’t realize how impactful everything is along the shores. I guess I didn’t realize that some leave their garbage and nets out there,” volunteer Rebecca Riley, who is from the Stʼatʼimc Nation, said.

“Once I saw that with my own eyes, I wanted to jump in and be a part of it.”

It’s a sentiment shared by many volunteers on the boat, who eagerly approach the shoreline when a ghost net comes into sight.

Whether the net will take one person to remove, or up to five to pull out of the water, is unknown until people get their hands wet.

During the non-rapid portion of the cleanup, countless stories were told, Indigenous people exchanged words in their languages to compare similarities and differences and folks even had hot chocolate along the shore, courtesy of Kumsheen Rafting, which was contracted to support the program this year.

During the first year of the program, 126 ghost nets were collected.

“This year we picked up six nets and some garbage, some years prior we’ve picked up zero. We have to remind our fishermen not to be lazy and take their gear home,” Kevin Duncan told the Narwhal.

Duncan is an elected councillor for TLKemchEEN in B.C.’s Southern Interior, part of the Nlaka’pamux Nation Tribal Council. He has been involved with the program since its inception. He attended both cleanup days this year with his son Aaron.

For TLKemchEEN, stewarding their waters as they always have is crucial — especially as salmon numbers decline. Life along the Fraser and Thompson rivers is deeply connected to salmon. And TLKemchEEn is home to where the two rivers meet, in the country’s largest salmon-bearing watershed.

“For future generations, sometime down the road, I’m afraid all they’re going to be looking at is pictures of salmon in a book,” said Duncan.

The nets recovered by the program are given to those in need, including single mothers, low-income families and Elders from the nation.

“As a Nlaka’pamux or Stʼatʼimc member … that fishery is a communal right, it’s not an individual right. It’s looked after by your nation,” Duncan said.

“Most times, you just get to see portions of the river from the highway, but once you’re down on the water, floating for eight hours from Lillooet to Lytton, it gives you a better perspective on where people fish.”

Removing fishing gear from the cold and fast-moving river can be dangerous, which is why Duncan starts each day of the program with a yémit, or prayer in Nlaka’pamxcín.

“Salmon are the lifeblood of our people, and when these fish pass through coastal communities, they pray for them in the lower Fraser. We’re doing the same thing as they pass through our territories in the mid Fraser. … These fish hear that prayer.”

Volunteer Riley is Lillooet Tribal Council’s fisheries manager. She has participated in the river cleanup program three times, beginning in 2018 when she saw an advertisement in the newspaper.

Although she’s been on the Fraser and Thompson rivers many times throughout her fisheries career, she said that the ghost net removal program was “eye opening,” regarding the number of fishers out on the water.

She said that Lillooet Tribal Council is happy to help as the neighbouring nation to Nlaka’pamux territory, given the large area to cover and the shared value of protecting salmon.

“When we’re having the long days and we’re getting splashed and it’s cold, if we save a salmon or two that are still alive and let them free, or we clear out a net so we know that other animals can’t go in, it’s very gratifying and humbling,” she said.

“At the end of the day, when you’re riding the bus home and you’re cold and hungry, you know in the back of your mind it was so worth it.”

While the focus of the program is cleaning up the river, volunteers take the opportunity to support other initiatives while on the water, such as mapping traditional fishing sites to prove occupancy.

According to Conservation Biology, “Proof of occupancy is the foundational legal and moral requirement needed to establish Aboriginal Title, regain territorial sovereignty and protect ancestral lands from unauthorized exploitation.”

Lillooet Tribal Council’s extreme heat resiliency planner, Lyza Adolph, did a lot of heavy lifting over the two days removing ghost nets, but also had a mission of her own.

A member of the Stʼatʼimc Nation, this was her first time participating in the ghost net removal program.

“It was very cold, but with the group that we had, it was very enjoyable and uplifting,” she said.

With a focus on planning for extreme heat in her position at the tribal council, Adolph monitored fishing sites during the program to see if there were any shade trees nearby, scoping out areas to potentially plant some.

“The program tied in with my traditional shade tree planting. I know more about the upper river, but from Lillooet to Lytton and beyond, I didn’t know too much about it. It was definitely nice to see the various fish camps and see if there were shade trees,” she told The Narwhal.

Shade trees are beneficial for both humans and aquatic life.

As spawning salmon break down, their nutrients feed the trees. In turn, trees offer shade to cool the water, which is critical for survival on a warming planet, and tree roots and branches give salmonids space to hide from predators while also preventing erosion.

In the Fraser Canyon, which includes the communities of Yale, Lytton and Lillooet, fishing sites can be hard to access. Adolph’s shade tree monitoring data, collected by boat, will be used in future tree planting projects.

“I would definitely do it again,” Adolph said. “Although it was very cold, it was very refreshing and rewarding by the end of the day.”

#2750. Jonathan, a 192-Year-Old Giant Tortoise, Holds Insights for Aging Well

Jonathan, the oldest known living land animal. Saint Helena Government

At 192 years old, Jonathan the giant tortoise is the oldest known living land animal. A new study offers clues as to his incredible longevity.

Jonathan was born in the Seychelles in 1832, but since 1882, he has lived on the South Atlantic island of Saint Helena, where he resides at the home of its governor. Today, he is roughly a century older than most other Seychelles giant tortoises.

So why has Jonathan lived so long? An analysis of cheek scrapings has found that he is unusually blessed with good genes — not just genes that encode for good health, but genes that resist wear and tear.

Jonathan bears dozens of genetic variants that help him combat aging by curbing inflammation, regulating insulin, and warding off cancer. But also, his genes are uncommonly good at repairing themselves, according to the research, which was published in Science Advances.

“Nature has already solved the puzzle of aging in remarkable ways, and Jonathan’s genome provides a blueprint for cellular resilience,” said lead author Stephen Clark, chief scientist at Kallel, a nonprofit that researches longevity.

Increasingly, scientists are finding that older animals play a critical role in nature by helping their younger kin to survive. While conservationists have long prioritized the survival of younger animals, a growing body of research shows that older animals hold knowledge that guides survival, play outsized roles in reproduction, and possess stronger defenses against disease.

“Not all individuals contribute equally,” Keller Kopf, an ecologist at Charles Darwin University, told Yale Environment 360. “Older animals play roles that are often invisible in simple population counts.”
