#301. Meta hit with $25 billion penalty for misleading Facebook users

A New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay $25 billion in penalties Wednesday after a jury found in September that the social media giant misled users about their data privacy.

Why it matters: The penalties come nearly a decade after the Cambridge Analytica scandal first broke and signal that Big Tech can face significant financial consequences for misleading users about their privacy.

Driving the news: Judge Francis Mathew determined the penalty amount Wednesday, handing New Mexico a win as the only state to take its claims to trial after 48 others settled.

  • Mathew concluded New Mexico was entitled to $219.5 billion based on the state's maximum $5,000 penalty multiplied by 43,899,720 violations. He reduced that to $25 billion.

What they're saying: "This ruling, and the Attorney General's approach to this enforcement, makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico," C.J. Mahoney, Meta's chief legal officer, told Axios in an emailed statement.

  • "At its core, this lawsuit seeks to impose a tax on speech to enforce new rules for what people can say online. We are confident we will prevail on appeal. In the meantime, Meta will continue to build a safe and free platform to connect millions of Americans, grow small businesses, and help the country maintain its competitive edge."

Catch up quick: In September, a New Mexico jury said Meta had committed 43 million "willful violations" of the New Mexico Unfair Practices Act.

  • Mathew found that Facebook's privacy controls failed to give users meaningful control over their data and that third-party app developers could access their information through the platform's systems.
  • Mathew found Mark Zuckerberg knew users didn't understand how data privacy works, and exploited that, noting that the CEO had once written that users "probably don't understand their current privacy settings or care about who sees their updates."
  • Zuckerberg also wrote that the company should give users "a chance to change their privacy settings but obviously don't encourage it."

Zoom out: The suit followed reporting that revealed data firm Cambridge Analytica had harvested data from more than 50 million Facebook users to craft political ads during the 2016 election.

  • After the scandal, Facebook promised more transparency, but the court found Meta failed to consistently inform users when their data was misused.

What Happened: A New Mexico Democrat in the House introduced a bill last week that would make it easier for U.S. citizens to sue immigration agents and the federal government over wrongful detentions and arrests.

Rep. Teresa Leger Fernández credited ProPublica’s reporting as the catalyst for the bill, the Justice for Citizens Wrongfully Detained by ICE Act. Earlier this month, we revealed that more than 500 U.S. citizens have been detained in warrantless arrests during the second Trump administration.

Leger Fernández said the legislation would roll back the protections from lawsuits that agents and the federal government currently have. It would also establish a minimum level of compensation for people who win their suits, and extend the statute of limitations for such cases.

The legislation would also take back any unspent Immigration and Customs Enforcement funding from President Donald Trump’s so-called One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

Leger Fernández’s bill was introduced with co-sponsor Rep. Bonnie Watson Coleman, a New Jersey Democrat.

Why It Matters: It can be difficult to sue federal officers for wrongful arrests or excessive force, or for local prosecutors to bring charges. Federal agents can claim immunity in response to civil lawsuits or to state charges and can move their cases to federal court.

People who accuse immigration agents of wrongdoing can currently only sue in a narrow set of circumstances, and previous Democratic bills aiming to change that have not passed.

Some local prosecutors have announced investigations into the wrongful arrests of U.S. citizens or prosecuted federal agents accused of wrongdoing during immigration enforcement actions.

What They Said: In an interview with ProPublica, Leger Fernández emphasized that accountability could not only address past wrongs, it may also help head them off in the future. “We know that when there are consequences, bad actors start behaving better because they don’t want to face those consequences,” she said.

Without legislation, Leger Fernández said, agents “will continue to violate the law, to violate their own policy, and to violate the Constitution without any consequences.”

Asked about the legislation, the Department of Homeland Security, ICE’s parent agency, repeated claims that it is not arresting U.S. citizens and defended its immigration operations as “highly targeted.”

Background: The federal government denies that there is any pattern of immigration agents detaining U.S. citizens, but according to ProPublica’s count, more than 100 citizens were held after agents questioned their citizenship. At least 70 children and teens were also detained.

Last year, congressional Democrats announced a wide-ranging investigation into immigration agents’ detention of U.S. citizens after our earlier investigation found that more than 170 citizens were detained by immigration agents.

#303. Vucic concedes defeat in Serbia's parliamentary election

October 25, 2026 / 5:30 PM EDT / AP

The long-serving strongman leader of Serbia, Aleksandar Vucic, conceded defeat on Sunday in an election that pitted him and his right-wing party against a diverse student-led opposition movement that spent two years protesting his government.

It was a seismic political shift in the Balkan country where Vucic, who had been endorsed by Russian President Vladimir Putin and who denounced the student movement as a foreign-led attempt to unseat him, led with virtually absolute power for 14 years.

High turnout and gathering tensions marked the highly contested parliamentary election that was a showdown between Vucic and his right-wing Serbian Progressive Party and university students who, accusing the government of corruption and dismantling democracy, had built a popular following across the country.

Serbia's 6.4 million voters were electing lawmakers to the 250-member Parliament. In conceding the election, Vucic said his party projects that his opponents, the youth-backed Students' List, will take between 135 and 140 seats while his SNS party will have around 105.

"The Students' List has won," Vucic told supporters at his party's election night event, though he blamed Serbia's independent media as well as alleged interference by unnamed European countries and an "American deep state" for the loss.

Vucic said that his SNS party won around 1.7 million votes compared to the Students' List, which he said took around 2.2 million.

Supporters of the Students' List immediately flooded onto the streets of Belgrade and other cities, honking horns, shouting and chanting: "Aleksandar is gone," referring to Vucic.

The outcome, which Vucic announced only around an hour and a half after polls closed, came after tensions had built as voting progressed during the day.

Police in anti-riot gear cordoned off an area in central Belgrade around the election commission headquarters. Special police vehicles could be seen near a Belgrade park where Vucic's loyalists have been camping for over a year, and large groups of black-clad men had congregated at various points throughout the city.

Vucic was seeking to become Serbia's prime minister after serving two near-full terms as president. He resigned the presidency last month to lead the campaign.

Support for the populist had eroded since 16 people were killed in 2024 when a concrete canopy collapsed at a train station in Novi Sad. That set off a wave of youth protests that blamed the collapse on high-level corruption, cronyism and rampant graft during renovation work at the station.

Despite the protests, Vucic retained considerable support among many of Serbia's voters. Admirers viewed him as a capable leader who can steer the nation through global uncertainties - the image that was carefully crafted by the mainstream media.

While the increasingly authoritarian Vucic has routinely outplayed Serbia's fractured opposition in the past, the leaderless youth movement proved to be a tough opponent that rattled his firm grip on power in Serbia like no one else in the 14 years since his party took office.

The newly formed Students' List movement was shaped by university students, but none actually appeared on the election list. Instead, its candidates are little-known experts, athletes, farmers and artists.

This approach was meant to counter Vucic's one-man rule and restore a sense of community and solidarity. The students' message during the campaign focused on a non-partisan effort to rebuild Serbia's economy and restore its democracy.

The students have used youthful energy to attract voters who are disillusioned with local politicians. Vilified by pro-government media, they cycled and walked through the country to reach voters in rural areas — Vucic's traditional strongholds.

The student victory echoed the recent electoral defeat of a Vucic ally in Hungary, where youth participation helped oust the autocratic Viktor Orbán after 16 years and bring in a new pro-European government. It was likely to resonate beyond Serbia as countries across Europe have faced angry youth and student protests.

Monitors predicted that total turnout in the election could reach around 70%, unseen in Serbia since 2000. During the day, long queues formed at polling stations in Serbia and in cities around the world. Some in the Serbian diaspora were made to vote in the middle of the night after authorities tied voting hours to local Serbian time.

Recent Serbian elections had been marred by irregularities, and the EU was closely watching the conduct of Sunday's vote in a candidate nation for the bloc's membership.

Independent election monitors reported dozens of cases of irregularities on Sunday such as photographing of ballots that suggested pressure on voters and attempted vote buying.

Both international and local election monitors had expressed concern about the safety and fairness of an election campaign marked by detentions, near-total government control over the media and reports of surveillance and cyberattacks against activists.

Local elections earlier this year in 10 municipalities in Serbia saw violent attacks on journalists and activists. The previous parliamentary vote in 2023 was followed by opposition protests alleging fraud.

#304. Your guide to the California lieutenant governor race: Ma vs. Romero

The race for California’s next lieutenant governor offers voters a familiar choice between a Democrat and Republican to succeed termed-out Lt. Gov. Eleni Kounalakis.

The job is largely a ceremonial post, but if the sitting governor dies, resigns or is removed from office, then the lieutenant governor would be elevated to take over.

In the June primary, state Treasurer Fiona Ma finished first with 19.1% of the approximately 8.5 million ballots cast. In second was former State Senate Majority Leader Gloria Romero, with 17.8%. Voters will pick a winner on Nov. 3.

The California Democratic Party is backing Ma, whose quarter-century in electoral politics includes stints on the San Francisco Board of Supervisors, in the State Assembly and on the State Board of Equalization. She was elected treasurer in 2019 and is termed out.

The California Republican Party has endorsed Romero, who has been campaigning with Republican gubernatorial candidate Steve Hilton on what the two term a “golden ticket” (though voters elect each office separately).

The Republican designation is relatively new for Romero. She was a Democrat when she served in the Legislature from 1998 to 2010. She became a Republican in 2024, asserting that Democratic party standards no longer aligned with her beliefs.

“I consider myself to be common sense, a new Republican with the values of American exceptionalism that once upon a time the Democratic party stood for, but it’s been overrun by the socialists and the communists that have taken over the party,” she told the Times.

Romero grew up in a Catholic household in Barstow, with pictures of the Pope and President Kennedy on the walls. She described her parents, a railroad worker and a mother who once cleaned houses, as “Depression-era Democrats.”

She started in community college and eventually earned a PhD. She was a college professor, an L.A. Community College trustee and a city of L.A. charter reform commissioner before being elected to the state Assembly in 1998. She moved to the Senate in 2001 and became the first female majority leader. Today she lives in El Sereno.

The lieutenant governor position often is described as essentially powerless. The officeholder serves on the California Coastal Commission (as a nonvoting member) and on the boards overseeing the University of California, California State University and community college systems.

Both candidates claim they would be more than a governor-in-waiting. Ma said her background in accounting and finance would allow her to be impactful. She pledges to use the “bully pulpit.”

“I am not shy to be out there, fight the good fight, take on challenges head on,” she told The Times.

Romero, who while in the state Senate wrote the “parent trigger” law giving parents more power over underperforming schools, envisions having a prominent role in the education system. She hopes to help lower the cost of attending a University of California school.

She said her background would enable her to take advantage of the lieutenant governor’s role as president of the state Senate.

“I look at it as, if you think you are powerless, you will behave in a powerless way,” she said.

The Nov. 3 ballot measure would put a retroactive, one-time 5% tax on Californians with assets above $1 billion. The aim is to raise money to offset federal healthcare cuts.

Both candidates oppose it, with nuance in their reasoning.

“We need our billionaires, our millionaires and all our companies and people who are paying taxes, especially when the federal government is not giving us our fair share,” Ma said.

She also believes the initiative is poorly written by aiming to tax people on their net worth. “Most of these people don’t have liquid money sitting around,” she said.

Romero bashed the proposition as an attempt at “asset seizure” and cast it as the first step in eventually taxing people further down the economic ladder.

“It is basically a death march that will throw California over the edge,” she said.

Another ballot measure would require voters to show government-issued ID at the polls, or provide the last four digits of a government-issued ID if voting by mail. It builds upon assertions by President Trump that the nation’s voting system is insecure, though elections experts have dismissed allegations of widespread fraud.

Ma opposes the measure, saying, “We are trying to make voting accessible and easier.”

Romero strongly supports it, saying people must show ID for things such as checking into a hotel. “It’s really about election integrity,” she says.

If Ma wins, she would be a high-ranking official in a state that consistently clashes with Trump and the federal government. Romero, meanwhile, might find herself opposite a Democratic governor in Xavier Becerra, who is the front-runner, and a Legislature in which Democrats have a supermajority.

“I have a reputation of working across the aisle during my time in the Legislature,” Ma said. She adds, “I’m willing to work with anyone who wants to work with me, and focus on the needs and priorities of California.”

Romero says she knows Becerra from when he served in Congress and she was in the state Senate and has “great regard and great respect” for him. She thinks he “took a lurch to the left,” but she knows that campaigning and governing are different beasts.

“I favor Steve of course, but I know Xavier, and I think he and I could work very well together,” she said.

#305. Trump is trying to overturn an election that hasn’t happened yet

Trump’s lawyers want to block Alaska Democrat Mary Peltola’s victory in an election that is more than a week away.

President Donald Trump’s Justice Department filed a lawsuit on Thursday seeking to block former Democratic Rep. Mary Peltola’s victory in the 2026 US Senate election. If you’re confused by this development, you should be. Because the election won’t happen for more than a week.

The case arises out of an odd candidacy that might undercut incumbent Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan’s reelection bid against Peltola. A man named Dan J. Sullivan also secured a spot on the ballot, and Republicans fear that the not-Senator Sullivan’s presence on the ballot might trick voters who wish to vote for the incumbent into voting for the not-senator instead.

Get the latest developments on the US Supreme Court from senior correspondent Ian Millhiser.

In the abstract, it’s a fair concern. But there are two reasons to doubt that not-Senator Sullivan will attract many voters who actually wanted to vote for the Republican. According to a sample ballot published by Alaska’s Division of Elections, Sen. Sullivan will be identified as a “Registered Republican” and as the “Incumbent,” so voters will be told which Dan Sullivan to vote for if they wish to reelect the one who is actually a senator.

Additionally, Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, a system where voters rank candidates in order of preference, and votes cast for losing candidates are redistributed to voters’ next choice. So an Alaska voter who wants to reelect the Dan Sullivan who is actually a senator, but who is confused as to which Dan Sullivan is the incumbent, can simply rank both Dans Sullivan as their top two choices, and the ranked choice system should ensure that their vote is counted as a vote for Senator Sullivan (and against Peltola).

The question of whether not-Senator Sullivan may appear on the ballot, moreover, has already been litigated. Initially, the Alaska Division of Elections, which is led by a Republican, attempted to disqualify the not-senator because it concluded that he did not seek election to the Senate in “good faith.” Not-Senator Sullivan sued, and an Alaska court ordered him reinstated as a candidate because “the Division’s decision to exclude Mr. Sullivan from the primary ballot was based on a ‘good-faith’ requirement that does not appear in the U.S. Constitution, the Alaska Statutes, or the implementing regulations adopted by the Division” — that is, there is not actually a law that prevents the not-senator from running, even if he is intentionally running as a spoiler candidate.

This lower court order was affirmed by the Alaska Supreme Court last June. If Republicans thought that this decision violates federal law, they could have asked the Division of Elections — which, again, is run by a Republican — to appeal to the US Supreme Court.

Instead, they appear to have let the issue lie until less than two weeks before Election Day, when Trump’s Justice Department filed a new, federal lawsuit claiming that not-Senator Sullivan’s presence on the ballot is somehow illegal, and asking a federal court to preemptively block a Peltola victory in the upcoming election. Trump says that he personally told the Justice Department to file this suit.

It is difficult to find an actual legal argument, much less a persuasive one, anywhere in the Trump DOJ’s complaint initiating the lawsuit. Trump’s lawsuit also runs afoul of something called the “Purcell principle” — a concept that, ironically, the Supreme Court reaffirmed the same day that the Trump administration filed its suit — which says that federal courts should be reluctant to intervene in election-related disputes when the election itself is about to happen.

It is likely, in other words, that the Trump administration’s attempt to overturn a Democratic victory that hasn’t even happened yet will fail. But the mere existence of this new lawsuit, which is known as United States v. Alaska, is an ominous sign that the Trump administration will use every tool in its shed to overturn Democratic victories in the upcoming election.

It is a bit of a stretch to even describe the Justice Department’s complaint in this case as a “legal” document, as the complaint does not so much as point to any law that either not-Senator Sullivan or the state of Alaska may have violated, and instead vaguely alludes to legal sources that use terms like “integrity.”

Specifically, the Justice Department points to a federal law which says that it has four purposes, one of which is to “protect the integrity of the electoral process.” It also points to the Supreme Court’s decision in Bullock v. Carter (1972), which said that “a State has an interest, if not a duty, to protect the integrity of its political processes from frivolous or fraudulent candidacies.”

But the statute that Trump’s lawyers quote, the National Voter Registration Act of 1993, does not concern which candidates’ names should appear on the ballot. As its name suggests, it is a law about voter registration, and it imposes requirements on states, such as requiring them to register voters when those voters obtain a driver’s license.

Similarly, while Bullock does include a single line indicating that states may enact laws targeting “frivolous or fraudulent candidacies,” nothing in the opinion suggests that sharing another candidate’s name makes someone’s candidacy frivolous or fraudulent. The decision itself concerned unusually high fees charged to a candidate for public office in Texas, and the Court concluded that Texas’s fees were unconstitutional.

The line about “frivolous or fraudulent candidacies,” meanwhile, was included to clarify that states still have some power to regulate ballot access. While Bullock concluded that Texas’s fees were excessive, the opinion also suggests that states may impose more reasonable filing fees on candidates to discourage unserious candidates from seeking office. Alaska, for example, charges a modest filing fee of $100 to US Senate candidates.

The Trump DOJ’s entire argument, in other words, appears to turn on the fact that it identified two legal sources that use the word “integrity” in contexts that bear little resemblance to the Alaska case.

Nevertheless, Trump’s lawyers claim that the court must prevent Alaska from certifying a Peltola victory “pending an investigation to determine whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan.”

Even if Trump’s Justice Department had a persuasive argument that leaving not-Senator Sullivan on the ballot is illegal, they should still lose Alaska because they waited too long to file the case.

In Purcell v. Gonzalez (2006), the Supreme Court warned that “court orders affecting elections, especially conflicting orders, can themselves result in voter confusion and consequent incentive to remain away from the polls,” and it added that this “risk will increase” as Election Day draws closer. More recent decisions, many of them joined only by the Court’s Republicans, have read this “Purcell principle” quite expansively to forbid federal courts from issuing election-related decisions in the months leading up to an election. Justice Brett Kavanaugh has even claimed that Purcell prevents federal courts from issuing election-related decisions as soon as nine months before the general election date.

Supreme Court decisions invoking Purcell to shut down lower court orders altering a state’s election rules are now very common in the weeks leading up to an election. On Thursday, for example, the Court relied on the Purcell principle to block a lower court order that would have made it easier for college students to vote in New Hampshire. The Court began its decision with this line: “This Court has repeatedly emphasized that lower federal courts should ordinarily not alter the election rules on the eve of an election.”

Trump’s DOJ, meanwhile, did not even file its lawsuit seeking to preemptively invalidate a Peltola victory until less than two weeks before Election Day. If Purcell means anything, it must mean that a federal court cannot force Alaska to kick a candidate off the ballot mere days before a federal election.

#310. Israeli military conducts massive "power projection" operation near Iran's border

On a cold February morning at West Virginia’s Capitol, children in winter jackets, holding signs and their homework, clogged the hallways outside the House Finance Committee room. Delegate Vernon Criss, the committee’s chairperson, popped outside his office, then promptly walked back in.

Five days earlier, the Republican lawmaker had proposed to limit payouts from a voucher program that diverts public tax dollars to private education. Since then, families had shown up each morning, and with them lobbyists and organizers posting inflammatory assertions that the proposal would kill the program.

Criss didn’t want that. Five years earlier, he voted to create the Hope Scholarship program, the nation’s first educational savings account program to have no income requirements. Since then, 11 other states have passed similar legislation.

In the first year of the Hope program in West Virginia, it cost the state $9 million. Now, as 2026 was beginning, it was set to expand, with estimates that it could cost $200 million or more a year. And Criss was concerned that unchecked growth of the program, coupled with a spree of tax cuts, could put the state budget into a tailspin. The state and its lawmakers needed “to face the music,” as Criss would later say.

On that February day in Charleston, a staffer unlocked the committee door. Parents and media filed in, taking seats along the narrow benches lining the committee room’s walls. Kids lay on the floor. A delegate asked if the Hope Scholarship bill would be discussed. When Criss said no, the parents and their kids stood up and filed out.

Within the next few days, lawmakers would stop the effort to curtail spending on vouchers. And Criss would soon be fighting for his political life.

The money and organizing behind the demise of the bill, as well as the targeting of Criss, can be traced to “school choice” advocates at the local and national levels and their ability to spend huge sums to have their way.

When Republicans assumed control of the West Virginia Legislature in 2015, school choice — their preferred terminology for voucher-style programs — became a rallying point for an array of political nonprofits. Through lobbying and supporting sympathetic lawmakers, they helped establish the Hope Scholarship six years later.

The first year, a little more than 2,300 students enrolled in the program, which was designed to permit more students over time. Today, 26,000 students are taking part, more than the enrollment in the state’s largest public school district.

West Virginia has been part of a nationwide push, partially funded by billionaire-backed political groups, to use taxpayer dollars to fund private schools.

Among the proponents of these programs are the influential Koch family network and Pennsylvania billionaire Jeffrey Yass, who has spent more than $350 million since 2015 on political campaigns, including a push for public funding for private education.

In West Virginia, Arkansas and Texas, proponents have been eager to challenge anyone who got in the way, including fellow Republicans who voiced concerns. Basic guardrails or efforts to cap spending have been met with intense resistance.

During the 2026 West Virginia primary, three political action committees spent heavily on independent advertisements, targeting 10 incumbent Republicans. They succeeded in ousting four, all of whom had disagreements with the governor; most expressed reservations about the Hope Scholarship.

Their biggest victory was unseating Criss, the long-serving incumbent and high-ranking Republican.

One of the three PACs working to defeat Criss is affiliated with the Koch family. Yass has contributed to the two others: School Freedom Fund and the Sugar Maple PAC. David McIntosh, president of School Freedom Fund and the affiliated Club for Growth, was jubilant afterward.

“These elections are a warning to Republican leaders like Vernon Criss — if you oppose school freedom, expect to be held accountable,” he wrote in a press announcement at the time.

Have you had trouble finding a school or using a voucher-style program? Do you have concerns about schools — public or private — in your area? Help us understand how families across the country are navigating their school options.

Criss had been unopposed in his 2024 primary. In 2026, the three PACs spent more than $145,000 to help a 23-year-old defeat him.

Criss’ proposal to put financial limits on the Hope Scholarship program came as West Virginia public schools arrived at a dangerous crossroads. The program directs dollars away from public schools, adding to funding shortfalls created by enrollment drops.

Since 2019, 70 public schools have closed throughout the state, with the system losing more than 30,000 students. By the end of this school year, up to 20 more schools could close.

During the same legislative session this year, when lawmakers passed emergency legislation to help one school district stay open, there was little drama — no crowds in the hallways or the galleries.

In 2021, as West Virginia lawmakers worked on the bill that would create the Hope Scholarship, they got some help from the outside: a lobbyist and political operative named Jason Huffman.

Huffman has been the director of Americans for Prosperity-West Virginia since the mid-2010s. The group is part of the larger Americans for Prosperity, which was set up by the billionaire industrialist David Koch and supported by his brother Charles, longtime donors to conservative causes. Americans for Prosperity advocates for low taxes, school choice and loosened environmental regulations throughout the country.

In shaping the West Virginia legislation, Huffman said, one goal was to create a school voucher program that other states could model. While other states had already passed voucher-style programs, they hadn’t made them open to all residents regardless of income.

Among those advocating in West Virginia were members of Cardinal Institute, a homegrown conservative think tank. Founded in 2015, the group made public funding for private schooling one of its first policy planks. Over the last decade, Cardinal has received significant donations from organizations that also have received funding from Koch entities and Yass.

To support the Hope legislation, Cardinal’s leaders distributed research, published columns in newspapers and linked up with other groups, like Americans for Prosperity and the Institute for Justice, which litigates libertarian causes, to push the issue. They also found allies in parents who wanted the financial flexibility to send their children to private schools or teach them at home or in small clusters of like-minded families.

Katie Switzer, a mother of six, became one of Cardinal’s most reliable advocates. She had started looking into schooling options after her second child was diagnosed with a learning disability.

“I just felt like this doesn’t feel good to send my nonverbal child to a school that you know isn’t really collaborative, and I don’t know what’s going on when she’s there because she can’t tell me when she gets home,” she said. The experience made her a proponent of alternatives to public school.

Her story appeared in some of Cardinal’s promotional copy for the bill. She sat in on a video call presentation featuring state Sen. Patrcia Rucker, a Hope Scholarship proponent. She took to Facebook to tout the program.

And she stepped up when the Hope Scholarship was challenged in court by other parents who argued the program would undermine funding for public schools.

Hope’s opponents in the case gained support from the state Board of Education, which argued the program siphoned money away from public schools. On the other side was the West Virginia attorney general, Patrick Morrisey, defending the law and the state.

The Institute for Justice was looking to get involved, but it needed to find somebody with standing in the case. Switzer and another parent signed up, giving the firm the ability to file motions and argue in court. The Institute for Justice has received $8.5 million from charities controlled by Yass over nearly a decade.

The case went to the West Virginia Supreme Court, where the justices ruled 4-1 in favor of the program.

The ruling meant the Hope program had survived. It was good timing for Morrisey. He was about to make a run for governor. When Morrisey waded into a tough four-way primary battle, the Club for Growth Action, a super PAC Yass has donated heavily to over the years, was ready to help.

“Our goal in all of this is to elect people who will be champions for school freedom, lower taxes, less regulation, so businesses can thrive,” McIntosh, the group’s president, said in an interview.

The Club for Growth Action pumped $10.8 million into that race. Morrisey won the plurality in the Republican primary and went on to trounce his Democratic opponent in the general election.

On a cold morning in January 2025, Morrisey stood bundled up in a black wool coat on the stairs of Capitol, its gold dome towering above him. He took the oath of office and gave his address.

“We will expand the Hope Scholarship, putting more control in the hands of parents to choose the best educational path for their children,” he said.

Hours before Morrisey gave his State of the State address earlier this year, the House Education Committee called a meeting to hear about a report from the Rand Corporation, a nonpartisan think tank.

Rand called on the state to fix what was becoming abundantly clear: Education in West Virginia wasn’t just in transition; it was in crisis.

Initially the Hope Scholarship was limited to students attending public school or children who would enter kindergarten. Participation grew over time. And as the 2026-27 school year began, the program opened to any student in West Virginia, including those who had spent their entire academic careers in private school or in homeschooling.

That translated into tens of millions of additional state dollars to private schools and homeschooling. Estimates for the 2026-27 school year ranged from $230 million to more than $300 million in the months leading up to the legislative session. The program costs for the year are still being tallied but are currently projected at an unprecedented $150 million.

The Rand report suggested placing an income cap on the Hope Scholarship and increasing funding for public schools.

While the Education Committee would work on public school funding, Criss’ Finance Committee would tackle the ballooning costs of the Hope Scholarship. He was not interested in Rand’s solutions but found his own. By capping the scholarship amount and limiting certain expenses, Criss was hoping he could help prolong the program for families well into the future. But families would soon be rallying against him.

In the time since the Hope Scholarship had withstood the court challenge, Switzer’s reach had only grown. On Facebook, she administered a group that offers practical advice to hundreds of parents, or “Hope Schoolers” as she called them, on questions like what items counted as qualifying expenses and how to properly file for the program.

Before Criss’ plan rolled out, Switzer said she received word from a member of the Finance Committee — though she did not disclose who — that the Hope Scholarship would be discussed.

Writing a lengthy Facebook post, she warned that the move would severely damage the program. “The bill takes away 19,000 children’s access to online education, tutoring, private schools across state borders, and many other beneficial academic programs,” she wrote.

Huffman, the Americans for Prosperity state director, took to radio and social media to decry what he described as an existential threat to the program. Parents with kids in tow echoed those fears.

Criss’ proposal would’ve capped the award amount for the Hope Scholarship at a little more than $5,000 a year. He said his intent was to prolong the program for the long run, not kill it.

Huffman countered that by capping the award amount, inflation would eventually erode families’ abilities to use the program for private education or homeschooling.

Looking back, Criss said he anticipated some pushback, because the Hope Scholarship is a “giveaway program.” He blamed political forces for riling people up.

At a caucus meeting with the Republican supermajority — which holds 91 of the 100 seats in the lower chamber — Criss said he couldn’t muster the votes. A bill to fix public schools that came out of the Education Committee died entirely.

Election season was right around the corner, and Americans for Prosperity-West Virginia and other groups hadn’t forgotten the Hope Scholarship battle. Criss had failed what Huffman called “a litmus test” in trying to restrict public money for parents looking for an alternative to public school.

Meanwhile, McIntosh, the president for Club for Growth, said he received word from his allies in the Morrisey administration. Morrisey was concerned about lawmakers who had opposed him on budget issues and school choice. The governor was looking to replace those he saw as not aligned.

While the legislative backlash had been about one issue, the campaign against Morrisey’s foes went well beyond education issues.

Ads depicted Criss as a liberal who wanted to open borders for illegal immigration. That was based on a no vote Criss had cast against a piece of legislation that would’ve mandated employers run a citizenship check on every employee.

Fliers mailed by the School Freedom Fund, a PAC affiliated with Club for Growth, depicted candidates as supporters of drag queens. Americans for Prosperity-West Virginia sent volunteers door to door to rally for their candidates, including in Criss’ district.

The newly created Sugar Maple PAC — headed by a former state delegate and Morrisey ally — ran ads accusing opponents of being Republicans in Name Only.

One mailer, depicting Criss driving a garbage truck, accused him of taking “West Virginia’s values to the dump.” On the back, it reiterated his vote against the citizenship check for employees and his vote against West Virginia’s abortion ban.

“Vernon Criss’ liberal trash,” the flier read, with an image of black trash bags.

“I think that the fact that they called me a liberal, it just stuck out,” Criss said. “You know, as the chairman of finance, I know that it’s a targeted job, obviously, that’s part of the deal. Those types of things happen, that’s just part of the politics involved here.”

The School Freedom Fund poured nearly $1 million into statehouse races across West Virginia to both retain Morrissey’s supporters and oust detractors, like Criss. Americans for Prosperity did the same, again targeting Criss, and spending around $700,000 across the state.

A spokesperson for Morrisey said in an email that the governor is a supporter of both public schools and the Hope Scholarship and “is also grateful that organizations that share his worldview of helping West Virginia children having the best educational opportunities in the country weigh in.”

Sugar Maple PAC and Yass did not respond to requests for comment. Yass contributed $450,000 to Sugar Maple in the lead-up to the 2026 primary. Among the three PACs, Sugar Maple spent the most on the Criss race: $86,000 to attack Criss and $15,000 to back his opponent, Charles Hartzog, a 23-year-old gas station cashier.

Criss didn’t just lose the primary; Hartzog beat him with 61% of the vote. Criss is now running a long-shot campaign as a write-in for his district in the general election.

#314. 100 years later, the mystery of Houdini's death endures

Updated on: October 25, 2026 / 9:25 AM EDT / CBS News

Harry Houdini, the greatest escape artist who ever lived, has been the subject of hundreds of books and movies. A pop icon, even today. He died 100 years ago this week, on Halloween – a coincidence that did nothing to diminish his mystique. But we'll get to that.

Erik Weisz was born in Hungary, in 1874. When he was four, the family moved to Wisconsin. As a teenager, he performed magic tricks in sideshows and beer halls. "He was a skinny kid, and he built himself up," said New Yorker staff writer David Denby. "He became this beautiful, incredible body, and he was a handsome man."

But according to Denby, he wanted a catchier name. He turned to his idol, Houdin, the great French magician of the 19th century: "So, he took the name Houdin and added an i and pronounced it as Houdini. But the name always sounded funny. It's vaguely Italian, but it's not. What is it?"

Houdini historian Roger Dreyer owns one of the world's largest collections of Houdini artifacts, such as the Metamorphosis Trunk. He says Houdini's magic tricks didn't exactly bring the house down. "If he was to perform it today, he wouldn't even make it past the second round on 'America's Got Talent,'" Dreyer said. "It wasn't until his Metamorphosis Trunk that Houdini really became bookable; theaters wanted to see his act. People today, over 100 years after Houdini's death, still perform this exact same trick."

After being locked in a trunk, Houdini switched places with his new wife, Bess, instantly. But once he added handcuff escapes to his show, Houdini became a sensation. Denby said, "He would be put in a cell, virtually naked, shackled. He'd come out 20 minutes later holding the handcuffs. The specialists were called out of their corners to produce the most complicated locks for him. He'd lick it every time. There's no record of his failure."

Denby says there was symbolism in his escapes: "He would defeat those police forces all over the world. And so, there was definitely a kind of anti-authoritarian message that people liked."

In time, the act was more than escaping shackles. It became about escaping death. "He always reinvented himself," Dreyer said. "Initially, he got out of a trunk. Then Houdini got out of a straitjacket. Then Houdini would get out of a straitjacket hanging from a crane. Then Houdini would go inside a live jumbo water tank upside down."

Denby said, "Curtain closed, one minute, two minutes … was he drowning? What happened to him? They'd finally open the curtain, and he'd come out and his eyes would be all bloodshot and he would be gasping. He had to risk death."

In 1904, at age 30, Houdini bought a townhouse in Harlem. He moved in with Bess, his mother, and his brother. Today, Vincent Toms lives in that house. He even gives tours. "People, when they come to the house, you know, this is Graceland for them, for all intents and purposes," he said.

After World War I, a new industry sprang up: fake spiritualist mediums. Dreyer said, "Ninety percent of the people that were attending séances back then were women trying to contact their loved ones that might have died in World War I."

In the final act of his life, Houdini became a debunker of fake mediums, exposing the tricks they used once the lights were out.

By the mid-1920s, Houdini was the man who cheated death. But that was one escape he couldn't perform forever. He died at age 52. But how he died is yet another mystery.

The usual account involves college kids visiting him backstage. Dreyer said, "One of them asked Houdini, 'Is it true that you can withstand a blow to your stomach?' Houdini said, 'Yes.' And before Houdini could brace himself, that gentleman threw not one, but a series of punches."

But most experts believe that the punches didn't act alone. Houdini had a precondition of appendicitis. "And the punches in the stomach might have aggravated it," said Dreyer.

But a new book, "Houdini: The Man Who Died Twice" by surgeon and medical researcher Larrian Gillespie, documents a very different telling of the story.

"There's something very wrong with this death certificate," Gillespie said. "I see numbers were copied and then rewritten over. This was a cover-up."

She says that ultimately what killed him was an intestinal blockage and an unreported last-minute surgery. Houdini's team favored the stomach-punch story, in part to get a bigger insurance payment. "Houdini had a double indemnity policy," said Gillespie. "The first policy would pay for his death, but if he died from an accident, it paid an additional – in today's money – $500,000."

According to documents Gillespie found, there's even doubt about when Houdini died. "I can put forth a very strong case that he died on October 30th and not the 31st," she said. "By moving the date over to the next day, every time Halloween came up, you would remember that Houdini coincidentally died that day."

But Gillespie does not believe this version of events would in any way tarnish Houdini's reputation and legacy: "Oh, not at all. Houdini was gifted, and that will never change."

But whatever the details of his death, Houdini never completely ruled out the possibility of an existence beyond. Vincent Toms said, "He did make a pact with Bess that after he died, he would try to communicate with her, if there was any way possible. And so, for the 10 years after he died, they held a séance in this room every year on the anniversary of his death."

Every Halloween since, Houdini's fans have tried to reach him. Toms will try this weekend, hosting a séance in the house at 1:26 p.m.

So many mysteries. How did Houdini do his escapes? How did he really die? Is there an afterlife? But this much, we know for sure: "If Houdini was around today," said Dreyer, "he still would be a household name, because of what he signified: the idea of hope, escape, the power of possibility, the idea of becoming an immigrant going from poverty to stardom to fortune."

And the fact that this charismatic performer, dead 100 years, is still getting new fans is, to Toms, "just mind-boggling."

Denby describes Houdini as "the ultimate symbol of liberation over death, over imprisonment. What a guy! I mean, an amazing man!"

#318. The Political Upstarts That May Decide Israel’s Election

Less than a week before an election that many Israelis consider the most significant in the country’s history, the two leading candidates are deadlocked. On one side is Benjamin Netanyahu, battle-hardened, beset by corruption charges, and increasingly desperate. (Early this week, his campaign polled voters on whether they would be more likely to turn out if this were his last run for office.) On the other side is Gadi Eisenkot, a centrist former military chief who is temperate and broadly liked but lacks the political instincts to become a clear favorite.

On October 18th, Donald Trump tried to tip the balance. Breaking a long tradition in which American Presidents at least pretend not to intervene in foreign elections, he praised Netanyahu on Truth Social as a “Great Warrior Prime Minister.” He also suggested that he would abandon the war against Iran if Netanyahu lost: “If Israel has the wrong Leader, which would be anyone other than Bibi, America will have to do this work ourselves, and I am not inclined to do so.”

The LedeReporting and commentary on what you need to know today.

Trump remains hugely popular in Israel, and in a normal news cycle his endorsement would have given Netanyahu an electoral boost. Instead, it was cancelled out by one of the Prime Minister’s most vocal supporters. Yinon Magal, a right-wing television host, aired a segment in which he called Eisenkot a “monkey.” Many Israelis viewed this as a racist slur against Eisenkot, whose parents emigrated from Morocco. To make matters worse, Magal also suggested that Eisenkot was the puppet of Jews of European descent: “All the Ashkenazi élitists who are managing him stand behind him, telling him what to say.”

Yet, even with all the media frenzy, the polls have hardly budged. For weeks, Eisenkot’s party, Yashar (meaning “straight,” or “just”), has generally maintained a small lead over Netanyahu’s Likud, while both candidates remain far from securing a majority in the parliament. After Israeli elections, the candidate with the most support from legislators in the Knesset (typically the head of the largest party) has forty-two days to assemble a majority coalition. If that candidate fails, his rival gets a chance. In practice, this gives smaller parties disproportionate power in determining who becomes the Prime Minister.

The upcoming election may well be decided by two small parties that didn’t even exist a few months ago. They are strikingly different, but both are headed by hawkish politicians, and both seem designed to appeal to right-wing voters who hold Netanyahu’s government responsible for the attacks of October 7, 2023, but don’t feel prepared to join the opposition. Polls show that both parties are currently hovering around the threshold necessary to enter the parliament.

The first one, Amcha Yisrael (People of Israel), was formed in August by Ofer Winter, a former brigadier general in his mid-fifties. Winter, a stern nationalist who famously encouraged the “voluntary” migration of Palestinians from Gaza, represents a new generation of military leaders who grew up on Jewish settlements and belong to the Religious Zionist movement. For months, he seemed to have electoral momentum—but his numbers dipped recently, as he declared an unequivocal allegiance to Netanyahu. “Winter is an enigma,” Amnon Abramovich, a veteran commentator on Israel’s mainstream Channel 12, told me. “He entered politics because of October 7th, with a slate of candidates who were not in charge on October 7th, but he pledged to go only with the Prime Minister of October 7th.” Abramovich said that Winter’s supporters wish he hadn’t tied his political fate to Netanyahu: “If he were smart—and he is not smart—he would have vowed that he would go according to principles and not according to people.”

Winter’s support comes overwhelmingly from voters who previously cast ballots for Likud or one of its coalition parties, Abramovich said. After the catastrophe that befell Israel on the coalition’s watch, many of those voters would likely have stayed home this time if not for a candidate like Winter—which led many observers to suspect that Netanyahu had encouraged him to run in the first place. But as polls began to suggest that Winter’s party may not gain enough votes to enter the parliament—that he might be “wasting” right-wing ballots—Netanyahu embarked on a campaign to force him from the race. It recently emerged that Netanyahu had met with a rabbi close to Winter, hoping that he could persuade Winter to drop out. “Netanyahu is right to be fearful,” Abramovich said. “He sees a scenario in which Winter brings in two to three per cent and still fails to pass.”

The second party, announced just last month, is an alliance between a center-right faction called the New Economic Party, which is focussed on pocketbook issues, and another one, the Reservists, which is intent on making the military draft mandatory for everyone—including ultra-Orthodox men, who are currently exempt. The alliance’s leader, Yoaz Hendel, is more sophisticated than Winter, and more telegenic: in campaign appearances, he presents himself as part tech executive, part combat soldier. He served as a senior aide to Netanyahu and as a communications minister under both Netanyahu and Naftali Bennett. Since leaving Netanyahu’s office, he has become a prominent critic.

“We’re the protest vote,” Hendel told me by phone recently. He said that his target voters were Israelis under the age of forty: reserve-duty soldiers with young families who are buckling under the high cost of living and disillusioned with the government’s promises of security. “We want to convince everyone in the State of Israel not to vote for Netanyahu,” he said. “I think he is responsible for October 7th.” Still, Hendel was quick to add that he had no qualms about praising Netanyahu where he felt it was due, as in the case of the Israeli operation that blew up Hezbollah militants’ pagers in Lebanon and Syria two years ago.

The Reservists Party has attracted roughly equal support from the pro-Netanyahu and the anti-Netanyahu blocs. Abramovich calls it a “splits party”—“one leg here and one there.” The ruling coalition and its opposition have thus largely spared Hendel the barbed political attacks that they direct at their enemies.

But it is unclear how Hendel would align himself after the election. He does not endorse the opposition motto “Just not Bibi.” He told me, “I am concerned with how to establish a Zionist state. If [Netanyahu] insists on staying with ultra-Orthodox parties, I will not join him. By the same token, if Eisenkot joins Arab parties that do not denounce Hamas, then I am not there, either.” He is also unwilling to contribute to a minority government—an increasingly likely scenario, in which Eisenkot fails to secure a majority but still manages to form a coalition with the support of Arab parties. As Hendel sees it, “You put together a government like that and you’re out in about two weeks, while giving Netanyahu seventy seats in the next election.”

If either party succeeds in securing enough votes to enter the parliament, it will be tougher for Eisenkot to cobble together a majority. If both fail, Netanyahu may be unable to continue as Prime Minister. Given that polls in Israel can be off by as much as four per cent these days, there is no way of predicting the outcome.

Yet the new parties’ emergence, so close to election day, indicates a real reckoning. “They are both a response to the frustration of many voters with the polarized bloc voting that is common among the parties of yesterday,” Yagil Levy, a professor at Israel’s Open University, told me. They also underscore the outsized importance of military service in a country that has been at war for three years.

Yohanan Plesner, the president of the Israel Democracy Institute, a nonpartisan research center in Jerusalem, told me that Israelis remain so entrenched in “identity politics and tribalism that new political frameworks are needed in order to create any kind of movement.” Still, he is skeptical of Hendel’s attempt to provide a platform for disaffected voters of all kinds. “There are a ton of reservists, and only a fraction of them will vote for him,” he said. What his party seems to represent is a kind of “safe haven for right-wingers who are uncomfortable with the government’s polarizing approach, its surrender to ultra-Orthodox demands, and an over-all sense of incompetence.” Plesner noted that this leaves some crucial concerns unaddressed: “There’s no substantive debate about Israel’s security approach, no real maturity to have a genuine discussion about the Palestinians or the war.”

A poll released earlier this month shows few points of commonality on pressing political issues. There is overwhelming support among Jewish Israelis for establishing a state commission to investigate the events of October 7th, and for imposing the draft on the ultra-Orthodox. (The exemption has rallied Israelis of all political persuasions around the idea of “sharing the burden.”) There is far less pronounced agreement on restarting peace talks with the Palestinians; three-quarters of those surveyed rejected the idea. On all the rest, the country is split—often bitterly so. It seems astounding, in light of what has happened in Israel over the past three years, that what may end up tipping the election is a pair of niche parties that no one in the country had heard of until this summer. The two versions of Israel that could result are so divergent as to seem impossible to reconcile. ♦

By the numbers: Oil futures for delivery in 2027 — contracts to buy or sell crude next year — have climbed to their highest levels since the Iran war began.

  • Prices for January 2027 are up 17% since early September, to more than $97 a barrel.
  • Markets still anticipate some relief next year, but considerably less than they expected just weeks ago. It's a reversal from late summer, when hopes of easing tensions with Iran and a recovery in Middle Eastern oil supplies briefly pushed futures lower.
  • June futures have jumped 18%, to $88 a barrel, while December futures are up 13%, to about $81.

State of play: Crude oil exports from the Persian Gulf, including shipments through the Strait of Hormuz and alternative routes, surpassed prewar levels in the week ending Oct. 18, according to the Wall Street Journal. But the costly workarounds enabling that recovery have their limits.

  • Those include ship-to-ship transfers outside the Strait of Hormuz, where security risks have deterred some vessels.

Zoom in: Economist Robin Brooks argues that oil prices reflect higher shipping costs and fears of further attacks more than actual shortages.

  • Using earlier monthly data, he estimates that Persian Gulf crude exports had recovered to 91% of prewar levels, with a "temporary fear factor" adding at least $20 a barrel.

What to watch: The Trump administration is seeking to ease fuel prices ahead of the midterms, most recently by allowing more Russian diesel onto the market.

  • But relief may be minimal: Ukrainian attacks have damaged Russian refineries, while sanctions complicate repairs, limiting additional exports, Haines says.

The bottom line: "The market narrative has shifted from "transitory" to "new normal" right before our eyes," Bob Elliot, chief investment officer at Unlimited Funds, wrote Wednesday morning.

#322. Ken Paxton’s Anti-Abortion Influence Already Extended Beyond Texas. He Could Take It Further in the Senate.

Investigative Journalism in the Public Interest

How Abortion Bans Lead to Preventable Deaths

How Recent Arrivals at the Border Have Changed the Country and Its Attitudes

SCOTUS Justices’ Beneficial Relationships With Billionaire Donors

ProPublica is a nonprofit newsroom that investigates abuses of power. Sign up to receive our biggest stories as soon as they’re published.

Early in his tenure as Texas attorney general, Ken Paxton issued a statement commemorating what he believed was a national tragedy.

“It’s impossible to fill the void of more than 50 million missing human beings,” he said in 2016. He was marking the 43rd anniversary of Roe v. Wade, the Supreme Court ruling that protected abortion rights — and had long stood in the way of efforts to ban terminations in early pregnancy, when most abortions occur.

Paxton spent the years that followed testing Roe’s limits and ultimately prevailed where others had failed: In 2021, he defended a near-total abortion ban that Texas legislators had designed to evade conventional legal challenges. The U.S. Supreme Court allowed it to take effect while Roe remained in force.

After the justices overturned Roe the following year, Paxton kept pushing, pursuing new ways to extend the reach of Texas’ restrictions. He prevailed against the Biden administration, picked fights with states that protect abortion access and pioneered strategies that his peers later adopted or expanded.

While Paxton’s tenure as attorney general has drawn criticism from across the political spectrum for his personal scandals and alleged misuse of the office, detractors and allies alike agree that his signal achievement as the state’s top legal official has been advancing restrictions on abortion.

A close look at his record shows a clear pattern in his successes: He relentlessly tests the limits of the law and employs novel legal strategies to advance his goals.

Paxton’s national role as an anti-abortion warrior would change if he wins his U.S. Senate race. As Texas attorney general, he has been able to act largely on his own authority. If he prevails in November, he would become one of 100 senators in a chamber where most legislation needs 60 votes to overcome a filibuster.

But allies who have witnessed his victories see an opening.

“He’ll have the ability to use some of the laws that he’s defended in Texas as influence to draft legislation in the U.S. Senate,” said Mary Elizabeth Castle, director of government relations at Texas Values, a Christian advocacy group that supports a national abortion ban.

Legal experts and advocates on both sides expect he could join colleagues who are pressuring the Food and Drug Administration to revoke its approval of the abortion drug mifepristone.

Anti-abortion activists at the state and federal level are also trying to secure legal rights for fetuses, which could have implications for reproductive care like birth control and in vitro fertilization. Paxton, who has called himself a strong supporter of IVF, recently said he was open to restrictions intended to prevent the loss of fertilized eggs.

His litigation has also raised questions that experts expect the Supreme Court to confront, including whether a federal law, which mandates hospitals provide stabilizing care to patients having an emergency, overrides state abortion bans; whether to treat a fetus as a separate patient that has its own emergency care rights; and how far states can go in pursuing out-of-state doctors who prescribe abortion medication to patients in states where it’s banned.

During the current race, abortion has played a far smaller role than questions about Paxton’s ethics. His Democratic opponent, James Talarico, has said that he supports restoring a federal right to abortion and that his Christian faith calls for an end to “extreme” laws that Paxton has defended and that have forced women to flee the state for emergency abortion care.

Lately, Paxton has said little about abortion — an issue on which he has defended restrictions more stringent than many Texans say they want.

But those who have watched Paxton extend the reach of Texas law beyond its borders are paying close attention to his Senate bid.

Dr. Jennifer Villavicencio, an abortion provider, said she has felt compelled to move her family twice to states with strong laws shielding doctors from out-of-state litigation, partly out of concern that Paxton is creating a blueprint for targeting providers like her.

“It’s very disheartening and scary to think that it’s maybe coming to the U.S. Senate,” she said.

When Paxton became Texas attorney general in 2015, there was no ambiguity about where he stood on abortion.

Raised by an anti-abortion activist, he married his sweetheart from a Christian college, who spoke proudly about the fact that she was adopted, saying her birth mother “chose life.” She campaigned for Paxton, but he had his own bona fides. A law he’d co-sponsored as a state senator banned almost all abortions after 20 weeks.

Similar 20-week bans had taken root across the country as decades of court rulings chipped away at Roe, but no state had managed to enforce a ban as early as six weeks. More than a dozen tried, passing laws that prohibited abortion when cardiac activity could be detected. One after another, they were blocked by judges.

Then, in 2021, a Texas lawyer named Jonathan Mitchell worked with legislators to craft a law that would withstand similar challenges. Instead of having state officials enforce their “heartbeat law,” the new act, referred to as Senate Bill 8, allowed citizens to sue physicians they suspected were providing abortions. The law offered up to $10,000 if they won.

When abortion providers and President Joe Biden’s administration separately challenged the law in federal court, Paxton’s office argued that state officials couldn’t be ordered to stop enforcing it because they had no power to do so in the first place. Only private citizens could.

“Because the executive officials are expressly prohibited from enforcing SB 8,” his office wrote to the Supreme Court, “there is nothing for a federal court to enjoin.”

The Supreme Court agreed that Paxton couldn’t be sued because “the petitioners have not identified any enforcement authority the attorney general possesses in connection with S. B. 8 that a federal court might enjoin him from exercising.”

The law remained in effect, becoming the first enforceable six-week ban under Roe.

Legislators in Idaho and Oklahoma subsequently passed similar laws.

For Dr. Sam Dickman, who worked at a San Antonio Planned Parenthood, the implications were clear: “I was going to need to either entirely change the type of medicine I was providing or leave the state,” he said. He opted for the latter, moving to Montana, where abortion is legal until viability, or roughly 24 weeks.

Days after the Texas ban took effect, Josseli Barnica arrived at a Houston hospital in the midst of a miscarriage. She was 17 weeks pregnant and her fetus’s head was pressed up against her dilated cervix. Because the situation put her at high risk of developing an infection, medical experts said doctors should have offered to speed up delivery or empty her uterus; the fetus was not viable, and losing the pregnancy was inevitable.

Barnica relayed to her husband what she said her medical providers had told her: “They had to wait until there was no heartbeat,” he told ProPublica in Spanish. “It would be a crime to give her an abortion.”

For 40 hours, Barnica waited, her uterus exposed to bacteria.

She died days later of sepsis. Medical experts told ProPublica her doctors did not follow the proper standard of care and that Barnica’s death was preventable.

In a statement, HCA Healthcare, the hospital chain that treated her, said “our responsibility is to be in compliance with applicable state and federal laws and regulations” and said that physicians exercise their independent judgment.

When the Supreme Court overturned the constitutional right to abortion on June 24, 2022, Paxton dismissed his staff at noon to celebrate. He would later designate the date as a paid office holiday, Sanctity of Life Day.

The Texas Legislature had prepared for the occasion, passing a law that went into effect shortly after the overturning of Roe. The ban threatened doctors with up to 99 years in prison if they performed an abortion. But soon came what Paxton framed as a federal attempt to thwart the state law.

The Biden administration issued a memo to doctors in states with abortion bans saying that a federal emergency care law protected their medical judgment, “regardless of the restrictions in the state.”

The federal Health and Human Services secretary at the time said doctors had an obligation to follow the Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act, better known as EMTALA. The law requires hospitals that accept federal funding to screen and stabilize every patient who arrives.

For those with complications such as severe preeclampsia, a potentially fatal, pregnancy-related blood pressure disorder, stabilizing a patient might require performing an abortion, even if the state forbids it, the guidance said. “The physician must provide that treatment,” it said, bolding the word “must.”

Paxton sued, accusing the administration of an attempt to use federal law “to transform every emergency room in the country into a walk-in abortion clinic.”

He argued EMTALA was created to make sure hospitals didn’t dump uninsured patients back on the street without treatment, not to override state laws. He took the argument a step further, suggesting that under EMTALA, doctors might need to consider “the life of the unborn child.”

The argument was novel, six legal experts told ProPublica.

“He’s basically suing the federal government to protect Texas’ right to force pregnant women to not get abortions, even when their health is threatened,” said Greer Donley, a law professor at the University of Pittsburgh. “I remember thinking, ‘Wow, that’s radical.’”

Medical groups warned patients would die.

But a federal judge sided with Paxton that August, and the 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the decision in January 2024.

The ruling did more than keep the administration from enforcing its EMTALA interpretation in Texas.

It barred the federal government from pursuing cases against members of two anti-abortion medical organizations that had joined Paxton’s suit as plaintiffs, the American Association of Pro-Life Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the Christian Medical & Dental Associations. This effectively exempted them from having to follow the federal guidance, regardless of where they worked.

It also served as immediate litigation fodder for another state fighting to enforce its own abortion ban.

Idaho had not had the same success in court as Texas. After the Biden administration sued, arguing the state’s ban conflicted with EMTALA, Idaho lost in lower and appellate courts, leaving its ban effectively frozen in emergency cases, with doctors instructed to follow the federal guidance.

But Paxton’s 5th Circuit win on Jan. 2, 2024, gave Idaho’s ban a lifeline. The ruling conflicted with the 9th Circuit’s decision in Idaho, creating the kind of disagreement between federal appeals courts that the Supreme Court often steps in to resolve.

The following day, attorneys representing Idaho asked the high court to do just that, saying Paxton’s success showed they were “likely to prevail.” In the meantime, they asked the court to unfreeze the ban.

On Jan. 5, the Supreme Court allowed Idaho to fully enforce its ban while the case proceeded.

Emergency doctors began hesitating over when they could legally provide abortions, Dr. Jim Souza, chief physician at St. Luke’s Health System, said on a press call at the time, according to NPR. “Is she septic enough for me to do this abortion and not risk going to jail and losing my license?”

In the three months that followed, St. Luke’s, the state’s largest hospital system, said it had to airlift six patients out of the state for abortions.

In Texas, ProPublica has documented the consequences for women experiencing pregnancy emergencies.

When Tierra Walker sought care later that year in San Antonio, she developed preeclampsia and ultimately died from it, ProPublica previously reported. None of the 90 physicians involved in her care offered her the option of terminating her pregnancy, even after she asked if doing so might keep her safe.

Her family sued Paxton in September, along with state medical officials and several healthcare providers, alleging that Texas’ abortion restrictions and the care Walker received led to her death. Paxton has not responded to the suit and did not respond to a request seeking comment.

ProPublica has identified at least two other pregnant Texas women who died without what experts said was appropriate emergency care. Paxton has not publicly commented on any of their deaths and did not respond to ProPublica’s questions about them. Our analysis also found worsening outcomes across the state for patients experiencing pregnancy complications.

The reach of Paxton’s legal victory may not end there.

The Supreme Court still hasn’t resolved the conflict between the circuit rulings. It declined to do so in the Idaho case, and after President Donald Trump took office, his administration dropped the suit and rescinded the EMTALA guidance.

But legal experts told ProPublica the Supreme Court has yet to resolve core questions that Paxton’s litigation raised: whether EMTALA preempts state abortion laws and whether a fetus also has a right to emergency treatment that could conflict with the mother’s interests in cases where her health is at risk.

To guard against future rulings, a reproductive rights advocacy group called All* Above All is pushing state-level legislation to safeguard emergency care. “We’re seeing the chaos right now and the confusion,” said its president, Nourbese Flint.

On Dec. 7, 2023, a state judge in Austin issued a ruling that Paxton immediately moved to stop.

A 31-year-old mother named Kate Cox wanted permission to get an abortion. She was 20 weeks pregnant and carrying a fetus diagnosed with trisomy 18, a genetic condition that kills the vast majority of babies born with it.

Texas doesn’t allow abortions for fatal fetal anomalies, but Cox’s doctor argued that if she was forced to give birth, it could imperil her ability to get pregnant again. It was also medically risky: Cox had already had two children by cesarean section, and the chances of complications increase with each procedure.

The day a Travis County district court judge granted her request, Paxton took a step legal experts told ProPublica they had not seen another state attorney general take:

He sent letters to three hospitals where Cox’s doctor had admitting privileges, threatening legal action if the doctor performed the abortion a judge said she could have. The court order, he wrote, did not insulate hospitals, doctors or anyone else from civil and criminal liability for violating Texas’ abortion laws.

At the same time, he appealed the case to the Texas Supreme Court, saying that Cox did not face the kind of threat to her life that fell under the law’s exception. If legislators had wanted to write an exception for fatal fetal anomalies, he said, they would have done so.

“The Texas Legislature did not intend for courts to become revolving doors of permission slips to obtain abortions,” he said.

The Texas Supreme Court agreed with Paxton and reversed the lower court’s ruling. By that time, Cox had sought an abortion out of state.

“It was incredibly scary,” Cox told ProPublica. “We saw in my case that Texas will go after doctors.”

Paxton was also the first — and, so far, the only — attorney general to criminally prosecute a provider in his state for violating an abortion ban.

According to court documents, investigators got an anonymous tip alleging that abortions were being performed at a medical clinic that catered to low-income, Spanish-speaking patients, managed by Maria Rojas, a licensed midwife.

Rojas was arrested and charged with practicing medicine without a license and violating the state’s abortion ban. Paxton also moved to temporarily shut down the clinic and others she ran while the case progressed through court. Months after her arrest, Paxton announced he had arrested and indicted eight other clinic staffers for practicing medicine without a license.

Rojas has pleaded not guilty to the charges. Her attorneys have accused Paxton’s office of a shoddy investigation and argued that at least one case prosecutors were treating as an abortion was actually the management of a patient’s miscarriage.

A trial has not yet been scheduled, but an appeals court this summer overturned the order shuttering the clinics, finding the state had not presented sufficient admissible evidence to justify a temporary injunction. Paxton said he is appealing the ruling. The court did not rule on whether Rojas had actually performed illegal abortions.

Paxton has not stopped at the state border in his pursuit of doctors.

In December 2024, he sued Dr. Margaret Carpenter, a New York-based physician, accusing her of violating multiple Texas laws when she treated a patient via telehealth who lived in Plano, a suburb north of Dallas, and prescribed her abortion medication.

Because the patient was based in Texas, he argued, Carpenter was subject to laws there that banned abortion and required a license to practice in the state.

Carpenter didn’t respond to the suit, so Paxton won by default. But Texas was unable to collect on more than $100,000 in civil fines and court fees that resulted; a New York county clerk refused to register the judgment, invoking a “shield law” that protects providers of abortion care that is legal in New York from certain out-of-state investigations, prosecutions and judgments. At least 22 states have passed shield laws since Roe was overturned, and New York’s is among the strongest. Carpenter did not respond to a request seeking comment and has not publicly responded to the case.

A New York judge dismissed Paxton’s case seeking to enforce the judgment, but Paxton didn’t give up, filing two more civil suits this year against practitioners and organizations in Delaware and California that mail abortion medication to Texas.

Three other state attorneys general have now expanded on the strategy. Last month, Louisiana Attorney General Liz Murrill — who brought criminal charges against Carpenter after Paxton sued her (and has faced similar obstacles in New York) — joined the attorneys general of Arkansas and Alabama in filing a sweeping federal civil suit against out-of-state providers of abortion pills. The case names some of the same providers Paxton pursued; they have challenged the suits. The three attorneys general filed a separate action directly with the Supreme Court asking it to strike down shield laws in New York, Massachusetts and California.

Paxton has moved beyond doctors to target other entities that help patients obtain abortions. Mary Ziegler, an abortion historian and professor of law at University of California, Davis, called him “an innovator in terms of broadening the network of people who were seen as culprits or aiders or abettors.”

He sent cease-and-desist letters to organizations that ship abortion medication into Texas; the attorney general of Alabama subsequently used similar tactics. And Paxton sued cities in his state to stop them from disbursing funds to help women travel for abortions — a move the Legislature followed with a law banning such funding.

Following his legal victory against the city of San Antonio early this year, in which it was forced to end the $100,000 program, Paxton made a vow:

“I will always do everything in my power to prevent radicals from manipulating the system to murder innocent babies.”

It barred the federal government from pursuing cases against members of two anti-abortion medical organizations that had joined Paxton’s suit as plaintiffs, the American Association of Pro-Life Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the Christian Medical & Dental Associations. This effectively exempted them from having to follow the federal guidance, regardless of where they worked.

It also served as immediate litigation fodder for another state fighting to enforce its own abortion ban.

Idaho had not had the same success in court as Texas. After the Biden administration sued, arguing the state’s ban conflicted with EMTALA, Idaho lost in lower and appellate courts, leaving its ban effectively frozen in emergency cases, with doctors instructed to follow the federal guidance.

But Paxton’s 5th Circuit win on Jan. 2, 2024, gave Idaho’s ban a lifeline. The ruling conflicted with the 9th Circuit’s decision in Idaho, creating the kind of disagreement between federal appeals courts that the Supreme Court often steps in to resolve.

The following day, attorneys representing Idaho asked the high court to do just that, saying Paxton’s success showed they were “likely to prevail.” In the meantime, they asked the court to unfreeze the ban.

On Jan. 5, the Supreme Court allowed Idaho to fully enforce its ban while the case proceeded.

Emergency doctors began hesitating over when they could legally provide abortions, Dr. Jim Souza, chief physician at St. Luke’s Health System, said on a press call at the time, according to NPR. “Is she septic enough for me to do this abortion and not risk going to jail and losing my license?”

In the three months that followed, St. Luke’s, the state’s largest hospital system, said it had to airlift six patients out of the state for abortions.

In Texas, ProPublica has documented the consequences for women experiencing pregnancy emergencies.

When Tierra Walker sought care later that year in San Antonio, she developed preeclampsia and ultimately died from it, ProPublica previously reported. None of the 90 physicians involved in her care offered her the option of terminating her pregnancy, even after she asked if doing so might keep her safe.

Her family sued Paxton in September, along with state medical officials and several healthcare providers, alleging that Texas’ abortion restrictions and the care Walker received led to her death. Paxton has not responded to the suit and did not respond to a request seeking comment.

ProPublica has identified at least two other pregnant Texas women who died without what experts said was appropriate emergency care. Paxton has not publicly commented on any of their deaths and did not respond to ProPublica’s questions about them. Our analysis also found worsening outcomes across the state for patients experiencing pregnancy complications.

The reach of Paxton’s legal victory may not end there.

The Supreme Court still hasn’t resolved the conflict between the circuit rulings. It declined to do so in the Idaho case, and after President Donald Trump took office, his administration dropped the suit and rescinded the EMTALA guidance.

But legal experts told ProPublica the Supreme Court has yet to resolve core questions that Paxton’s litigation raised: whether EMTALA preempts state abortion laws and whether a fetus also has a right to emergency treatment that could conflict with the mother’s interests in cases where her health is at risk.

To guard against future rulings, a reproductive rights advocacy group called All* Above All is pushing state-level legislation to safeguard emergency care. “We’re seeing the chaos right now and the confusion,” said its president, Nourbese Flint.

On Dec. 7, 2023, a state judge in Austin issued a ruling that Paxton immediately moved to stop.

A 31-year-old mother named Kate Cox wanted permission to get an abortion. She was 20 weeks pregnant and carrying a fetus diagnosed with trisomy 18, a genetic condition that kills the vast majority of babies born with it.

Texas doesn’t allow abortions for fatal fetal anomalies, but Cox’s doctor argued that if she was forced to give birth, it could imperil her ability to get pregnant again. It was also medically risky: Cox had already had two children by cesarean section, and the chances of complications increase with each procedure.

The day a Travis County district court judge granted her request, Paxton took a step legal experts told ProPublica they had not seen another state attorney general take:

He sent letters to three hospitals where Cox’s doctor had admitting privileges, threatening legal action if the doctor performed the abortion a judge said she could have. The court order, he wrote, did not insulate hospitals, doctors or anyone else from civil and criminal liability for violating Texas’ abortion laws.

At the same time, he appealed the case to the Texas Supreme Court, saying that Cox did not face the kind of threat to her life that fell under the law’s exception. If legislators had wanted to write an exception for fatal fetal anomalies, he said, they would have done so.

“The Texas Legislature did not intend for courts to become revolving doors of permission slips to obtain abortions,” he said.

The Texas Supreme Court agreed with Paxton and reversed the lower court’s ruling. By that time, Cox had sought an abortion out of state.

“It was incredibly scary,” Cox told ProPublica. “We saw in my case that Texas will go after doctors.”

Paxton was also the first — and, so far, the only — attorney general to criminally prosecute a provider in his state for violating an abortion ban.

According to court documents, investigators got an anonymous tip alleging that abortions were being performed at a medical clinic that catered to low-income, Spanish-speaking patients, managed by Maria Rojas, a licensed midwife.

Rojas was arrested and charged with practicing medicine without a license and violating the state’s abortion ban. Paxton also moved to temporarily shut down the clinic and others she ran while the case progressed through court. Months after her arrest, Paxton announced he had arrested and indicted eight other clinic staffers for practicing medicine without a license.

Rojas has pleaded not guilty to the charges. Her attorneys have accused Paxton’s office of a shoddy investigation and argued that at least one case prosecutors were treating as an abortion was actually the management of a patient’s miscarriage.

A trial has not yet been scheduled, but an appeals court this summer overturned the order shuttering the clinics, finding the state had not presented sufficient admissible evidence to justify a temporary injunction. Paxton said he is appealing the ruling. The court did not rule on whether Rojas had actually performed illegal abortions.

Paxton has not stopped at the state border in his pursuit of doctors.

In December 2024, he sued Dr. Margaret Carpenter, a New York-based physician, accusing her of violating multiple Texas laws when she treated a patient via telehealth who lived in Plano, a suburb north of Dallas, and prescribed her abortion medication.

Because the patient was based in Texas, he argued, Carpenter was subject to laws there that banned abortion and required a license to practice in the state.

Carpenter didn’t respond to the suit, so Paxton won by default. But Texas was unable to collect on more than $100,000 in civil fines and court fees that resulted; a New York county clerk refused to register the judgment, invoking a “shield law” that protects providers of abortion care that is legal in New York from certain out-of-state investigations, prosecutions and judgments. At least 22 states have passed shield laws since Roe was overturned, and New York’s is among the strongest. Carpenter did not respond to a request seeking comment and has not publicly responded to the case.

A New York judge dismissed Paxton’s case seeking to enforce the judgment, but Paxton didn’t give up, filing two more civil suits this year against practitioners and organizations in Delaware and California that mail abortion medication to Texas.

Three other state attorneys general have now expanded on the strategy. Last month, Louisiana Attorney General Liz Murrill — who brought criminal charges against Carpenter after Paxton sued her (and has faced similar obstacles in New York) — joined the attorneys general of Arkansas and Alabama in filing a sweeping federal civil suit against out-of-state providers of abortion pills. The case names some of the same providers Paxton pursued; they have challenged the suits. The three attorneys general filed a separate action directly with the Supreme Court asking it to strike down shield laws in New York, Massachusetts and California.

Paxton has moved beyond doctors to target other entities that help patients obtain abortions. Dug a nail through the end and peeled its yellow skin off. It came away in strips, three thick strips. I looked for bruises. I can’t stand eating a banana with bruises and was glad to see there were none. I peeled another one for my brother. “We don’t have much time,” my dad said, hurrying us out the door.

He had a great smile, a mouth full of even teeth, and a dimple in each cheek. He was wearing his work clothes, not his nice ones. He wore nice clothes no matter the occasion, except when he went to work. None of the wool suits at the shops around here fit him, so he would buy the fabric at a store and take it to the one tailor he knew. That way, the shoulder stitching wouldn’t hang below his shoulder and his pants wouldn’t drag on the ground. You couldn’t tell he was short.

The tailor worked out of his home, not far from where we lived. My dad’s pieces were side projects the tailor took on for extra cash. He worked on them when he had time. There was never a finish date set, and my dad wasn’t pushy about it. My dad wasn’t tall, but he was handsome, especially when he wore his suits. He wore them to weddings, funerals, any kind of party or place with a dance floor. And he loved to dance. With Mom, especially. They were the first ones on the dance floor at a wedding. My dad always made sure that anyone who wanted to dance would have a chance to with him.

Whenever my dad went to drop off fabric at the tailor’s or received a call letting him know that the pieces were finished, we’d go with him, to give Mom a break. We’d wait in the car outside the tailor’s. It wasn’t long. Twenty minutes, maybe. He said, “That man doesn’t like kids.” Even if my father was angry or disagreed with someone, he smiled. Just smiled through it all.

My father worked somewhere that had a lot of cardboard boxes. We had a lot of cardboard around our place. We lived in a basement with two bedrooms. My father taped cardboard to all our windows so that no one could see us from the outside, but that also meant that we couldn’t see out from the inside. Still, he kept the cardboard taped to the windows because, he said, “It’s cheaper than curtains. More privacy, too.” My father also cut out a small square of cardboard for us to put our winter boots on. He said, “This way we won’t ruin the floor in the entryway.” I don’t know why he worried about that. It wasn’t like he owned the place. “Doesn’t matter. What if we move out, and someone else comes to live here? We don’t want them to think we’re pigs,” he said.

My brother and I shared one bedroom. He took the top bunk, and I took the bottom. He was two years younger than me, but that difference wasn’t a big deal. We talked about all our friends at school, and their parents, and our parents. “Did you see her dad? I thought he was her grandfather because he was sooooo oooold!” We were proud of how young our parents looked. They were often mistaken for teen-agers at the high school just down the hill from our school. And the guys in sixth grade would say to our mom, “What’s uuup, girl? You new here?” She’d smile, and me or my brother would say, “That’s our mom!” They’d apologize and ask, “Why’s she young?”

At night, there was always some noise outside that I couldn’t recognize, and it would take me a while to make up something to explain it away before I could calm down and go to sleep. I was a child who lived in my own mind and scared myself, and had a hard time talking myself out of it. Once, I thought I heard gunshots outside, but it was firecrackers going off. It was the Fourth of July. I thought there was a fire outside when what I was smelling was a car’s exhaust. A woman screaming at night turned out to be the blaring siren of an ambulance. For the longest time, there was this pretty, ethereal sound, as if a fairy were about to appear before me, but the fairy never appeared. I couldn’t sleep. When we walked to school, my brother showed me what it was. “Look, it’s that fairy music you hear,” he said, pointing to a porch with a small wind chime hanging from the ceiling.

My brother was really sensitive and noticed things that I didn’t. “Did you see Ryan at school today? He didn’t have anything to eat, and his mom doesn’t pick him up anymore. It’s a woman, but it’s not his mom. And Miss Stevens got a perm,” he said. “Someone called her Miss Curly in class, and everyone laughed. It must be hard to be a teacher. To go to school for so many years and know all that stuff and then come teach us so we know it, too.”

Our dad said that he never got to go to school like us. In Laos, the war came, and that was it. At least he knew how to read people, he said. “I just poo-poo paa-paa my way around until something makes sense to them. That’s how English works.” He smiled, like someone who was about to be hit but thought he could stop the blow if he shrank into himself and made like he was so tiny he wasn’t worth anything. Our mom didn’t know how to read and would tell us to take our questions to our dad. When she was still living with us, she’d grab the side of her head and tell us it hurt, and then she would go into their bedroom and close the door. Because of the cardboard, our place was always so dark.

When I think about it, other people were always trying to break up our parents. There was the woman on the sixth floor, who often forgot her keys and would press our buzzer to get Dad to go out and open the door for her. She knew that our intercom didn’t work properly and that the superintendent hadn’t come around to fix it. So she’d buzz, and Dad would have to go out there and let her into the building, and they’d talk in the hallway. I don’t know what they talked about, but Dad would come back and Mom would be so angry. He’d do his three smiles for her. He’d start with a smile that made his dimples sink in, then his lips would turn tight, as if he were pretending to smile, and then he’d flash a more relaxed smile where the wrinkles in his forehead didn’t show. He’d say he was ugly and no one wanted him, let alone wanted him enough to steal him away. “What for, to be a pet?”

It wasn’t the same when it came to Mom. Any man Mom talked to, our dad said, was a man trying to steal her. So it was our job to watch out for these men and make sure they didn’t come near our mom. “She’s married,” I would say if a man sat next to her on the bus. Me and my brother would squeeze in between to let him know she had children.

One time, at a park on a hot summer day, an old man came out of nowhere and sat on the blanket with our mother. He had hair the color of my pee, and there were so few hairs on his head I could count them all. I’d never seen him before, and I didn’t know who he was. He was the type of person who called my mom a lotus flower or yelled “Ten dollars! Ten dollars!” at her. People like that were always telling us to go home. I don’t know why. It was just a basement. We didn’t even have a balcony.

I tapped my brother on the shoulder and showed him that I had some stones in my hand, and I turned my chin toward the man with my mom. My brother understood. We crept close and hid behind a tree stump that was half poking out of the ground, and we threw the stones at him. We were nice about it at first, using small ones that mostly hit him on the arm. He laughed and kept on trying to talk to her. After that, we knew we had to do greater damage.

I signalled like a soldier to my brother to try a spot on his face. He said, “I don’t want to. He’s just a man.” I did it myself—picked up some more stones, heavier ones, and threw them. They whizzed by the man’s head. Then I picked up another one, and it hit the man on the mouth. My mom looked at us, and then back at the man, and laughed. The man got up off the blanket and shouted at me, “Fucking kids!” I stared back at him and took my index finger and slid it across my neck like it was a knife. I yelled “Fucking old man!” and stuck out my flat chest. My brother slunk away and hid inside an orange tube slide.

We didn’t tell our dad. He wouldn’t have been able to get past the fact that some man had come to talk to our mother, so I couldn’t even tell him how I’d protected her. Some things you just do and you know you did right. You don’t have to wait for someone to tell you.

I didn’t know what homework was. And since my parents had never gone to school themselves, they never asked me about it. I thought that because we spent all day at school sitting at a desk—working—when I got home my evening was supposed to be like Dad’s. It was supposed to be for rest, for watching television as long as my eyes would stay open. I brought schoolwork home with me, but I just left it in a plastic bag by the door. The science project, though, was a big thing. Our teacher had talked about it since the first day, and we were supposed to work on it all year. I’d forgotten about it after Mom left, and now there were only three weeks until this big show. There would be prizes. I’d never won anything before, and my brother said, “Dad can make something easy. First place, too, I bet,” so I told Dad and we scrambled something together two nights before it was due.

He took a piece of cardboard from the corner of our kitchen window and asked me to cut it into small strips, the size of Popsicle sticks. We needed more sticks, he said. The more cardboard we took from the window, the more light we let in, and the more we let people see us. But once we’d got about a hundred of those sticks he began building something. It looked like a house, but not like the houses here. It was on stilts. He said he’d lived in a house like that before we came here. “Now I’m a refugee,” he said. I didn’t know what a refugee was, and neither did my brother, but I thought that since my dad had said he was one that must mean that we were, too, like the way we carry our last name. Whether we wanted that name or not, it was just there, telling everyone where we came from and that we were related.

The thing my father made was tiny and beautiful and so delicate and tender. If it weren’t for the glue, I was pretty sure the whole thing would have fallen apart real easy. I didn’t want to take it to school and have it on display. It was so obvious that I hadn’t built that thing. This was an adult’s hand at work. All the same, I was proud of my dad and what he’d made, and I wanted everyone to see it.

When I brought my dad’s little house into the classroom and placed it on top of my desk, a crowd formed quickly. Everyone was impressed. There were doubts, even accusations already that I hadn’t made it. I didn’t defend myself. I just stayed quiet. I looked around at the other projects. A bowl of water. If you dropped ice cubes into it and then dropped a thread with a bit of salt in, it would melt the ice and the thread would sink into it. A kid held up a thread, and an ice cube dangled from the end of it. Someone else had made an eyeball. It had labels, explaining what each part was called and how it all worked—what happened when a part got damaged and a person couldn’t see. What it means when we wear glasses. I thought that was interesting. Then, there was this brown thing that looked like a pile of shit. I really did not know what it was. It was gigantic and took up a whole table all by itself. Smoke came out of a hole. When someone poured liquid into that hole, you could hear something rumbling and foaming. It was impressive. The moment I saw that thing, I knew that the competition was over and it would win. By the time school ended for the day, the prizes had been awarded. First, second, third, and honorable mention.

I walked home with the thing my dad had made and left it in the living room. I should have just trashed it, but I didn’t have the heart. I knew it was something you didn’t destroy. So I just brought it home.

When my dad came home from work, he came running toward me, excited, and he asked me what we’d won. He was so certain and confident.

I told him that what he’d made didn’t win anything.

I don’t know why it meant so much to him, but my dad began to cry then. His shoulders dropped, and his arms hung by his side. He looked like he was smiling, but he was crying. Tears wet his whole face, even though his eyes were closed. My brother cried, too. He cries at just about everything, and the sight of my father crying—well, it didn’t matter why. He was done for.

I didn’t know what to do for my father then. It didn’t seem right to tell him that things would be fine. It didn’t seem right to tell him there was always next year. It was just that he’d never been to school. He didn’t know you could work so hard and no matter what you did, it wouldn’t be good enough. He didn’t know that once you began to understand something, something else would come along and you’d have to learn to understand it all over again.

He turned to me after he was done crying, wiping his nose and tears. Who won? he asked. He wanted to know. I told him that I couldn’t remember. And it really didn’t matter. What mattered were the hours he’d put into making his house. Making something beautiful that no one had ever seen before. And I don’t know why I said this or where it came from or what I meant exactly, but I said, “Sometimes the winner doesn’t win.”

I didn’t want my father to know that something called a “wall cano” that looked like a pile of shit with foam coming out of its hole had won first place. Or that an eyeball thing had won second. Or that a stupid trick with ice cubes had won third. I just knew not to bring my schoolwork home and have my dad help me ever again for as long as I lived. I would go it alone. The man just wasn’t cut out for that stuff.

Not long after this, I thought I saw my mom across the street from my school. I felt something bright draw my eyes, and when I looked a woman was standing there, but I wasn’t sure if it was her. She had messy hair, and her clothes looked too big. I told my dad that night. He smiled at me without showing any of his teeth and pretended that I hadn’t said anything, and told me to finish my dinner. I tried to talk to my brother about it when we were alone in our room. I thought maybe he could tell me if it was her, and we’d talk about where she was or might be. But he also got quiet, tried a few words, and then disappeared into the dark. He pretended that I hadn’t said anything, too. People become who they are, and I think that’s a moment when my brother became who he is now.

I am not ten years old anymore, but I still have the same feelings at thirty-seven. Once, I was visiting a friend when she put up a finger like she’d heard a sound from the ceiling, but that was her way of telling me to pause our conversation. She leaned back in her chair and called out to her ten-year-old to remind him about his book report. He said he’d done it already, and he brought the report over for her to read, to look for spelling mistakes, to check for clarity. She reminded him what to aim for. A clear beginning, a middle, and an end. My friend had gone all the way in school. She’d finished and taken all the degrees. When I was ten, I did not have her, someone like her. I had to learn all that by myself. Out of order, probably. Middle, beginning, end. End, beginning, middle. End. What is an end, and when you think you have one, where does it go, exactly, when there is no one to tell you? ♦

A few months into President Donald Trump’s second term, political appointees at the Department of Homeland Security made an unusual request of its agency focused on cybersecurity: Secretly acquire a voting machine and hack it.

The appointees wanted the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency to procure the machine, examine it and report back the findings so they could be given to the White House, according to two former administration officials with knowledge of the request.

The proposition was shocking to CISA’s career staffers, according to one of the former officials. CISA typically worked to thwart hackers. Moreover, the agency had been directed not to alert the voting machine company if it uncovered flaws, contrary to its standard practices. The request made the CISA employees fear they were being asked to take part in political interference.

“My thought was they could keep it in their back pocket to invalidate any [election] results they don’t like,” one of the former officials said. “This goes against everything CISA represents.”

The CISA employees repeatedly told the political appointees that their request was impossible, in part because the agents capable of pulling off such an operation had just been laid off by Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency initiative. The appointees eventually stopped pressing CISA.

Though not acted on, the request shows how the Trump administration has tried to use the agency for its own ends while draining it of resources for one of its critical missions: protecting the nation’s voting systems.

In Trump’s second term, his administration has laid off or pushed out more than 1,000 CISA employees, shrinking the agency by about one-third. The president’s 2027 budget proposal would reduce funding for the agency by another $700 million, which could necessitate 900 additional layoffs.

In response, Democratic lawmakers have called for an investigation into CISA’s ability to do its job and demanded answers as to why federal funds are not being used to support election security as directed by Congress.

Since Trump signed the CISA Act in 2018, the agency has worked with state authorities and voting machine vendors to help protect election infrastructure from cyberattacks. During Trump’s second term, though, CISA has abandoned much of this work. Several state election authorities said the agency provided them no assistance — and no explanation for its absence — during the most critical planning stages for the upcoming midterms.

“There is a difference between deprioritizing and being nonexistent,” said Ryan Macias, an election technology and cybersecurity expert who has worked as a consultant for CISA. In 2025 and through mid-September 2026 — when the bulk of preparations for the election typically would have been completed — “they were nonexistent. Literally nonexistent.”

While Trump has repeatedly warned that America’s elections are vulnerable to hacking, in some cases repeating disproven claims, experts said his administration is undermining the federal government’s role in helping secure them.

Though CISA re-emerged in September with a last-minute security plan for the election, some state officials told ProPublica they fear that what is left of the agency has been compromised by a conspiracy-obsessed presidential administration. In recent months, in reviving debunked theories about the 2020 election, the Trump administration has declassified troves of records, including sensitive material that state officials provided to CISA. Some state authorities said they no longer trusted the administration enough to share information with CISA, a key to the agency’s success in past elections.

“They’ve violated the entire premise of that information-sharing relationship,” said one former high-ranking federal cybersecurity official who now advises state governments on national security. “There’s no fucking way they’re ever going to give any information to CISA. And it’s unclear if they ever will in the future.”

A spokesperson for the White House declined an interview request but defended Trump’s handling of election security in an emailed statement. “President Trump has done more than any other President to ensure we have free and fair elections,” said Lauren Bis.

Bis referred specific questions about CISA’s handling of elections to DHS. DHS and CISA did not respond to interview requests or to a detailed list of questions.

“There’s no fucking way [state governments are] ever going to give any information to CISA. And it’s unclear if they ever will in the future.”

Current and former CISA employees and state election officials describe an agency that has become significantly less equipped to help states fight legitimate cyber threats. State leaders say they have used their own resources to shore up gaps left by CISA, such as paying for sensor technology that can detect unusual activity in election networks. But some services, like intel on foreign actors, can’t be replicated. Some people interviewed said they were worried that Trump and his allies continue to ratchet up rhetoric about voting security, particularly about threats to voting machines. Officials in one large voting jurisdiction said they have met privately with machine vendors to come up with a contingency plan if the federal government seizes their machines or declares them unusable.

“I don’t pay attention to what the president says. I pay attention to what his administration does,” Arizona Secretary of State Adrian Fontes said. “And what they’ve done is basically cut us off and left us on our own.”

Defending elections from hackers wasn’t always part of DHS’s remit.

Throughout the mid-1990s and 2000s, a series of cyberattacks rocked the federal government, demonstrating the emerging threat. In 2007, hackers broke into the Pentagon and stole terabytes of sensitive data. The digital heist, by an unknown foreign adversary, was so damaging that one intelligence leader later described it as an “espionage Pearl Harbor.”

Under George W. Bush, DHS underwent a reorganization to more effectively combat terrorism and respond to disasters like Hurricane Katrina. The department dedicated resources under an office called the National Protection and Programs Directorate to help fortify advanced cyber defense systems and important infrastructure. The office’s work ranged from helping secure events like the Super Bowl to protecting chemical plants to issuing cybersecurity warnings to the private sector and the public. In 2018, Trump signed a bill making the office a stand-alone agency, allowing for more federal funding and transforming it into, essentially, the ultimate tech support desk for anything DHS designated “critical infrastructure.” The bill renamed the agency CISA.

Though Trump helped create CISA, he didn’t much appreciate the work the agency did under its old name to expose the extent of Russia’s meddling in the 2016 presidential election. Russian agents had unleashed a massive disinformation campaign, with a Kremlin-backed troll farm creating thousands of social media accounts posing as Americans and promoting Trump, according to bipartisan reports from the U.S. Senate Select Committee on Intelligence released before the 2020 election. A dozen Russian military agents were indicted for what the Justice Department described as “a sustained effort to hack into the computer networks of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee, the Democratic National Committee, and the presidential campaign of Hillary Clinton.”

Trump publicly downplayed this interference, but DHS leadership saw the Russia attack as an escalation. In January 2017, before Trump took office, DHS upgraded America’s elections to the status of critical infrastructure. This placed the polls and all voting technology used in federal elections under the watch of what would soon become CISA.

To carry out this function, CISA needed help. But states and voting machine vendors were wary about sharing data on weaknesses in voting systems, fearing it could be exploited if certain details were to become public. State officials were also reluctant to cede any role in election preparation to the federal government.

Gradually, though, CISA and state election officials negotiated a fragile trust. At its center was a commitment: The sensitive information states and vendors shared with CISA would be kept confidential.

“It took years and years of building trust, of talking to election officials, of hiring people with elections experience who could be validators for the work that CISA wanted to do, before they finally built strong working relationships,” said Derek Tisler, who specializes in election security as counsel for the nonprofit Brennan Center for Justice, a nonpartisan institute based out of New York University School of Law.

Under Director Chris Krebs, CISA hosted penetration testing in which its agents would try to hack into a state’s system to locate weaknesses that could then be patched, along with tabletop exercises simulating a series of potential catastrophes around elections. CISA also offered regular secret-level briefings to secretaries of state to share federal intelligence. And CISA paid for sensor technology that looked for potential malicious online activity on election networks, notifying officials about emerging threats.

“The staffing was there, the expertise was there, the visibility was there, the funding was there,” Macias said.

The consortium shared data as run of the mill as spambots as well as more serious threats. If Virginia caught unusual activity in its system, it could let other states know to be vigilant.

While CISA had prepared for attacks from the Chinese and Russian governments, or lone wolf hackers, few there had expected the threat to elections would come from inside the White House. But when Trump refused to concede to Joe Biden in 2020, claiming the vote had been rigged, CISA became a key component of the guardrails that ultimately led to the election results being certified.

The agency, in a statement co-signed by secretaries of state and voting machine vendors, vouched for the election’s integrity, calling it “the most secure in American history.” When Krebs publicly rebutted claims amplified by the White House as “unsubstantiated” or “technically incoherent,” Trump fired him over Twitter.

As Congress prepared to vote on certifying the election results, it was members of CISA, along with the FBI, who convinced then-Attorney General Bill Barr that the conspiracy theories embraced by the president could be explained by human error, not systemic fraud or hacking.

When Trump returned to the White House in January 2025, his administration immediately started to dismantle CISA. The administrative notices were sent out just a couple of weeks after the inauguration and included the team in charge of dispelling disinformation.

For the employees under the DOGE microscope, work became impossible, said one former senior CISA employee. “Those groups couldn’t even order a pizza.”

DOGE laid off people critical to the agency’s election work, including those who’d worked closely with states. The Trump administration cut funding for the technology that monitored web and election servers, blinding the federal government to breaches it had detected.

When it came time for states to prepare for the 2026 midterms, CISA had disappeared.

On the evening of July 16, in the Black Hills of South Dakota, dozens of election officials and their staffs gathered for a reception near Mount Rushmore. It was the third night of an annual conference for secretaries of state, drawing participants from red and blue states. They’d spent the week discussing state voter roll maintenance and voter ID — issues that at times made for disagreements.

At about 8 p.m., a wave of discomfort swept through the crowd, visible on attendees’ faces as they glanced frequently at their phones, according to people at the event. Any minute now, Trump was going to address the nation on election security. Some at the reception worried he would declare a national emergency, as his allies had urged him to do months earlier, and use it as a pretext to direct the government to seize voting machines.

Then reports on the speech began rolling in, and the mood shifted. The claims Trump was making were unsubstantiated and outlandish. Many had been examined ad nauseam, including by those at the reception, and roundly debunked. The collective anxiety that was palpable moments earlier melted away, as many saw the speech as another attempt to revive disproved theories unlikely to be taken seriously by most voters.

“Everyone just sort of guffawed and put their phones away and went to the bar,” recalled Quinn Raymond, co-founder of VoteShield, a nonprofit that works with states to help monitor voter registration data, who was at the event.

Still, if the speech made little impact, records published by the Trump administration to support the president’s claims damaged the already eroding relationship between CISA and state election officials. The material included raw information states had shared with the agency that was supposed to remain private, such as a log of communications from Election Day 2020 detailing activity being monitored in several states.

“It betrayed the trust of all partners who were willing to share information at the time,” said Geoff Hale, who worked on CISA’s elections team from the agency’s inception until last year. Now he specializes in election security at the Center for Democracy & Technology, a nonprofit that advocates for digital and civil rights.

Bis, the White House spokesperson, denied that the Trump administration broke any agreements in releasing the information. “The release of such information is exactly the sort of transparency news organizations should applaud,” she said. Bis also defended Trump’s speech, saying it had been fact-checked “by the agencies whose evidence was released and did not mischaracterize any information.”

Hale and others said the speech was one of several instances demonstrating the Trump administration’s reckless attitude toward sensitive data collected by CISA. Earlier this year, Politico reported that acting CISA Director Madhu Gottumukkala had uploaded records to ChatGPT, even though DHS had barred its employees from using the unsecure program. A CISA spokesperson told Politico that Gottumukkala had been permitted to use the technology for a “short-term and limited” basis.

Last May, an independent journalist named Brian Krebs exposed that CISA published private data, including passwords, cloud keys and other information, on its public GitHub page, in a folder labeled “Private-CISA.” A CISA spokesperson told the journalist there was no indication that sensitive data was compromised.

Some state election authorities were also alarmed to see the lengths to which the Trump administration went to legitimize the president’s fixation on the 2020 race.

In fall 2025, Missouri election officials produced a memo stating Andrew McCoy Warner, a Department of Justice official and prominent 2020 election conspiracy theorist, had called several clerks asking for access to voting machines. Tulsi Gabbard, then the director of national intelligence, had also led an operation in Puerto Rico to seize election machines. When the private cybersecurity contractor hired to examine the machines, Mojave Research Inc., failed to find evidence supporting claims about 2020, the federal government became “dissastified” with its services and stopped renewing its contract, its CEO later told reporters.

A spokesperson for the Office of the Director of National Intelligence told reporters in August that Mojave’s work ceased because it had completed its contract. Testifying before Congress in March, Gabbard said the seizing of voting machines came at the request of an assistant U.S. attorney in Puerto Rico.

“What happened in the second Trump administration is from almost day one — I mean, literally the first two months — they took all that was built up by Trump 1 and just decimated it,” said David Becker, founder of the nonpartisan Center for Election Innovation and Research, a nonprofit that works with state election authorities. “Election officials lost trust in the federal government.”

It didn’t help that, in his speech, the president mocked CISA’s defense of the 2020 election, calling the statement about it being the most secure in history a “very pat line” concocted as part of a deep-state cover-up. One former career CISA employee described being crestfallen at the Trump administration’s erosion of all they’d accomplished.

“We worked so hard to build that trusted relationship,” said Caitlin Durkovich, former DHS assistant secretary for infrastructure protection and co-founder of the cybersecurity firm Civira Partners. “I don’t know how you put it back together.”

A few weeks after Trump’s speech, the state election officials gathered once more, this time for a video call with Nick Andersen, who had replaced Gottumukkala as CISA’s acting director.

Though the primaries were already under way, this was the first time many had met Andersen, a former cybersecurity adviser to Trump’s White House who had joined CISA a year earlier. Andersen is also a district representative for the Republican Party in Virginia, according to the party’s website. In March, he gave a speech to the Fairfax GOP chapter titled “Fighting Democrat Redistricting.”

ChatGPT can give decent financial advice — to the people who need it the least.

A complaint I hear a lot from my millennial peers is that our parents didn’t teach us enough about money. I can say that personal finance wasn’t dinner table conversation in my house, and even as I went off to college and started fending for myself, it was almost a taboo subject. When it came to money, people either had it or they didn’t, and you definitely didn’t talk about it.

Maybe this is why the average American isn’t exactly fluent in personal finance, and the situation is getting worse. Americans can only answer about 50 percent of basic questions about borrowing, savings, and risk on a financial literacy survey, according to a 2026 study by TIAA, a retirement services company. About a third of those surveyed said they have a hard time making ends meet and are debt-constrained. These figures have remained relatively unchanged for nearly a decade, but dropped to their lowest level in this year’s study.

A weekly dispatch to make sure tech is working for you, instead of overwhelming you. From senior technology correspondent Adam Clark Estes.

It makes sense: It’s hard to learn about money when it’s not taught in schools, and it’s such an intensely private topic. It’s also hard to make good financial decisions when you’re one of the growing number of American households struggling to pay bills, repay debts, and save for the future — and feeling very stressed about it.

In a perfect world, each of us could sit down with a financial adviser to get help — but most of us can’t afford that. Instead, average Americans tend to find personal finance tips on TikTok, in Reddit threads, by watching Suze Orman on YouTube or How to Get Rich on Netflix, or by Googling their questions.

With access to an internet’s worth of information and financial advice, ChatGPT, Claude, and other AI chatbots can answer any question you ask. They can help you put together a basic budget or, armed with your bank statements and the details of your student loans and your retirement accounts, tailor solutions to your specific situation. Or, at least, that’s the promise.

AI could create a more utopian, more equitable future, where reliable, hyper-personalized advice is just a prompt or two away for people who can’t afford to meet with an expert in real life. But machines are built by people, and there’s good reason to be concerned that these machines will simply replicate the biases and disparities built into our current system.

Here’s what experts say about how to make the most of these tools — and how to think about all of the inherent risks of uploading a lot of personal financial information to a chatbot or, worse, giving an AI agent the keys to your bank account.

Nearly 20 percent of Americans have used AI tools like ChatGPT for financial advice, and close to 25 percent for Gen Z adults and millennials have tried it, according to an August Gallup poll. These numbers could tick up too: In October, OpenAI started rolling out a dedicated personal finance tool to its ChatGPT Free and Go users in the United States, which effectively means anyone can try it. ChatGPT Finances lets you securely connect your bank account to the tool using a platform called Plaid, and then you can basically have a conversation with the chatbot about your real-time finances down to the penny. It’s like an AI-powered Mint (RIP) that you can talk to directly.

Regardless of where you encounter a chatbot or how much data you give it, experts say these tools could genuinely help improve financial literacy. You can talk to them about money problems you’re too embarrassed to discuss with friends and family, or ask questions and receive actionable, personalized information. There are real upsides to using AI this way.

Taha Choukhmane, an associate professor of finance at the MIT Sloan School of Management, has been studying outcomes when people turn to chatbots for financial advice. In general, these AI tools give pretty good advice, and that advice gets better as people become more practiced in asking the right question, according to research he published earlier this year.

“We were actually quite surprised that relative to the status quo, the average American would be better off if they were to follow the advice,” Choukhmane told me. But he added a caveat: “Just following the advice without understanding the advice puts you at risk of not fully understanding the risk.”

But you shouldn’t trust everything a chatbot tells you and should verify everything before making any decisions.

Chatbot advice has some significant limitations. First, it’s not impartial. The finance industry has a vested interest in steering you toward certain ways of managing your money, and that typically means selling you products, like loans. If you’ve ever searched for personal finance tips on Google, you’ve probably noticed that a lot of the top results come from banks and other financial institutions. This is by design, according to John Campbell, an economics professor at Harvard. But because AI is aggregating all of that advice, the specific influences and possible bias is less obvious.

“AI is very promising for providing low-cost financial advice in plain English that will arguably be better than what many people would be able to do on their own,” said Campbell, who’s also the co-author of Fixed: Why Personal Finance Is Broken and How to Make It Work for Everyone. “One caveat is that chatbot advice is quite likely — unless a model has been carefully trained — to reflect the average of whatever’s being posted on the internet, and that may or may not be first-rate advice.”

An example Campbell gives is the question of when to start taking Social Security benefits. While you’re eligible at age 62, many economists agree that you should delay your benefits, which, based on the way the government calculates your monthly payouts, will allow you to enjoy much higher benefits later in life. But paid financial advisers can recommend starting as early as possible, since waiting longer reduces the amount of money that needs to be managed, which reduces the adviser’s fees. That means there’s a lot of conflicting advice online, much of it supplied by the finance industry. And that’s the information used to train AI models.

“The advice is much more mixed,” Campbell said. “And so if a chatbot is learning from that, it may not actually give the best advice.”

It can be hard to recognize bad advice anywhere in life, but especially when you’re talking to a machine that’s programmed to please you. A chatbot could encourage you to consider a balance transfer, to lean in on credit card rewards, or to refinance your mortgage — all decisions with upsides and downsides for you, but definite upsides for banks — without you realizing how much of its training data comes from the finance industry selling these products. What’s worse is that if you do end up taking bad advice, it might be years before you realize it. You won’t know that you refinanced a mortgage at a bad time until a better time comes along.

That leads me to the other major issue with using chatbots for money advice: People who lack financial literacy are the most likely to fall for bad advice, because they simply don’t recognize it as such. They also tend to be less wealthy, which means they can’t afford big mistakes.

One way to mitigate risk, oddly enough, is to give the AI chatbot more personal information. The more details about your situation it has to consider, the more it can offer personalized advice. More context also means better advice, according to Choukhmane.

“There’s a trade-off there,” Choukhmane said. “There’s real risk around privacy, and so I think everyone needs to find their level of control.”

Any time you’re talking to a chatbot, you’re putting a lot of trust in tech companies to take care of the personal details you’re sharing. Sending sensitive financial data is an especially risky proposition. While tech companies, including OpenAI, promise to keep your data secure, there’s already a long list of breaches, leaks, and privacy violations that serve as cautionary tales.

It’s also impossible to know what tech companies might do with your data in the future. Most of them will use it to train their models, unless you tell them not to. Some of them will use the data to serve you personalized ads.

Then there’s the risk of bad actors getting into your ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini account. If you’ve been using a chatbot for financial advice and uploading your bank statements, a hacker could gain access to a stunning amount of information that could enable them to do all kinds of terrible things to your life.

“They could steal your identity and open accounts and then quickly bankrupt a person,” said Delicia Reynolds Hand, a senior director at Consumer Reports. “It’s not even so much the primary underlying accounts that could be at risk, it’s more just access to this information.”

The steady march of AI progress is making this situation even more complicated. The previous era of chatbots offering financial advice is quickly bleeding into a new phase of AI agents logging into bank accounts on users’ behalf. If you’re using a tool like Claude Cowork or even an AI assistant like Muse, you can give an agent the login credentials to your bank account, and because the agent is operating its own computer, it can actually make transfers, apply for loans, or really do anything in your account. This is reportedly driving major banks wild, since they don’t know which logins are coming from humans and which are agents. And because we know that AI agents are unpredictable, it’s hard to know how that access might go wrong.

Beyond data security and privacy, you should also be aware of the simple fact that nobody’s really at fault if you end up losing money using these tools. Chatbots are not regulated in the way that human financial advisers, who have a fiduciary duty to act in your best interest, are.

“If I get bad tax advice from H&R Block or a tax adviser, there are a clear set of rules and guardrails in place to manage what I can expect in that relationship and where the service they use has liability and where that liability ends,” Hand said. “Currently, that’s not the case for these tools.”

In the final years of the Cold War, Ronald Reagan popularized an old Russian proverb: Trust, but verify. The phrase is somewhat nonsensical, since trusting someone implies that you don’t need to verify what they’re saying, and verifying what’s said implies you didn’t trust them in the first place. But if you are willing to shoulder a bit of risk, consider approaching AI this way, especially when your life savings are on the line. I’d also suggest adding a coda to the phrase: And then delete your data.

Chatbots are not stand-ins for licensed financial advisers, he said, and you shouldn’t let them make decisions for you.

The great paradox with tools like ChatGPT and Claude is that they’re very good at generating truthy text. It sounds authoritative and looks correct — and usually is. Except when it isn’t. AI is famously unpredictable and prone to hallucinations. You can ask the same question twice and get two different answers. That doesn’t mean these tools are useless. It just means you have to approach them with caution. You have to interrogate these chatbots, challenge whatever advice they give you, and then crosscheck that with other, more reputable, sources.

“If you’ve never used these tools, I would not encourage you to just change all your financial plans based on your first conversation,” Choukhmane said. “You want to be quite careful. Start using these tools, start understanding, ask a lot of follow-up questions.”

When it comes to mitigating risk, consider giving ballpark figures for your income or spending when you’re talking to a chatbot about your finances. Uploading financial documents, even if they’re redacted, is inevitably filling a tech company’s vault with your sensitive information without necessarily knowing what will happen to it next. You should also dig into your privacy and security settings to make sure you’ve turned on multi-factor authentication and that your personal data is not being used to train AI models and gets deleted after 30 days.

Ethan Bloch, the product lead for ChatGPT for Financial Services, made a similar set of recommendations when I talked to him recently. Chatbots are not stand-ins for licensed financial advisers, he said, and you shouldn’t let them make decisions for you. They’re designed to inform and help you think through complex topics.

“You should liken it to just talking to a friend who has some very informed opinions about finance,” Bloch told me. “But if you’re actually making some large financial decision — your portfolio or retirement — you still need to seek out the help.”

Regardless of how experienced you are with chatbots or how much data you’re prepared to hand over, remember what Campbell, the Harvard professor, said. If your financial literacy isn’t great, AI chatbots can be trouble. And if you’re like most Americans, you could probably stand to learn a little bit more about things like mortgages and interest rates.

Yes, Chatbots can be a good way to brush up on financial literacy. You can ask them the money questions you’re too embarrassed to discuss with friends and family. But you can also learn about money the old-fashioned way: Reading books and websites written by reputable, independent sources. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. has a portal of financial education resources, including games and quizzes. Khan Academy has a course that covers everything from credit scores to college savings. There’s a personal finance subreddit that I joined years ago and read almost daily. There are budgeting apps, like YNAB which has its own huge Reddit presence.

Whatever you do to learn more about personal finance, simply doing something is progress. Talking about personal finance isn’t the taboo it once was in the US, and it’s even a required course in the vast majority of American high schools now. And while AI won’t magically solve your money problems, it can help get you started. Just be careful. Your livelihood could be on the line.

#338. The State Officials Defending the Midterms from the President

Now and then, not often, Deidre Henderson will still work up the nerve to drive herself to the market for milk or bread. She will sit in the parking lot in her big S.U.V., engine running, doors locked, ruminating on risk. Generally, she heads home without getting out of the car.

Henderson, who is fifty-two years old, is the lieutenant governor of Utah, a Republican midway through her second term. That makes her the state’s chief election officer. (Elsewhere, the responsibility most commonly falls to a secretary of state.) It also makes her a target. Along with her counterparts in dozens of other states, Henderson has refused to allow the Trump Administration to obtain state voter rolls and to oversee the removal of voters it deems unqualified, in advance of the midterms, on November 3rd. For that resistance, election conspiracists have deluged her with threats of sexual violence and murder.

“It’s scary,” she told me late this summer. “It’s horrible. Honestly, I’ve had to have counselling just to function, to deal with the burden of fear that comes from the threats I’ve been getting.”

Many of the messages, however disturbing, are protected speech under the First Amendment. Some, though, have been treated by law enforcement as posing a credible danger to Henderson’s life or safety. In Utah, a lieutenant governor does not routinely get a security detail. But in Henderson’s first term, when she opposed Donald Trump’s attempts to overturn the 2020 Presidential election, she received threats that were so intense and specific that the Department of Public Safety placed her under protection for more than two years. The protection resumed before the 2024 election for a period. Henderson said that at times she has hired other protection. (The D.P.S. declined to comment on Henderson’s security detail.)

It was not always this unnerving to be an election official. But persistent lies about voter fraud, a centerpiece of Trump’s political narrative, have set loose torrents of hostility toward the men and women who oversee balloting nationwide. There are more than ten thousand voting jurisdictions in the United States, ranging from townships to states. In a national survey of local election officials this year, a third of them reported that they had personally been subjected to threats, harassment, or abuse because of their work. Half said they feared for the safety of their colleagues.

In this election season, the familiar threats, online and often pseudonymous, have been accompanied by new ones from federal authorities. The Department of Justice has warned Henderson and her counterparts that they may face criminal prosecution and imprisonment if they fail to surrender state voter rolls and purge them as the President directs.

Never before, not even when Trump tried to nullify his defeat by Joe Biden, six years ago, has an American President marshalled the coercive authority of the executive branch to reshape an election for his own benefit. Trump fought in court, in Congress, and in state legislatures to reverse the results of the 2020 election, but, as Brendan Nyhan, a Dartmouth political scientist who studies democratic erosion, told me, “he did not use his powers to try to undermine that election in advance.” And Trump ultimately failed to persuade high-level subordinates, such as William Barr, who was then the Attorney General, to wield their government power on his behalf. This year, multiple federal agencies, following Trump’s directives, attempted to strong-arm state election officials long before the first votes were cast. Trump, Nyhan told me, has tried “to tilt the playing field the way authoritarian populist leaders do in countries around the world.”

Article I, Section 4, of the Constitution reserves for the states the power to prescribe the “Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives,” with the proviso that Congress may change the rules by statute. The Framers granted the President no role in elections. Trump has declared otherwise. In an August, 2025, post on Truth Social, he wrote, “Remember, the States are merely an ‘agent’ for the Federal Government in counting and tabulating the votes. They must do what the Federal Government, as represented by the President of the United States, tells them, FOR THE GOOD OF OUR COUNTRY, to do.” Trump is open about his motives. He has warned Republicans that if they fail to purge the “millions and millions” of noncitizens who, according to his fantastic claims, are voting, they “will never win another election as a Republican.”

Early in his second term, Trump issued an executive order to restrict voter registration, impose new standards for voting machines, step up prosecution of “election crimes,” and forbid state authorities to count ballots that are mailed on time but arrive after Election Day. This past March, he issued another executive order with fresh instructions for election oversight, including a call for the Department of Homeland Security, in concert with other agencies, to “compile and transmit to the chief election official of each State a list of individuals confirmed to be United States citizens” and eligible to vote. The same order outlined new restrictions on voting by mail, specifying that the U.S. Postal Service “shall not transmit mail-in or absentee ballots from any individual” who does not appear on a U.S.P.S. list of eligible voters. How that list would be compiled, and by whom, and under what criteria, was left unclear. A coalition of states, the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee, and voting-rights groups separately filed suits. (The Brennan Center for Justice at N.Y.U. School of Law, where I am a senior adviser, is a co-counsel in two of the legal challenges.) For now, courts have blocked the most consequential provisions of both orders, primarily on the ground that the President has no lawful authority to impose them.

Meanwhile, the Justice Department set to work on a separate path. In a string of nearly identical lawsuits, filed beginning in September, 2025, it accused chief election officers in thirty states and the District of Columbia of violating Title III of the Civil Rights Act by failing to hand over electronic copies of their unredacted voter-registration lists. All but one state fought the lawsuits, including seven that had Republican chief election officers. (Oklahoma agreed to submit to the D.O.J. demand in exchange for dismissal of the case.) In response to United States of America v. Deidre M. Henderson, the Utah lieutenant governor, like most of her counterparts, cited privacy laws that obliged her to protect the personal information of voters, and said that the Trump Administration had no legal power to demand access in any case.

Since the litigation began, twenty-seven U.S. district judges and one appellate panel have reached decisions. All of them ruled against the Trump Administration. The case against Henderson was dismissed on October 23rd. One representative opinion, by Judge David O. Carter, in California, held that the D.O.J.’s declared purpose was “contrived” and that its demand for voter lists “wholly disregards the separation of powers.”

As its civil lawsuits foundered, the Justice Department reached for another weapon to compel obedience from state election officers. In a letter dated July 7th, the Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon warned Henderson and her counterparts that the D.O.J. was “also authorized to prosecute criminal violations” of the relevant statutes. Winning the civil litigation, in other words, would not prevent a state election official from being indicted for unspecified crimes. None of the election officials surrendered to that threat. “Garbage—I’m not breaking the laws,” Arizona’s secretary of state, Adrian Fontes, a Democrat, told me. Trump, he said, is “trying to pick his own voters, because he doesn’t believe in the American people. He believes in preserving his own power.”

The following week, the Secretary of Homeland Security, Markwayne Mullin, issued a warning of his own. His department had repurposed a program called SAVE(Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements), which previously had been used only to check immigrants’ eligibility for federal benefits, and pressed state election officials to use it to verify citizenship. A federal judge had blocked this use of SAVE. Mullin, however, warned that election officials who failed to use SAVE to screen voter rolls for noncitizens could face “prison time.” (The Supreme Court later stayed the district judge’s order but noted that it was too late to make large-scale changes to voter- registration lists.)

The Trump Administration’s efforts to cull the electorate before the midterms have, by and large, failed. Courts stripped the President’s executive orders of meaningful impact. At least sixteen states, all Republican-controlled, voluntarily agreed to turn over voter rolls to the Justice Department, but only three—Alaska, South Carolina, and Texas—signed agreements allowing the D.O.J. to oversee the removal of supposedly ineligible names. Twenty-eight states have voluntarily registered to use the SAVE data to check their voter lists for noncitizens, but it is unclear how many have relied on it to remove voters. In any case, it is too late for further purges: the statutory deadline to “systematically remove” ineligible voters is ninety days before a federal election.

In a last-ditch effort, the Justice Department brought a new lawsuit on October 15th, asking a California judge to require the state to set aside the ballots of about twenty thousand people, who the Administration alleged were “likely noncitizens.” The following day, the acting Deputy Attorney General, Trent McCotter, sent a letter to Los Angeles County election officials, threatening them with prosecution if they knowingly count the alleged noncitizens’ votes. On October 19th, McCotter said that twenty-eight California residents had been charged with violating federal voting laws.

Similar D.O.J. claims of widespread voter fraud elsewhere have proved spurious. In Nevada, the Administration alleged that there were nearly sixteen thousand noncitizens on voter rolls and produced the names of a hundred and eighty-five people, saying that it had manually verified their alien status. In September, state officials established that all of them were U.S. citizens. (A spokesperson for the D.O.J. said that the department continues to investigate individual cases of alleged noncitizens who voted in recent Nevada elections. A White House spokesperson said that, though “extreme Democrats and the media continue to dismiss concerns about voter fraud, the Trump Administration is taking decisive action to protect our elections, including prosecuting election crimes.”)

In another late-breaking intervention, this one even more extraordinary, the Trump Administration asked a federal judge on October 23rd to prevent certification of the Alaska Senate race if the Republican incumbent, Daniel S. Sullivan, loses narrowly. D.O.J. lawyers argued that the presence of a candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan on the ballot may deprive the senator of votes intended for him. (The Alaska Supreme Court had already ruled, in June, against an effort by the senator and the state G.O.P. to disqualify the challenger.)

Michael Waldman, the president of the Brennan Center, told me, “It turns out that our backstop is the fragmented nature of our political system, and federalism—that the election isn’t run by decree by any President, good or bad.”

If one thing has been decisive in safeguarding the nation’s electoral machinery, it is the tenacity of state officials who willingly accepted the political, financial, and personal risks of defying the President. They did so in a strong majority of states, including some under Republican control. “In order to protect democratic institutions,” Nyhan said, “we still depend on politically ambitious people sacrificing their careers.”

Henderson is among the most intrepid of the chief election officers who have stood in Trump’s way. For months, not a single Utah G.O.P. leader offered her public support. Yet, even as other Republicans who were resisting federal orders avoided open conflict with Trump, she spoke out against unlawful intervention from Washington. “It sure looks like they’re trying to take over control of elections to make sure only people they want to vote are voting,” she said recently. “To me, as an American and a Republican, it’s offensive.”

The Justice Department launched its campaign to seize control of voter rolls in May, 2025, when it began sending letters to state election officials which alleged that they had “anomalies” on their lists. Henderson received a letter that July, citing the Attorney General’s authority to bring “enforcement actions” under the National Voter Registration Act and questioning whether Utah was obeying the law. “Utah has the lowest rate in the nation for voter registration records removed” from the rolls, it alleged. The letter directed Henderson to “produce for inspection” a full electronic copy of Utah’s voter rolls, which contain sensitive personal information such as driver’s-license numbers, partial Social Security numbers, full dates of birth, phone numbers, and e-mail addresses.

It was unexpected and perplexing, Henderson said, to “get a letter from the U.S. Attorney General’s office citing all sorts of federal code.” Scott Cheney, a Utah assistant attorney general who serves as her principal legal adviser on elections, was out of the country. Henderson requested help from the office of Utah’s attorney general, Derek Brown. Brown sent over two lawyers, who, Henderson said, seemed to believe that she had no choice but to comply.

Henderson was skeptical. “I knew there were other federal statutes that applied,” she said, including the Privacy Act and the Driver’s Privacy Protection Act, which limit the release of personally identifying details. Henderson and the lawyers decided that the D.O.J. had overstepped. Their ensuing correspondence with the department began politely but devolved into threats and demands emphasized in italic type. She declined to hand over personal information about voters, citing state and federal privacy laws, and instead offered “the most current version of the public statewide voter registration list.” The next letter came from Dhillon, the Assistant Attorney General. Henderson’s reply was unacceptable, she wrote: “The Attorney General is demanding an electronic copy of Utah’s complete and current” voter rolls, and “Utah must ensure that it contains all fields.” Any contrary obligations that Henderson thought she had under Utah election and privacy statutes, Dhillon wrote, were “clearly preempted by federal law.”

Months later, the D.O.J. explained for the first time precisely why it was seeking the voter rolls. On December 2nd, a new D.O.J. lawyer, Eric Neff, sent Henderson a proposed “memorandum of understanding,” which disclosed that the D.O.J. intended to do a complete analysis of the rolls. Neff, a longtime legal warrior on behalf of election conspiracy theories, explained that the department would notify the state of any “insufficiencies, inadequacies, deficiencies, anomalies, or concerns” that it found. Utah would have forty-five days to “clean” its voter rolls and send back an updated list proving that “proper list maintenance has occurred.” The existence of this agreement between the state and the federal government, according to the memorandum, could not be publicly disclosed. The Justice Department sent a similar memorandum of understanding to many of Henderson’s counterparts in other states. Henderson decided to disregard the request for secrecy. She made all the correspondence public and announced that Utah would not comply with the D.O.J.’s demands.

On September 16th, the Justice Department filed lawsuits against the chief election officers of Maine and Oregon. Later that month, it did the same in California, Michigan, Minnesota, New Hampshire, New York, and Pennsylvania. In January, at a conference of the National Association of Secretaries of State, Henderson denounced Dhillon’s contention that states needed federal help to clean up their rolls. (Dhillon had claimed that the D.O.J. had “found two hundred and sixty thousand plus dead people” on state registration lists.) “The things that have been said publicly, frankly, are quite appalling,” Henderson told a White House official, Jared Borg, in open session, adding, of Dhillon, “She’s pretty much slandered all of us.”

By the time Dhillon’s office filed suit against Henderson, on February 26th, the Justice Department had already lost three of the cases against state election officers. Henderson began to taunt the Trump Administration.

“0-8,” she posted on Threads on May 21st, after judges dismissed five more of the D.O.J.’s lawsuits.

“0-9,” she posted on June 22nd, when a Maryland judge did the same.

“Persistence in the face of defeat is usually an admirable quality, but not in this case,” she posted on July 14th, when the count was 0–13.

I asked Henderson one day why she had chosen this provocative tone.

“Am I provoking?” she replied, sounding a little annoyed. “Now, it’s interesting that the person bringing the conflict into the light, highlighting the injustice of the situation, the unconstitutionality and illegality of the situation to the public, is the one that’s considered provocative.”

Had other Utah Republicans urged her to turn down the heat? I asked.

Many of them, though, including the highest-ranking state legislators, criticized her for refusing to coöperate with the Trump Administration. Some, notably the former gubernatorial candidate Phil Lyman, accused her of being involved in election fraud. “I don’t think that her voter rolls can withstand scrutiny,” he told me. Others privately expressed fear that Henderson would provoke retaliation from the President, perhaps causing the state to lose pending federal grants to build nuclear power plants and to restore the Great Salt Lake.

For years, Henderson had told her staff that her No. 1 rule was “If it feels good, don’t say it.” She wanted to do her job and stay out of political combat. But that stance was changing. Utah’s governor, Spencer Cox, has said that he will not seek a third term in 2028, and Henderson told me that she is “seriously considering running.” I asked whether she thought her public conflict with Trump would hurt or help that campaign. “I have no idea, and I don’t care,” she said. “If discharging the duties of my office with fidelity means that I am unelectable in 2028, I am O.K. with that.”

Renae Cowley, a Republican political consultant who admires the lieutenant governor, said that she did not doubt Henderson’s commitment to principle but thought another consideration might also be on her mind. “I think that Deidre is strategically poking the bear, and it is both a brilliant political strategy and exactly what her voters want her to do,” Cowley said. “We’re Westerners for a reason,” she continued. “We wanted to be able to live our lives and do so free from the all-seeing eyes of the government. Of all the things to push back on, this is perhaps the most palatable, strategic, winningest issue that she could pick.”

In mid-August, I visited Henderson in Utah. As I rode with her to her office, she steered her S.U.V. onto the capitol grounds and edged toward what appeared to be a blank granite façade. An unmarked entrance opened to reveal a dimly lit parking bay, just big enough to accommodate side-by-side vehicles, for Henderson and Governor Cox.

In an hour, at Henderson’s request, a team of election and law-enforcement officers from across the state would gather in her conference room for a crisis-management exercise. She had asked an outside consultant to devise scenarios for the midterms to test the preparedness of her team. Election officials around the country run similar tabletop exercises before each voting cycle, to plan for contingencies ranging from extreme weather and power failures to bomb threats and foreign interference.

First, Henderson convened her senior staff for an update on the workaday operations for the election: auditing certification procedures, offering assistance to voters, unsnarling data conflicts, interpreting obscure rules. They met in the lieutenant governor’s private office, which is outfitted with polished dark wood, green curtains, and tan leather chairs. A painting on one wall depicts Seraph Young, who in 1870 became the first woman to cast a ballot in the Utah Territory. It is a point of pride for Henderson that voting rights for women there predated the Nineteenth Amendment by half a century.

Ali Majid, the state director of election systems, told Henderson that his quarterly audit of voter rolls had been updated to comply with a Utah statute that required a passport, a birth certificate, or similar proof of citizenship to vote in state races. He reported that thirty-five hundred people would soon be disqualified from voting in state elections because they had not responded to requests for that documentation. (Most states have no such requirement.)

Henderson was disturbed by the number. She had been mistakenly disqualified a few years back, because she was born in the Netherlands, where the Air Force had stationed her father. “Are we making sure clerks are actually reaching out to voters?” she asked.

In one database, Majid said, the list of registered but inactive voters included “thirty-three voters that were unconfirmed and ten voters that were likely noncitizens.”

“None of them ever voted,” Henderson said, in what seemed like more of a statement than a question.

“None of them ever voted,” Majid confirmed.

Next, Henderson and her staff discussed a new D.H.S. rule, announced in July. Mullin’s department had said that it would cut twenty per cent of FEMA homeland-security grants to states that did not comply with “election security” requirements. No one at the table was sure what the state attorney general planned to do about the rule, but they hoped that Utah would join lawsuits by other states to challenge it.

“Because we’re going to lose all the funding, not just twenty per cent,” Henderson said, sounding resigned.

“There’s the nonsense of it,” Ryan Cowley, Utah’s election director, replied. “You have FEMA, who knows nothing about elections, now dictating not just, like, ‘You have to have secure elections.’ Now they’re dictating exactly how.”

On September 28th, a federal judge ruled that FEMA could not withhold the funds.

The President’s campaign to seize control of the midterms has gone far beyond voter purges and threats to withhold federal funding. Ranging widely across the executive branch, it has included F.B.I. raids to seize election and voter-registration records, and menacing suggestions that Trump may send armed ICE agents to the polls.

One of Trump’s earliest attempts to “take over” elections, as he has put it, was the 2025 executive order on voting, which would have required, among other things, that people show a passport or comparable proof of citizenship in order to register. A national survey found that at least twenty-one million Americans do not possess or have no ready access to those documents. The House had passed a similar requirement, called the SAVE (Safeguard American Voter Eligibility) Act, but Republicans in the Senate were struggling to wrangle enough votes. Trump tried, unsuccessfully, to bypass Congress and impose the rule himself.

The same executive order directed the Election Assistance Commission, an independent federal agency, to “rescind all previous certifications of voting equipment” and establish new standards to address some of the claims that Trump and his allies had made about rigged election machinery in 2020. This provision of Trump’s order was among those struck down by a federal judge.

The President also enlisted law-enforcement agencies to produce new evidence of voter fraud, past and present. This past January, Kurt Olsen, a prominent election denier and at the time the newly appointed White House director of election security and integrity, asked the F.B.I. to launch a criminal investigation in Fulton County, Georgia, according to an F.B.I. affidavit, and the Bureau did so with uncommon dispatch. Trump had brought unsuccessful lawsuits alleging extensive fraud by Fulton County election officials in the 2020 Presidential election. The F.B.I.’s mission was to revive those allegations. In Georgia, dozens of F.B.I. agents—accompanied by Trump’s director of National Intelligence, who called the President immediately afterward—seized more than six hundred and fifty boxes of ballots, tabulator tapes, electronic ballot images, and other records from the 2020 election.

Five months after the Georgia raid, about a hundred F.B.I. and D.H.S. agents descended on the Cleveland offices of the Ohio Organizing Collaborative, a left-leaning group that advocates for voting rights and organizes voter-registration campaigns. The agents seized records and electronic devices, looking for evidence of unspecified criminal behavior. Last year, the President also ordered the D.O.J. to launch a criminal investigation of Chris Krebs, a former director of the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, in part on the ground that he had publicly disputed Trump’s claim that the 2020 election was rigged.

Federal law-enforcement agencies have also been involved in what amounts to a psychological operation ahead of the midterms. Trump’s subordinates have been hinting for months that ICE agents or other federal officers may turn up at polling places on Election Day. Federal law forbids the presence of “troops or armed men at any place where a general or special election is held.” But in March, Todd Blanche, who was then the Deputy Attorney General, said that he saw nothing wrong with it. “Why is there an objection to sending ICE to polling stations?” Blanche asked at the Conservative Political Action Conference. “Illegals can’t vote.” Mullin has raised the prospect several times, saying that ICE is not restricted from conducting enforcement operations at polling places, and may even be needed to protect them. (In a statement, D.H.S. said that it has no plans for operations “targeting polling locations.”)

Voting-rights advocates regard these statements as thinly veiled efforts to intimidate lawful voters. “They make headlines to use them as deterrents with no intention of deploying” on a large scale, Maria Teresa Kumar, the founding president of Voto Latino, said, noting that Latino voters have seen a lot of ICE violence in their communities, and that millions of citizens live in “mixed-status families,” with relatives who are vulnerable to deportation. Kumar said that the spectre of illegal voting was a pretext for the ICE threats, citing a study that found that fewer than 0.0001 per cent of ballots in the 2016 Presidential election, or one in a million, were cast by noncitizens.

The Trump Administration, for all its efforts, has failed to establish a commanding position over the federal voting landscape. No indictments or evidence of voter fraud have emerged from the raids in Ohio and Georgia. A Trump-appointed judge blocked a subpoena to collect what he called a “staggering” amount of personal information about election workers in Fulton County. Senate Republicans never found enough votes to pass the SAVE Act.

David Becker, a former senior trial attorney in the D.O.J.’s voting section and the founder of the Center for Election Innovation & Research, said that he saw “no coherent strategy” in the disparate attempts to carry out Trump’s wishes for the midterms. “There’s malevolence, but there’s a lot of incompetence,” he said, citing competing directives from the D.O.J. and D.H.S. and the stunning record of setbacks in court. “They don’t have a single success.”

Becker continued, “I think there’s a lot of people within the conspiracy-theory ranks—within the economy of grift that’s grown around election denialism—who are seeking approval from President Trump. And they’re all doing their thing to do that. And often those things conflict.”

Control of the House and the Senate will be decided by some very close races. Even the marginal success of Trump’s effort to disqualify voters in states that agreed to use D.H.S. citizenship data might tip one or more of these contests. And there is still a hypothetical risk, election experts say, that the President could do something new and outlandishly lawless on or close to Election Day.

In Henderson’s conference room, a test of nightmare scenarios commenced at 10 a.m. with a cheerful slide that read “Ready for Anything.” Cowley and Majid, the state election officials, were there. So were several lawyers and the election clerks of two of Utah’s twenty-nine counties.

The consultant who wrote the scenarios said she had devised “scary situations” that Utah’s previous contingency planning might not have addressed. About nine in ten Utah voters cast ballots by mail, but most of the scenarios would involve federal agents dispatched to Utah polling places or tabulation centers.

“I will just let you know they’re a little bit provocative, and that’s intentional,” she said. “I want to drive up the temperature just a little bit.”

Utah, which has long been represented exclusively by Republicans in Congress, might seem an unlikely place for conflict with Trump over elections. But in 2025 court-ordered redistricting created a strongly Democratic-leaning First Congressional District centered in Salt Lake County, where Kamala Harris beat Trump by ten points in 2024.

In one scenario, the consultant said, “the President announces that federal agents are going to ‘flood the zone’ on Election Day in certain areas with large immigrant populations.” He does not specify where the agents will go but states that “2026 will be the most secure election ever, thanks to ICE, C.B.P., and D.O.J.” Angry protests begin to build, but the protesters find no federal agents to confront.

“I think the first thing to do,” Henderson said, is “we try to find out from the federal agency, Who are you sending? What is the purpose of it?” State law-enforcement leaders, she said, should handle communication with their federal counterparts.

“I think this is an opportunity to remind the public of what you can and can’t do around polling places,” Cowley added.

The consultant laid out an escalated scenario. Now the election is days away, and federal agents have set up checkpoints on roads near early-voting sites. News cameras arrive. The Salt Lake County clerk requests assistance to secure the polls, but the sheriff, unsure about jurisdiction, declines. Meanwhile, “the neighboring chief of police deploys officers to ‘protect and support the federal agents.’ ”

One of the lawyers said that the legal questions were complicated because federal agents could say, “We’re not interfering with elections. We’re investigating a bomb threat.”

“You don’t want to pit yourself against the Feds,” a senior staff member said. Instead, he suggested, state law enforcement could try to coördinate with the federal agents to clear the roads to alternative voting centers: “The win is we are trying to help voters get to polling places.”

Another escalation. Federal agents arrive at multiple Utah voting centers on Election Day, enter the “no-electioneering zone,” and begin to question voters. The state attorney general posts on social media that federal officers are expected to comply with all state and federal laws. Trump responds, “This guy has no idea what he’s talking about. He has no power over our federal officers.”

The agents in this scenario clearly appeared to be breaking the law. Could state authorities do anything to stop the incursion?

Henderson said that she would leave it up to the commissioner of public safety to decide whether “state law enforcement needs to intervene.” Nobody wanted a standoff between armed state and federal officers. (Voting-rights lawyers, however, would already be racing to federal court.)

Yet another escalation. Citizens’ groups are now setting up counter-perimeters and demonstrations outside a voting center where federal agents are deployed. A car drives through the scene, injuring one of the Feds. The President posts that he is sending more federal officers to “secure the election against violent individuals.”

“It’s totally law enforcement,” the lawyer told the room. “It has nothing to do with elections or free speech at this point. They’ve got to de-escalate the situation.” All a local election official can do now, Henderson said, is give directions to move voters away from danger and toward another polling place.

One last escalation. The clock winds back to October 29th. F.B.I. agents show up at a county election office and execute a search warrant to seize “all mail ballots and mail-ballot return envelopes, a current list of all voters eligible to vote in Salt Lake County, and a current list of all voters who were sent a mail ballot.” An election worker on the scene reports that half the ballots and envelopes have been loaded onto F.B.I. trucks. “So, if that’s the case, I’ve already been taken to jail,” one of the county clerks said. She meant that she would have tried to safeguard the election by resisting the seizures.

Another lawyer cut in. “First of all, do you recognize you’re dealing with a signed federal search warrant? Do not interfere,” he said. “There’s certain precautions that you can take.”

He rattled them off: Stay calm. Don’t hide files or shut down computers; doing so would look like obstruction. Contact counsel immediately. Request the lead agent’s name and agency, the prosecutor’s name, and a copy of the warrant. Don’t consent to searches beyond the warrant’s scope. Assign an observer to watch what is taken. Mark whether that includes privileged materials.

Henderson’s mind seemed to be elsewhere. She was thinking, she later told me, about the broken chain of custody of the confiscated paper votes. How, she was wondering, could election officials reliably reconstruct how the votes were cast?

“Are these ballots that have been counted?” she asked finally.

If Trump manages to interfere with the midterm elections in a meaningful way, experts told me, it will probably resemble the final minutes of Henderson’s tabletop exercise.

Stephen Richer, a Republican who was the Recorder of Maricopa County, Arizona, in 2021, resisted chaotic efforts by Trump, state legislators, election deniers in Washington, and conspiratorial litigants to discredit Biden’s victory in the state. Now a fellow at the Cato Institute and Harvard’s Ash Center for Democratic Governance and Innovation, Richer has studied just about every way an election can be attacked. He believes that nearly all the election threats commentators have talked about this year are “overblown.” But there is one that still worries him.

“I think Fulton County was the real warning shot,” he said. “It showed an increased appetite for actually going into voting jurisdictions and taking tabulation equipment or ballots. And if they do that after all the ballots have been counted and certified, then it’s old hat, and we’ll defend the election if it’s defensible against liars. If they do it during a live election, I don’t know what we do.” The problem is the same one that preoccupied Henderson: chain of custody over ballots.

Suppose, Richer said, the F.B.I. rolls up to a voting center and seizes paper ballots before they are digitized or tabulated. Even if election authorities obtain an injunction and get the ballots back the same day, the ballots will have been “in an unobservable place for an hour or two hours, whatever it is,” he told me. “And I don’t know if I would trust those anymore.” Much the same quandary arises if agents seize and later return tabulation equipment.

Becker, the former D.O.J. voting lawyer, agreed that “if they were to break chain of custody, it would be very bad and very difficult to reconstruct.” (Depending on circumstances, he explained, “there might be ways” to authenticate the votes.) But in order to seize ballots, he noted, federal authorities would have to obtain a warrant, and the magistrate judge who signed the Fulton County warrant came in for a drubbing by legal commentators because the F.B.I. affidavit she approved did little more than recycle disproved conspiracy theories. After that, Becker said, “I don’t know if they’re going to find a federal judge who will issue a warrant” to seize voting materials, “especially in the middle of elections.” Richer’s hypothetical, he concluded, is “extremely unlikely yet disastrous if it happens.”

After the tabletop exercise, I walked with Henderson through the vaulted marble halls of the capitol on her way to lunch. We ran into Brown, the attorney general, who had been elected as a MAGA stalwart and has carefully avoided public comment on Henderson’s conflict with the Administration. I introduced myself as a journalist and told Brown what I was writing about. He took a step back and shot a look of unease, or perhaps reproof, at the lieutenant governor.

“Election law is a growth industry, and not in a good way,” he declared, in a faux-jocular tone. His expression did not convey amusement. He excused himself and walked briskly away.

Henderson told me that Brown “has been very good and supportive” behind the scenes, and she said the same thing about Cox. Still, she added, “there’s been a lot of silence. And that’s been challenging. Because, in my mind, I’m standing up for our legislature. I’m standing up for our state. I’m standing up for our voters.” A few weeks later, more than a year into the dispute, Cox spoke out in defense of Henderson for the first time. “It is wrong for the D.O.J. to investigate or threaten the lieutenant governor for carrying out her responsibilities under Utah law,” he posted on X.

When Henderson started her political career, she was closely aligned with her party’s “most conservative elements,” according to the Utah senator Todd Weiler, a Republican who has similarly turned against Trump. A lifelong member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, she had dropped out of Brigham Young University at eighteen to get married. (She earned her degree in 2022.) She had her first child at twenty. In the fall of 2007, she was, at thirty-three, a stay-at-home mother of five, wondering, as her youngest started preschool, what came next.

In those days, Henderson listened to a lot of right-wing talk radio: Limbaugh, Hannity, Beck. She considered herself a political junkie and thought that she might like to get involved. At the time, Jason Chaffetz was a little-known conservative firebrand running a long-shot challenge to the incumbent congressman in Utah’s Third District. Henderson went to hear Chaffetz speak one evening, at a small gathering in a private home. His campaign platform emphasized limited government, border enforcement, opposition to same-sex marriage, and a ban on federal funding for abortion. Henderson’s husband, Gabe, a physical therapist, recalled, “She said, ‘We found our candidate. We are going to help Chaffetz.’ ”

Chaffetz, recalling those early days, said he knew nothing about Henderson at first except that she was willing to work for free. She began to put in daily shifts, and soon she was planning events, giving speeches, and reaching out to local notables. After Chaffetz won, he gave her a paid position as his campaign manager.

Bryan Schott, a veteran political reporter at the Salt Lake Tribune and the founder of Utah Political Watch, a local journalism outlet, said that Henderson played a decisive role in Chaffetz’s victory: “The people who run campaigns here in Utah will tell you that she’s responsible for him getting into Congress the first time.” Chaffetz balked at handing her that much credit but said, “She was a talent, and I encouraged her to run for her own seat.” Henderson did just that in 2012, seeking and winning an open seat in the state Senate. By then, Gabe Henderson said, her political philosophy was evolving beyond the Chaffetz talking points: “She came home and said, ‘I am tired of being muzzled by what he wants me to say. I want my own voice.’ ”

Henderson built a reputation in the Senate for pragmatism but also for stubborn resolve on matters of conviction. She championed a bill to reduce criminal penalties for polygamy, arguing that fear of prosecution kept women trapped in abusive marriages. Although she supported the state’s ban on abortion after eighteen weeks of pregnancy, she walked out during the vote for a bill that required women seeking abortions to have ultrasounds, which she said was too intrusive. Knowing that the measure would pass, she recalled, “I thought, Well, I’ll just take a walk during roll call.” The way she recounted the story, she was surprised to discover that “all the other women followed.”

On the morning of the first day I spent with Henderson, she drove me to a graveyard. “It’s a little dorky,” she said, climbing out of the driver’s seat. “But this is a place I go on occasion when I need to reset.”

Henderson had dressed for work in heels that were not made for the rain-soaked grass. She led me to a modest bevel marker, embedded at an angle into the ground and inscribed “MARTHA HUGHES CANNON—1857-1932.”

Cannon, Henderson explained, was an immigrant from Wales who joined a wave of nineteenth-century pioneers from the Church of Latter-day Saints as they fled violent persecution back East. It was a history of faith and hardship that Henderson had learned as a child.

Cannon “became deeply involved in the women’s-suffrage movement,” Henderson said. “And one of the interesting things about Utah women that people don’t understand is they were portrayed by the Eastern press as subjugated and enslaved. They were ridiculed and mocked because of their religious beliefs and practices, but they were very much empowered.”

Cannon had earned a degree in medicine, a rarity for women at the time. She studied oratory. She became a well-known advocate for women’s right to vote, testifying in Congress. And in 1896, the year Utah achieved statehood, she became the first woman to be elected to the state Senate. Henderson said, “She ran against her husband, Angus, and won.”

“On the hard days, and there are a lot of them, I remember Martha,” she said, standing over the grave. “If you wonder why I’m the nail that’s sticking up to get hammered down, or why I’m saying the things I say, why I’m taking my role so seriously, it’s because it’s not mine. It’s entrusted to me right now. And I think about Martha and other women all the time.” Later, she described herself as “a heretic” within her party, adding, “I’m not going along with the Holy Scripture of the current Administration, which is election denialism.”

Henderson attributed the silence of some of her peers to timidity. “I think one of the biggest problems we have is that not everybody in the Republican Party thinks that we’re doing the right things, but a lot of people in the Republican Party are afraid to say that,” she said. “They’re afraid to push back. They’re afraid to speak out.” Chaffetz, the man she helped elect to Congress and a likely opponent if she runs for governor in 2028, said that, in his view, Henderson was the one who had strayed: “Politically, she’s a very different person than when I knew her, not nearly as conservative.”

On a drive south of Salt Lake City, Henderson paused for a pit stop at one of Utah’s drive-through “dirty soda” franchises, a regular habit, and ordered a quart of Diet Mountain Dew infused with a syrupy jolt of artificial strawberry and pineapple. (L.D.S. doctrine, as she understands it, forbids coffee and tea but does not prohibit caffeine.) I told her what Chaffetz had said about her becoming less conservative. Nothing in her expression suggested that she took offense at the remark, but her choice of words—in response to a man who now works as a Fox News podcast host—was tart. “I’ve mellowed,” she said. “I’m not always so certain of my rightness in everything. I’m much more curious now than I was before. I’ve seen that there’s talking and then there’s doing. And when it comes especially to state government, local government, you gotta have doers. Talkers don’t matter.”

On September 4th, Henderson received a reminder of the retribution that might await her. (Similar reminders arrived in other states.) Neff, the D.O.J. voting lawyer, notified Henderson formally that she might be in breach of federal law because she had failed to comply with the Justice Department’s demands. “As you are aware, you are currently under investigation,” he wrote, directing her to preserve all relevant records.

“Um, no, I was not aware, but I guess I am now,” Henderson responded on Threads. “It would be nice if the good folks at the D.O.J. would focus on actual problems instead of baselessly harassing election officials.”

Henderson and I had spoken about Trump’s many political enemies who had been placed under criminal investigation or indictment. These were expensive and life-changing burdens, even for those who avoided trial and conviction. How did she think about the risk to herself?

“I’m actually not terribly concerned about being prosecuted,” she told me. “I don’t presume to be someone important enough for the President to actually care about.” But, she added, “on the other hand, too, it gives me a little bit of hope when they start escalating like this. Because they’re getting a little desperate. Their attempts aren’t working.” ♦

#340. Brazil election results 2026: Key takeaways from Lula’s historic win

Lula wins a fourth term after a dramatic run-off marred by voting confusion and allegations of interference.

Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva has pulled off a political comeback that no Brazilian presidential candidate has managed in nearly four decades.

On Sunday, the 80-year-old leftist president won a record fourth non-consecutive term, overcoming a first-round defeat to right-wing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of jailed former President Jair Bolsonaro.

Lula won more than 51 percent of the vote to Bolsonaro’s 48 percent. Some 98.76 percent of the ballots were counted when electoral authorities declared him the winner.

Sunday’s results were far from certain, keeping Brazilians on edge until the very end. Bolsonaro and Lula had been virtually tied in the polls in the lead-up to the election.

A last-minute decision to extend voting hours sparked accusations of election interference and questions about whether the result could face legal challenges.

Already, Senator Bolsonaro has pledged to annul the vote, crediting the extended hours with sowing voter confusion.

Just three weeks ago, Lula looked like he was in trouble.

Bolsonaro had surprised many observers by beating the incumbent in the first round of voting on October 4, winning 47.03 percent of the vote to Lula’s 45.16 percent. The difference was 2.2 million votes.

But Lula spent the next three weeks trying to bring back voters who stayed home in the first round, while making a renewed pitch to those concerned about the cost of living and the future of his signature social programmes.

In the final days of the campaign, opinion polls showed the two candidates virtually tied.

Bolsonaro briefly took the lead as ballots were counted on Sunday evening. But Lula ultimately pulled ahead as results began arriving from the northeast, according to the Reuters news agency.

The northeast has long been a strong source of support for Lula and his Workers’ Party.

Bolsonaro, meanwhile, has large bases of support in the south and southeast of the country, as well as the centre-west.

For Lula, the victory means an unprecedented fourth term as president. For Bolsonaro, Sunday marks a defeat in his bid to return his family to the presidency.

No other candidate in modern Brazilian history has placed second in the first round of a presidential vote only to win in a run-off.

At age 80, Lula is also on track to beat his own record as the oldest Brazilian president to be sworn in since the country’s return to democracy.

As the presidential run-off entered its final hours on Sunday, both campaigns were calling for voting to be extended.

Lula’s campaign accused local officials of deliberately reducing public transport services in several cities, making it difficult for lower-income voters to reach polling stations.

Bolsonaro’s campaign, meanwhile, requested more time for voters in areas affected by heavy rain and flooding.

In Minas Gerais, the regional electoral court reported that 349 polling locations had lost electricity, although voting machines continued operating on backup batteries, according to local media.

Originally, voting was scheduled to end at 5pm Brasilia time (20:00 GMT).

But faced with the two campaigns’ petitions for an extension, Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin ordered polling stations to remain open for another two hours, until 7pm (22:00 GMT).

The decision, however, came after some stations had already closed and shut down their electronic voting machines.

Zanin later clarified that polling stations that had already closed would remain shut. Only those that were still open could continue accepting voters until 7pm.

The result was confusion over who could still vote, with some Brazilians turned away despite the extension.

In a brief post-election address, Bolsonaro pointed to those issues as a reason to annul the vote. He also accused Zanin of judicial malfeasance.

“He changes the rules of the game mid-game,” Bolsonaro said.

Brazil’s Federal Police are investigating alleged election-related transport disruptions in five states.

In Salvador, a court ordered the arrest of the city’s transport secretary over allegations he had directed cuts to bus services. Lula’s camp pointed to such incidents as evidence of election interference.

Bolsonaro levelled similar allegations against Zanin, pointing to his history as Lula’s personal lawyer. Lula named Zanin to the Supreme Court in 2023.

“It is impossible to recognise the results of the election,” Bolsonaro told his supporters, calling the circumstances “unacceptable”.

He is likely to pursue a court challenge in an effort to overturn his defeat. Already, some figures on the global right have signalled their support.

Donald Trump Jr, the son of United States President Donald Trump, voiced outrage over the decision to extend the voting hours.

“If a conservative government in Brazil did this, everyone in the mainstream press in America would lose their minds, but I guess they’re just choosing to be silent because it’s all for the cause,” Trump Jr posted on social media.

The Trump administration has backed the Bolsonaro family, echoing its claims that Jair Bolsonaro, the former president, was subject to a political “witch-hunt” in the courts.

Flavio Bolsonaro has called for reforming Brazil’s courts in the aftermath of his father’s legal troubles. Earlier this month, Bolsonaro said that if he were elected, he would seek changes to Brazil’s Constitution to “re-democratise” the country.

Al Jazeera correspondent Monica Yanakiew signalled that the tensions between Brazil’s right wing and its court system are likely to re-emerge after Sunday’s election results.

“The judicial system itself is very divided,” Yanakiew said, pointing to rifts within the Supreme Court. “There will need to be a lot of political negotiation.”

Higher-than-expected turnout may have helped Lula win, according to Andrei Roman, the head of polling firm AtlasIntel, who cited a drop in voter abstention as one factor behind the president’s victory.

An estimated 33.4 million Brazilians stayed home during the October 4 first round, despite voting being compulsory for most adults. That amounted to about 21 percent of the electorate, the highest abstention rate in a general election since 1998.

The question now is how much of a difference those voters made. With more than 2.2 million votes separating the candidates in the first round, even a relatively small change in turnout could have mattered in the run-off.

The final turnout figures, particularly in each candidate’s strongholds, could help explain how Lula managed to turn the race around.

Lula also made last-ditch pitches in the final weeks of his campaign, designed to energise his working-class base. Among them was a promise to pursue free public transport on weekends.

Lula’s victory comes amid a wave of right-wing electoral wins across Latin America, where Trump has sought to expand Washington’s influence.

The Bolsonaro family has long enjoyed Trump’s support. Reuters reported on Saturday that officials in Trump’s State Department had pushed for sanctions against Brazil as part of an effort to weaken Lula and return the Bolsonaro family to power.

Asked on Sunday whom he wanted to win, the US president would not name a candidate.

“I don’t want to get into that, but we are watching,” he told reporters.

Though Trump has been vocal in his support for other right-wing candidates in Latin America, he was relatively circumspect with regard to the Brazilian election, something critics have speculated was intentional.

While Bolsonaro has promised closer ties with the US, Lula made defending Brazil’s sovereignty and resisting foreign interference a central theme of his campaign.

He has often pointed to Trump’s aggressive tariff campaign and his public statements about the Jair Bolsonaro trial as evidence of foreign interference. Lula’s campaign has also clashed with US-based social media companies, prompting Trump to accuse his government of censorship.

In his victory speech, Lula described having to “overcome interference coming from the outside” in his bid for a fourth term.

“The election was a defeat for denialism,” Lula said on Sunday night. “It was a defeat for lies.”

He finished his speech by praising his “army” of supporters.

“I highly doubt that Russia, China, or the United States possess an army as victorious as the army of democracy in this country,” Lula said, thanking the voters.

And while ballot measures might seem like small potatoes in the grand scheme of electoral politics, they could have an outsized impact this year. The Trump administration has been systematically rolling back protections for endangered species, taking a hammer to longstanding pillars of climate regulation, and cutting funding to agencies like the National Park Service and National Forest Service, both of which oversee vast stretches of wildlife habitats. In many cases, states have had to step up, putting more resources into conserving the habitats and species within their borders, and ballot measures can help shape those decisions.

This ballot measure would shorten environmental review periods for “essential projects” like housing, water systems, clean energy, and medical facilities, among others. This amendment might seem a little in the weeds — and mostly in the interest of farmers and ranchers looking for tax breaks. #348. The Great, Cynical Fun of “The Traitors”

The reality-competition series “De Verraders” premièred in the Netherlands in 2021. Originally, Marc Pos, the creator, had designs to set the action entirely on a ship. He wanted to strand his contestants at sea as a nod to national lore: the seventeenth-century wreck of the Batavia, a Dutch East India Company ship. In 1629, the ship, carrying roughly three hundred souls, was headed from Amsterdam to its namesake port in Indonesia, when it struck a coral reef not too far from the landmass that would become known by the Europeans as New Holland, or Australia. What followed was the stuff of legend: the survivors were marooned, and the ship’s under-merchant, who had long been plotting a mutiny, orchestrated a series of killings, with everyone guessing who was in on it. Wouldn’t this serve as a juicy reference for Pos’s pop-psychology contest? How about throwing the losers into the sea? But the plan was ultimately too expensive. So production shifted the activity to a castle, all chandelier and shadow, and ordered a set of hooded cloaks for the contestants, setting the aesthetic that informs the now global franchise “The Traitors.”

Recently, Indonesia, in a kind of post-colonial cultural boomerang, announced its own version of “The Traitors.” About forty international adaptations are either airing or have been greenlit, putting the series on par with reality bigwigs such as “Survivor” and “Big Brother.” The premise of “The Traitors” is a gleeful ripoff of the parlor game known as Mafia, in which unwitting players must suss out the secret designated killers among them, not unlike what happened on the Batavia. At the start of the series, a small number of players are tapped to be the “traitors”—literally tapped, by the show’s host, on the shoulder during a blindfolded ceremony. The rest are “faithful.” Each night, the traitors meet in a turret to decide which players to “murder,” or eliminate from the game. By day, the whole group takes on missions, to build up a shared prize pot, with the traitors posing as faithful throughout. Then the group votes on whom to banish from the castle. Ideally, the faithful root out the duplicitous among them; if any traitors are left standing at the game’s fiery end, they can steal the prize money. Paranoia abounds! Did you hear So-and-So mention that there were “three” traitors? How could they possibly know that? Was that a slip? Did he look you in the eyes when he said, “I am one-hundred-per-cent faithful”? The descent into foolish and self-destructive groupthink provides the drama.

We know this game structure well: a group is dropped into a fishbowl environment with a charismatic host, who sets arduous tasks (physical, psychological, or both), and every evening the contestants convene, in a council format, to rant, cry, trade accusations, and, eventually, exile someone, all while a pot of cash sits on the horizon.

But however derivative of competition television “The Traitors” may be, the mood of the series is totally different—it is both quaint and greasy—because it so seamlessly matches our culture of incentivized fraud. Embedded in the production of the show is the feeling of being trapped in a hall of mirrors; scenes are edited flagrantly, drowning the viewer in red herrings. The manipulation tactics of the traitor are what you root for; the faithful exhaust you. “The Traitors,” which has won Emmys, is like “Jeopardy,” in a way. Jeering at the contestants for their naïveté and their shortsightedness, you feel like your brain is working.

The American version of “The Traitors,” which premièred in 2023, is hosted by a Scot, Alan Cumming. He is a scheming ringmaster, his eyes kohl-lined, his body wrapped in couture finery for which there are no descriptive words. He can roll consonants in a way that has not happened before in human speech. He makes a meal of the goth gig. He is too good to be doing this, to be on NBC on a Thursday. But his presence, his apparent delight as adults in scuba suits shoot themselves out of helicopters in pursuit of gold, is part of what makes the show so appealing. Against the singularity of Cumming, who brings the American players to his “home” country, for a game set in a handsome castle in the Scottish Highlands, the group always seems dinky, which is interesting, because it’s largely composed of B- and C-list celebrities.

The U.S. adaptation of “The Traitors,” with its gravestone and haunted-doll ambience, was seemingly lifted wholesale from the U.K. version of the show, which had been greenlit less than a year after the Dutch original. There’s just one critical difference: the U.K. version had cast all regular people, with regular jobs—except for that one magician, in Season 1. (“I’m one of the top magic creators in the world and I can read people; it’s my job,” he insisted, even though his predictions were regularly wrong.)

Initially, American producers tried to split the baby: half nobodies, half stars. But the hybridity didn’t take. The celebrities sucked up a lot of air, and so the show leaned in, switching to all-celebrity casts. The all-civilian versions—in the U.K., Ireland, New Zealand, and, at least at the beginning, Australia—have the thinnest veneer of social consciousness. For viewers, that is the undertow of “The Traitors”: the feel of a social experiment. Certain occupations, such as a teacher, make a contestant seem “smart,” whereas others—say, a lawyer—have an inherent shadiness. Women are suspected quickly. White people last long, etc. Communication is as fragile as glass.

What we have had in America, for the most part, is a social experiment conducted on a very particular population: reality-TV alumni. Instead of teachers and lawyers, the contestants are former “Bachelorettes” and “Biggest Losers”; the two main blocs are the “Gamers” (people who competed on shows like “Big Brother,” “The Amazing Race,” and “The Real World”) and the “Housewives” (self-explanatory). U.S. “Traitors” has been a reality show for reality-show stars, veterans of the genre, which is why it has typically been the least interesting version of the competition. The participants are dressed to the hilt by a surely overworked stylist, and they are only ever fake-indignant. The “Housewives,” such as Lisa Rinna (Beverly Hills) and Dorinda Medley (New York City), find themselves victims of a prejudice, of being seen as vindictive or else less strategically minded than the “gamers,” like Parvati Shallow, or Rob Mariano, a.k.a. Boston Rob, both alumni of “Survivor.” The winner of the last celebrity season was a traitor: Rob Rausch, a snake wrangler from Alabama, and a recent graduate of the romance den of “Love Island.” As a white-boy dreamboat in his overalls, he exploited the “low” reputation of dating shows in the entertainment hierarchy, managing to manipulate the faithful, winning in the end. On “Love Island,” he was seen as a liar, as a cad who, like, led on Leah just to dump her; on “The Traitors,” he committed a much worse betrayal, but to widespread approval and even admiration.

“The Traitors” is evolving. The U.K. is airing two versions this year: the typical civilian one, and its first celebrity spin-off. The Americans, in a twist, are the sole civilians. This season is called “The Traitors: New Blood.” (Celebrity traitors will return next year.) In “New Blood,” the fifth season of the U.S. show, “The Traitors” is now established enough for the cast to get self-referential. As the season opened, Kim Daily, a Black, sharply dressed trial attorney, said in her confessional that she would be telling the group that she is a basketball coach, obscuring her real profession. (Daily, obviously a viewer of the franchise, likely knew, too, that Black and brown players tend to go first.) But she can’t fool the cast, who immediately clock her as an intellectual threat; the traitors, sensing her influence over her fellow-faithful, murder her first. And so “The Traitors” is like a cheery hell for some players, who can’t, for all their suave maneuvering, escape the set rhythm of the show. Bias will win. Lessons are never learned. It’s great, cynical fun.

Joe Vanella, a crimson-cheeked funeral director from Long Island, is currently falling apart this season. He is playing the role of castle dad all while he connives in the turret alongside Tomica Adams, a pilot, and Katie Fites, a marketer. Joe is feeling guilty because he has grown close to Michael Foote, a gay lawyer and a faithful, befriending him by lying to his face. Sexuality and disability are the main “differences” that the production team allows to enter its ambit; the contestants rarely touch the third rail of race. (This past week, the banishment of the “Industry” actress Myha’la from U.K. “Celebrity Traitors” finally brought high-profile attention to the racial-bias question.) So Joe is flubbing missions, nearly blowing his cover, in one profoundly hilarious episode, as he tried to “murder in plain sight,” by tossing someone an apple. Overwhelmed, he eats the apple instead.

I don’t know if teary Joe is going to make it. I watch “The Traitors” with a blank mind, usually. But this week I found myself more alert to its theatre of lies. I think it’s because I’m observing the heated reactions to a film that owes some debt to reality television: “You Can See Everything,” Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s portrait of the former Theranos C.E.O. Elizabeth Holmes. One invective accuses the directors of destroying the truth-telling potential of documentary. Put another way, in bringing the footage into the realm of film, and not reality television—his domain—Fielder breached some ethical standard. This is a ridiculous complaint, though it’s rooted in a hunger for veracity that one can instinctively understand. In the film, Fielder installs himself at a beach house with Holmes; her partner, the vexing Billy Evans; and their two children, as she prepares to go to prison for fraud. He gets so close to Holmes, who claims innocence, whose steel opacity makes her his double, that he can no longer see her. And so, in Fielder fashion, after Holmes goes to prison, he restages or re-creates reality in order to try to get to some truth about her. He makes a Rube Goldberg machine of reality. But that doesn’t work. Can we trust him? Can we trust Holmes? Can we trust the relationship between Holmes and Evans? Is she manipulating her documentarian, her husband, or vice versa? If you saw that film expecting to gain an answer about Holmes’s innocence or culpability, then, yes, you came up frustrated. You want to live in a faithful world of councils, discernment, and banishment. But you are in the turret, the hall of mirrors. ♦

#350. Trump Says the Border Is Secure. Texas Gov. Greg Abbott Keeps Declaring an Emergency.

Last month, Texas Gov. Greg Abbott repeated a claim he’s made for the past five years. He stated that the number of people crossing the Texas-Mexico border illegally “posed an ongoing and imminent threat” across the state. In doing so, he renewed a border security disaster proclamation that has greatly expanded his powers without going through the state Legislature.

Abbott’s declaration stands in sharp contrast to the numbers reported by the Trump administration, which says the drop in the flow of migrants crossing the border has been unprecedented and has reached historic lows since it took over in January 2025.

Yet, since Abbott first declared an emergency at the border during the Biden administration in 2021, he’s renewed or amended the proclamation at least 69 times — 21 of those during Trump’s tenure.

The Republican governor, who is running for his fourth term next month, has consolidated power like no Texas governor before him, building on his predecessors’ efforts to strengthen what by design is supposed to be a “weak governor” state as the constitution limits what can be done without legislative approval, ProPublica and The Texas Tribune have reported. One of the methods he’s used to do that is issuing executive orders and disaster declarations that allow him to shift funds, suspend regulations and award no-bid contracts at times of emergency.

Since January 2025, two of the top agencies involved with border enforcement — the Department of Public Safety and the Texas Military Department — have awarded more than $5 million in noncompetitive contracts citing the emergency for items like a Ford F-150, rifles and apartments, state records show.

“I think it’s unprecedented,” said Cal Jillson, a political science professor at Southern Methodist University in Dallas. “I don’t think that there is a similar example, certainly not as to the length of the emergency, and certainly not after the emergency has seemed to pass.”

Abbott’s office did not respond to specific questions for this story, including about the noncompetitive contracts, but Deputy Press Secretary Madeline Waschak said in a written statement that the governor has been clear that border security is an ongoing priority.

“The damage caused by four years of failed border policies under Democrat rule did not disappear overnight, and the progress we have made will not sustain itself without continued vigilance,” she wrote.

“Securing the border requires constant, deliberate action to combat illegal crossings, cartel smuggling, fentanyl trafficking, and other criminal activity that threatens public safety in Texas. Governor Abbott will ensure the tools remain in place to safeguard our state as we work alongside the Trump Administration to deter, arrest, detain, and deport illegal immigrants,” she added.

At the time of Abbott’s first border declaration, four months into President Joe Biden’s term, Border Patrol was making more than 180,000 apprehensions along the southwest border in a single month. According to Abbott, the federal government’s inability or unwillingness to enforce immigration laws had created a humanitarian crisis in many communities in Texas.

“The Biden Administration’s open-border policies have enabled escalating violence from the cartels, dramatically increased the risk of human trafficking, and created a severe crisis of illegal drugs crossing into the United States,” the order stated.

Since then, apprehensions along the southwest border have dropped on average to about 8,000 a month this year.

The border security declaration remains Abbott’s longest continuously running emergency proclamation. Abbott similarly renewed his 2020 COVID-19 disaster declaration for three years, even as he downplayed the severity of the pandemic.

“This is the quiet power of the governor,” said Brandon Rottinghaus, a professor of political science at the University of Houston. “It’s not something often that they brag about because the more that it becomes a political issue, the more that there might be some contestation of it.”

Other than border security, the governor’s second longest-running emergency declaration is related to droughts. He declared a state disaster in July 2022 and within days amended it bringing the total of counties to 189. He’s continued to renew it, but now it applies to about a third of the counties originally identified.

State lawmakers have repeatedly tried — and failed — to curb the governor’s powers under the state’s disaster act. Last year, legislators from both parties filed at least a handful of measures that would mandate Abbott get approval from the Legislature if he wanted to extend a disaster declaration, but none passed.

Abbott’s emergency declarations have been accompanied by record spending on border security efforts. Texas budgeted over $11 billion during the Biden administration and allotted an additional $3 billion last year, after Trump took office.

It’s all part of Abbott’s Operation Lone Star, an initiative he launched in March 2021 contending it was needed to stem the smuggling of drugs and people into the country through Texas. Because of the emergency declaration, police could charge immigrants with criminal trespassing for crossing the border through private property, and the governor was able to shift money from other agencies to fund the operation and build border barriers, deploy the National Guard and get help from other states.

Much of the funding for Operation Lone Star is funneled through the Texas Military Department and DPS, two of the agencies responsible for major enforcement efforts. But the governor’s share of border spending has also ballooned since Abbott took office over a decade ago. State lawmakers allotted his office nearly $230 million last year to distribute through grants, largely to local prosecutors and government agencies helping the state with Operation Lone Star.

Though the situation on the ground has changed, one aspect of the emergency declaration has remained constant: the ability of state agencies to award noncompetitive contracts.

DPS and the Texas Military Department have awarded at least six noncompetitive contracts so far during the second Trump administration for work related to the operation where they cite an emergency. This includes a more than $2 million contract to Texas-based CTC Gunworks LLC for rifles and pistols for active-duty personnel. CTC Gunworks officials could not be reached for comment.

Officials with the Texas Military Department referred questions about its procurement process to the governor’s office. A DPS spokesperson said in a written statement that even though the disaster declaration allows for expedited purchases, the department still follows best practices and “aims to select vendors offering the best value, determined by cost, quality, and ability to meet the specific disaster response requirements.”

Regarding the specific contracts, the DPS spokesperson said they were purchases tied to existing state contracts.

The continued funding and expansion of Operation Lone Star has come even as it’s been plagued with problems. In 2022, an investigation by ProPublica, the Tribune and The Marshall Project found that the state’s reported success included arrests unrelated to the border or immigration and counted drug seizures made by troopers from across the state who were not directly assigned to the operation. The Tribune and Army Times also exposed poor working conditions, pay delays and suicides among National Guard members deployed as part of Operation Lone Star.

During a Tribune-hosted event this week with state lawmakers from El Paso, Democratic state Rep. Mary González, vice chair of the Appropriations Committee, said state legislators have already started to scale back when it comes to border funding and there’s interest from both sides of the aisle to continue to do so in the upcoming legislative session.

“I think that the problem is that some of that border security money still lies in the governor’s office which has no checks and balances and because we are still under an emergency — which is not an emergency anymore — an emergency border security statement which he can just use that money, you have seen some questionable appropriations under the veil of border security,” she said.

Abbott, however, has repeatedly said that Texas’ work in securing the border is not over.

“We must secure the border not just for the next two years of the Trump administration, but we gotta do it forever,” Abbott said at a press conference in early October where he discussed the federal government’s $7.5 billion reimbursement for border security expenses the state had incurred.

Rottinghaus said that Texas will always have to confront border crossings and drug trafficking, but the existence of those problems don’t always justify an emergency declaration.

“Even someone who strongly supports the governor’s board of policies might ask whether they want a future governor of the opposite party using the same interpretation of emergency authority for years,” Rottinghaus said, adding that the issue is that “governors, once they get a power, are hesitant to give it back.”
