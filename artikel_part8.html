#351. Your guide to the California insurance commissioner race: Allen vs. Kim

See more from the L.A. Times in Google Search. Set us as preferred

The race to succeed Ricardo Lara as California insurance commissioner following the devastating January 2025 wildfires is a classic study in contrasts.

It pits a veteran legislator, state Sen. Ben Allen (D-Santa Monica), serving his third and final term in the Senate, against an upstart progressive candidate, Jane Kim, a former San Francisco supervisor. They topped a packed field of candidates in the June 2 primary.

Kim was the leading vote-getter with 26.8% of the vote to Allen’s 19.6%, amid a progressive wave nationally that has led to Zohran Mamdani being elected mayor of New York and Abdul El-Sayed winning the Democratic nomination for senator in Michigan.

With widespread anger over how some insurers have handled claims stemming from wildfires in the state, Kim has stood out with a proposal to establish a nonprofit “single payer” insurance plan run by the state to cover catastrophic fire damage — something that never has been done.

Allen has pointed to a long legislative record seeking to reform the industry and make the home insurance market more attractive to insurers — something heavy on the details but hard to condense into a campaign slogan.

Allen, 48, represents the Palisades fire zone and has a lengthy history of insurance-related legislation, including leading the effort to put a successful $10-billion climate bond on the 2024 ballot, which he calls a key step in reducing fire risk and lowering premiums. A native and resident of Santa Monica, Allen attended Harvard and has a law degree from UC Berkeley. He previously served on the Santa Monica-Malibu Unified School District Board. He is endorsed by the California Democratic Party and leading elected party officials, including U.S. Sens. Adam Schiff and Alex Padilla.

Kim, 49, served as a San Francisco supervisor from 2011 to 2019. Her progressive credentials include leading a campaign to make the city’s community college tuition free and serving as California political director for Bernie Sanders’ 2020 presidential campaign. The daughter of Korean immigrants, she attended Stanford and also has a law degree from Berkeley. Though she ran as a Democrat, she is a former director of the Working Families Party and is endorsed by Sanders (I-Vt.) and the California Federation of Labor Unions.

The cornerstone of Kim’s platform is her call for the state-run property disaster insurance program, which largely would be funded by policyholders. Coverage would be universal, with policyholders automatically enrolled. With no need to generate shareholder profits, fund marketing or pay high executive salaries, she contends, the plan could better invest in fire prevention and make buildings and property more fire resilient. Such a drastic change in the insurance system would require legislation and support from the governor.

“Voters I’ve talked to don’t want to see the same old tinkering with the system, because most voters agree that it’s actually magical thinking that doing the same tinkering of the existing system is going to lead to a different result,” she said.

Allen is sharply critical of Kim’s plan, which he says is woefully short on details. Instead, he is focusing on the mechanics of improving the current system — for both homeowners and insurers. He wants to speed reviews of insurer rate applications, create a business office to attract more carriers to the state and work with elected officers at all levels to design programs that reduce fire risk of individual properties and at the neighborhood and community scale.

“I’m focused on what’s actually doable. The people who suffer from these terrible fires don’t need a 10-year experiment. Voters of all stripes, not just Republicans, are not interested in making the state a guinea pig,” he said.

With some insurers accused of delaying, underpaying and unfairly denying home insurance claims following the Los Angeles County fires, Kim wants to require insurers to immediately pay 100% of contents coverage in a disaster, stop rate hikes following a claim and conduct department probes of insurers with elevated complaint rates. She also wants guaranteed replacement costs for total losses.

“The way that I am approaching the framework of insurance is to really transform the insurance industry to work for consumers. I think my opponent and a lot of different political leadership has been fixated on how to make the market work for insurers,” she said.

Allen says he has sought to address those issues, including through a bill last year that increased up-front payouts in fire losses without requiring itemization by policyholders during states of emergency. He also cites a busy legislative session this year in which he was an author of bills the governor signed that give policyholders time to resolve issues that led them to be dropped by an insurer, require insurers to fix any deficiencies uncovered by department examinations and establish a low-interest loan program for residential fire-proofing.

“People are frustrated. I get it, and that’s why I’ve worked so intensely on taking on the industry through my time in the Legislature,” Allen said.

Both candidates support creating a “dashboard” where consumers can check on a carrier’s claims record before buying insurance from it.

Kim’s platform includes a plan to expand the state’s low-cost auto insurance program, which offers limited policies written by private carriers to low-income drivers, to all drivers. Kim also wants to centralize authority over health insurance programs within the department and is proposing a plan to guarantee healthcare for every child in California — two ideas that would need extensive legislative authority.

Allen also has talked about expanding the low-cost auto insurance program and has a number of proposals to improve insurance department operations. They include investing in data and digital infrastructure to better handle rate filings, consumer complaints and disaster claims issues. He wants to improve the department’s communications with the public, create the office of a consumer advocate and bar top staff from immediately working for insurers after leaving the department.

#352. Giants defense has a new motto after ‘traitors’ callout hits hard

See more of our coverage in your search results.

HOUSTON — The Giants defense was full of loyalists Sunday.

In the first game after coordinator Dennard Wilson publicly called out the defense — and held a private team meeting where names were named and film was shown — his unit showed few, if any, “traitors.” That’s what Wilson called players who don’t give a full effort running to the ball.

“Swarm — that was the word — and empty the tank,” Jason Pinnock said after the Giants beat the Texans 27-21 in overtime to rebound from an ugly hustle-lacking loss to the Saints. “That’s what we showed. I feel like the defense responded.”

“Swarm” is an interesting — but appropriate, given the timing — word choice for the Giants because it’s the Texans’ defensive motto. The Giants forced six punts and allowed only 237 yards of offense.

The biggest defensive stop happened in overtime, after Houston scored the game-tying touchdown with 52 seconds remaining.

The Giants won the coin toss and elected to kick off. Micah McFadden blew up a run for no gain, Kayvon Thibodeaux dropped an interception thrown onto his hip (incompletion) and Pinnock broke up a third-down pass that only got out of C.J. Stroud’s hand because of (declined) offensive holding.

“The biggest thing was energy,” Pinnock said. “Last week, the biggest thing was the ups and downs of the game. We were big on, ‘When something bad happens, have energy.’ It’s a momentum game.”

DJ Reader is an 11-year veteran. Short of forcing takeaways, did he consider that as good as a team can respond to being called out by its coordinator?

“I think so,” Reader said. “You heard about the callouts and we needed to look inward. Obviously, the beginning response is going to be a little machismo, but you really try to take in the things that the coach is saying. That’s a good way to respond as a team.”

John Harbaugh praised McFadden (10 tackles) in his first start of the season, Deonte Banks for his tight coverage and Abdul Carter for his “empty the tank” message.

“That was a players win in every way,” Harbaugh said.

The Giants tackled Harbaugh to the ground after the win — their fifth straight game decided in the last 20 seconds of regulation or overtime.

“Football is funny,” Reader said. “It always gives you a chance to redeem yourself. It’s going to come.

“We’re so happy to get a win. All that other s–t, it’s like arguing with your brother. It doesn’t matter anymore.”

#353. Serbia election: Vucic concedes defeat to student-led movement

In a seismic political shift, Serbia's longtime strongman leader Aleksandar Vucic has conceded defeat to a student-led movement in the country's parliamentary elections.

Serbia's longtime leader, strongman Aleksandar Vucic, conceded defeat to a student-led movement in the country's parliamentary elections on Sunday in what represents a seismic political shift.

High turnout and growing tension marked a hotly contested parliamentary election that was a showdown between the Balkan country's longtime leader and a diverse movement of university students who have led protests against him for almost two years.

The result brings an end to more than a decade of rule by Vucic and his Serbian Progressive Party (SNS).

Pre-election polls had suggested a tight race between the SNS and the newly formed, youth-backed Students' List, which emerged from a mass student protest movement.

"Based on a stratified sample, it is clear that we lost these elections," Vucic said as he addressed supporters at SNS headquarters in Belgrade.

In his concession speech, Vucic said projections had the youth-backed Students’ List taking between 135 and 140 seats, whereas the SNS was projected to win around 105.

Vucic blamed Serbia's independent media for the loss while also alleging interference from unnamed European countries and the "American deep state."

Celebrations erupted on the streets of the capital as soon as Vucic conceded defeat. Passing cars honked horns and crowds began to party near the election commission's headquarters.

At Students' List headquarters, supporters cried tears of joy, cheering, hugging and chanting — "Victory! Victory!"

Sunday's result is the culmination of nearly two years of protests after the deadly collapse of a train station canopy in the city of Novi ‌Sad in 2024.

The disaster killed 16 people and stirred widespread anger over alleged corruption, poor construction standards and a lack of accountability for public infrastructure projects.

Protesters accused the government of allowing corruption and political patronage to undermine public safety, allegations authorities have rejected.

Demonstrations demanding accountability have ultimately snowballed into a movement larger than anything Serbia has experienced since the fall of Slobodan Milosevic in 2000.

What began with university students staging blockades and marches soon drew support from teachers, farmers and other segments of Serbian society, turning demands for justice over the disaster into a broader challenge to Vucic's grip on power.

SNS won parliamentary elections in 2012 and has dominated Serbian politics ever since. Vucic himself first came to power as prime minister in 2014 before becoming president in 2017.

After calling snap elections last month, he stepped down as president to run for prime minister.

His second presidential term was due to expire in May 2027, and Serbia's constitution prevents him from serving a third term.

Critics have accused him of concentrating power, undermining independent media and weakening democratic institutions. He and his party deny the allegations.

The student movement has managed to channel the energy of the street protests into an electoral challenge the likes of which neither Vucic and his ruling party nor Serbia's established opposition has ever seen.

​Serbia's election commission put turnout at nearly 70%, the highest since the breakup of Yugoslavia in 1992.

The election was closely watched in Europe, where Serbia is a candidate for European Union membership.

EU Commission President Ursula von der Leyen congratulated the Students' List on their victory. "We stand ready to support reforms on the rule of law, media freedom, the fight against corruption and strong democratic institutions, and to work together to advance Serbia's future in the European Union," von der Leyen said on X.

Vucic maintained close ties with Russia and refused to join Western sanctions against Moscow over its invasion of Ukraine.

The vote comes six months after Peter Magyar's victory in Hungary , when he defeated Vucic's ally, Viktor Orban. Magyar congratulated Serbia’s student-led Students’ List on its victory in a post on X.

Serbia’s bid to join the European Union has been hampered by concerns over democratic standards and the rule of law. But the election result has prompted a positive response from another European, non-EU institution — the Council of Europe.

"The Council of Europe stands ready to work with Serbia’s democratically elected leadership to strengthen human rights, democracy and the rule of law," the organization’s Secretary-General Alain Berset said on X.

#354. AI is getting better at hacking power and water systems, new research shows

The world's most advanced AI models are suddenly capable of blueprinting cyberattacks on critical services like power grids and water systems with alarming success rates, according to new research.

Why it matters: Critical infrastructure operators are running out of time. Top-tier frontier models are dangerously adept at mapping out attacks, new research from cybersecurity company Frenos shows, and it's only a matter of time before freely available AI models make such hacks easier to execute.

  • "It's a clock that is moving faster than we've ever seen," Steve Swick, former top security executive at American Electric Power, told reporters during a briefing.

Driving the news: Researchers at Frenos tested 10 AI models on their ability to plan attacks against industrial systems, such as the software that monitors water treatment processes.

  • Three out of 10 models tested successfully constructed attack plans in roughly 90% of attempts.
  • Performance varied widely: Three models the researchers considered to be at the frontier of capabilities succeeded in just 17.9% to 31.9% of attempts.
  • The strongest foreign open-weight model succeeded 36.2% of the time, while the strongest U.S. open-weight model succeeded just 5.2%.
  • Frenos did not disclose the names of the models tested.

Zoom in: The research included nearly 17,000 tests, including 2,520 attempts to construct complete attack plans.

  • The models received information about potential targets and were tasked with piecing together the steps needed to achieve specific objectives.

The big picture: Critical services like water systems and manufacturing plants have long been targets for nation-state adversaries and cybercriminals.

  • Many operators face staffing and budget constraints. Testing and upgrading their systems can also risk disruptions to essential services that they can't afford.
  • Unlike traditional corporate networks, industrial systems can be sensitive to unexpected traffic and commands. Even an unsuccessful attack attempt could disrupt essential operations.

Threat level: Once attackers gain access to an industrial network, AI could help them quietly map out systems and identify weaknesses without the same time constraints facing human hackers, Frenos co-founder and CTO Harry Thomas said.

  • "AI doesn't have the problem with patience," Thomas said. "It doesn't need to eat. It doesn't need to sleep, and it doesn't need to use the restroom."
  • Paul Shaver, head of operational technology at cybersecurity firm Armadin, added that AI agents can already conduct reconnaissance and analyze large volumes of data without necessarily triggering security alerts.

The intrigue: Thomas said the researchers used a relatively simple setup to guide the models through their tasks. More sophisticated tools that provide additional context and feedback could improve their performance.

  • "A more capable harness could have given these AI models more of an edge," he said.

What to watch: Several initiatives — including from the White House, frontier AI companies, and cybersecurity organizations — have cropped up in recent weeks to help lower the costs and provide resources for critical infrastructure organizations.

#356. Two storms slam northern Europe, cut power to 150,000 homes

Storms Boelo and Austen kill a man in Scotland and cut power to thousands of homes in the UK, France and Germany.

A pair of storms battering northern Europe have killed one man, cut power to more than 150,000 homes, and disrupted flights and phone networks.

More than 82,000 homes in the United Kingdom, nearly 60,000 in France, and at least 10,000 in Germany had no electricity on Sunday after storms Boelo and Austen passed through the region within 24 hours, according to authorities in the countries.

A falling tree killed a man, 60, in southwestern Scotland on Sunday, while another falling tree injured two women in central Scotland, the Press Association reported.

Winds of up to 135 km/h (84 mph) were recorded in the Argyll and Bute regions of western Scotland. Trees and power cables brought down by the storm blocked roads and rail lines across Scotland and northern England.

In Brighton, on England’s southern coast, Austen blew away an unused pier on Friday.

Authorities have warned of flood risk in regions of both Scotland and England.

The Met Office, the UK’s national forecaster, warned that Boelo would bring a danger to life from flying debris and crashing waves and said people should stay indoors as much as possible.

Across the English Channel, Europe battled its first big storm of the autumn, with strong winds inflicting widespread power cuts.

French energy company Enedis said that at Austen’s peak on Saturday, some 288,000 homes had no electricity. Some 59,000 remained without power at midday Sunday, mainly in Normandy and Brittany.

A marathon runner in the midst of a race in northern France was lightly injured by a tree knocked over in strong winds.

In Germany, Austen cut power to 10,000 homes in and around the southwestern town of Freiburg-Breisgau on Sunday. The town’s main hospital said it had to limit service to emergency cases because of the blackout, which also cut the mobile phone network.

German police said two people were seriously hurt on Saturday by scaffolding blown away by Austen’s strong winds at a market in the town of Nastatten.

The storms also hit the Netherlands and Belgium, where falling trees injured four people in Brussels on Saturday.

In the Netherlands, north of the island of Schiermonnikoog, three crew members on board a freight ship were injured in the storms, according to the country’s coastguard. Two of them were winched off and taken by helicopter to a hospital on the mainland.

The storms caused hundreds of flights to be cancelled or diverted across northern Europe, including at Amsterdam’s Schiphol airport, one of the continent’s busiest hubs.

Experts have warned of more extreme weather events due to climate change.

#357. The Great Schlep

Yoram Ariely left Israel more than thirty years ago and found his way to New York City, where he worked in the food-and-beverage industry. “Mostly juices,” he told me. He eventually retired to Florida, but on Wednesday he was back in New York, sitting in the departures terminal at Kennedy Airport. Ariely is eighty-four years old; when I approached him, he requested that I give him a moment so he could put in his hearing aids. His journey, which had begun that morning, at 7 A.M., in Sarasota, would end up totalling twenty-four hours. He already looked exhausted.

Ariely, along with others clustered around Gate 18B, waiting to board a non-stop flight to Tel Aviv, was returning to Israel to cast a ballot in the country’s upcoming election—the first time Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu will face voters since his contentious effort to neuter Israel’s judiciary; his failure to prevent the Hamas attacks of October 7, 2023; and his prosecution of the ensuing war on Gaza, which has shredded Israel’s international reputation and resulted in a warrant for his arrest, for war crimes, from The Hague. “This is the most important election of my lifetime,” Ariely said. (Given that he’s six years older than the Israeli state, his lifetime includes every previous Israeli election.) It will be the first time he’s voted in Israel since moving away.

Around eleven per cent of the Israeli electorate lives abroad. The country does not permit absentee voting, except in rare cases, so any citizen who wants to cast a ballot must return and, on election day, go to the designated polling place for their last official in-country address. This puts a huge price, in time and money—often hundreds of hours and thousands of dollars—on casting a single vote. For Ariely and many like him, it is no deterrent. Eight in ten Israeli voters who live abroad share his view that the upcoming election is “the most crucial” in Israel’s history. Polls show Netanyahu’s right-wing coalition in a dead heat with his chief rival, the centrist Gadi Eisenkot.

Ariely is to the left of Eisenkot politically, but “these considerations must be put aside to give him the majority,” he told me. A man named Hiam Simon, a Jewish American dual citizen who lived in Israel for a stretch in the seventies, piped up from the row of seats opposite us. Ariely, he said, should throw his lot in with the Democrats, a smaller party that would likely become part of Eisenkot’s government, so as to “pull the coalition to the left.”

Ariely was booked in a block of coach seats that had been reserved by the America-Israel Democracy (AID) Coalition, a nonprofit based in Pennsylvania that seeks to mobilize Israeli citizens and “concerned Americans” in “preserving the democratic foundations of the state of Israel.” (Simon bought his own ticket, in business class, on the same flight.) The AID Coalition was formed in 2023, after Netanyahu proposed that the Knesset, by majority vote, should be able to overrule Supreme Court decisions. That move sparked enormous street protests in Israel, and alarmed many Israeli citizens overseas.

When the election date was set, this past July—spurred by the legal mandate that Israel hold a parliamentary election at least every four years—the war that Israel and the U.S. had launched against Iran was in its fifth month. Many foreign airlines had paused service to Israel. On those airlines that hadn’t, prices were steep; in May, a round-trip coach seat on our route from New York was running as high as twenty-five hundred dollars. The AID Coalition launched an initiative, Fly&Vote, to help people get back to cast their ballots.

Officially, AID is nonpartisan and assists any eligible voter, irrespective of their political leanings. “We don’t ask,” Batell Blaish-Sultanik, the group’s executive director, told me. Under Israeli election law, it is illegal to provide free or subsidized trips. AID bought blocks of seats on Arkia Israeli Airlines at a commercial rate, securing around a hundred seats on each of five inbound flights before the election and five outbound flights after it. Tickets were then sold to voters at set prices at the lower end of the market rate—topping out at nine hundred dollars each way. Hagai Nassau, an environmental consultant in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, who left Israel as a five-year-old and now sports a graying beard, told me that he had glanced at flights on El Al “and it was, like, three thousand bucks. So I was, like, I’ll do Fly&Vote.”

Of course, for some overseas Israeli citizens, price is no object. Soon after chatting with Nassau at our gate, I spotted Neri Oxman, an Israeli-born architect and the wife of the hedge-fund billionaire Bill Ackman, by a barista counter in the Centurion Lounge. Ackman was sitting nearby. Oxman told me that she saw no option but to return for this election. “It’s so important,” she said, stiffening up like a soldier. “I’m doing my civic duty.” They would be flying El Al.

More than thirty-five thousand overseas Israeli citizens registered to receive information about the AID Coalition’s reserved seats, and for access to a bespoke search engine that had been programmed to find other, affordable options, with complicated itineraries and long layovers. AID estimates that more than sixty thousand will ultimately vote on October 27th.

Netanyahu’s Likud Party clearly regards overseas voters as an obstacle—perhaps even an enemy. While there is scant survey data on the political leanings of Israelis who live abroad, they tend to be secular and more educated, and it is assumed that they skew anti-Netanyahu. More than a quarter of a million people have left the country since 2023. In August, Likud’s attorneys accused the AID Coalition of bribing voters, and brought legal action to try to block its efforts. Three weeks before the election, the chairman of Israel’s Central Elections Committee dismissed the case, but the AID Coalition agreed not to buy more blocks on additional flights. Over the summer, anonymous sources inside Israel’s Transportation Ministry, which is helmed by the populist Likud provocateur Miri Regev, leaked to Israeli media that officials were plotting to reduce capacity at the country’s only major international airport in order to stop anti-Likud voters from arriving. (The AID Coalition has distributed the phone numbers of attorneys to call if any of its passengers run into trouble at passport control.)

Besides the Fly&Vote initiative, several other efforts to bring in voters seem to be under way. A group called Mind-IL is running something called Israeli Science and Academia Week—fifty-nine scholarly conferences, ranging in topics from A.I. to zoology, which all conclude the day before the election. Organizers expect to draw well over three thousand participants, many from overseas. Guli Dolev-Hashiloni, an Israeli-born scholar and activist who is flying in from Berlin, where he is a Ph.D. student in Jewish history, categorized the conferences as “a very good subversive act of Israeli academia that enables people to come back to vote without taking vacation days.” Ran I. Snitkovsky, a member of Mind-IL’s steering committee and a professor at the business school of Tel Aviv University, insisted that any intimation that he and his colleagues were gaming the electoral system was unfair. “We realized there will be a lot of people that want to be here, and we wanted to take advantage of that,” Snitkovsky told me. The result, whatever the intention, will be hundreds of foreign-based academics flying to Israel the week of the election, many on stipends from their universities. Meital Avivi-Reich, an Israeli-born expert on the science of speech perception, who teaches at CUNY, applied for fifteen hundred dollars in funding to present her research at one of the Mind-IL conferences. That will help with the airfare, she told me, but she still plans to stay with her mother and then crash with friends during her shoestring voting-cum-conference trip, doing “a night here, a night there.”

More generous travel budgets will be provided to overseas tech workers flying to Tel Aviv for Tech1, a conference of some seventeen hundred attendees, a fifth of whom come from abroad, that focusses on A.I. and cybersecurity. Last year, the event, which is billed as annual, was held in May. This year, it starts in October—on the day after the election. Tech1’s organizers have, like Snitkovsky, asserted that the timing was adjusted simply to take advantage of the fact that so many expat Israelis would be flying in to vote. Maybe so. Tech1 turned down my requests to interview its organizers, though they did encourage me, repeatedly, to buy a ticket to the conference.

In an election that could hinge on a single seat, those pulling for the opposition think—or at least hope—that the overseas contingent may put it over the top. With nearly forty parties vying for a hundred and twenty seats in the Knesset, a complicated and fractious process of coalition building will commence after the votes are tallied. A few weeks ago, the liberal newspaper Haaretz, in a lead editorial, issued “an urgent plea to Israelis abroad” to “come home and help save our democracy.” Dahlia Scheindlin, a veteran pollster who works for left-of-center candidates and parties in Israel, speculated that the number of returning overseas voters was unlikely to be big enough to change the outcome of the election. Still, she was quick to add, “I wouldn’t discourage any of them from coming.”

Snitkovsky, of Mind-IL, anticipates another draw for those travelling back: group-therapy sessions for highly educated Israelis to process the post-2023 brain drain. More than one in ten Israeli Ph.D. holders already live abroad. In a recent survey, half of medical residents told pollsters that they aim to practice overseas after completing their training. Itai Ater, an economist at Tel Aviv University, who earned his Ph.D. from Stanford, found that more than thirteen hundred doctors have left Israel in the past three years—about one out of every twenty-five. The rate of emigration by physicians has nearly doubled since the twenty-tens.

On the AID Coalition flight, I met Danielle Gutman, a forty-one-year-old with a nose ring, a Star of David tattooed on her right forearm, and a Ph.D. in biology, from the University of Haifa. As a graduate student and young mother in Israel, she had been appalled that her kids were being taught creationism in public school. Still, Gutman wanted to build her life in the country. The judicial overhaul made her reconsider. She got a job at a biotech company in Philadelphia, based partly in Israel and partly in the U.S. On Tuesday, she’ll be voting for the Democrats. (Gutman had built a local protest group of Israelis in Philadelphia. Of the sixty or seventy involved, she told me, somewhere over the Atlantic, “all but ten are going back to vote.”) Losing the election, she said, with a look of stricken disbelief, would turn her native country “into Afghanistan, or Iran.”

Ater, in his paper “Recent Findings on Israeli Emigration,” noted that “although the recent increase in emigration from Israel is significant and concerning, the current number of emigrants does not yet constitute an immediate threat to the Israeli economy.” When I spoke to him by phone, he sounded more panicked. He recalled recently attending an international conference with brilliant colleagues from Russia, Turkey, Hungary, and Poland, and realizing that none of them still lived in their home countries. He feared that Israel would meet the same fate. He spoke with alarm of the risk of “spiral collapse.” If Netanyahu is reëlected, he said, he, too, may leave.

A few weeks ago, I spoke to Shachar Kariv, a former chair of the economics department at U.C. Berkeley. Born in Israel, he would be flying in to attend a Mind-IL-organized conference, and so he could vote. After teaching his weekly class on the Thursday before the election, he would travel halfway around the world—through ten time zones—and fly back in time to teach his next one. He’s a dual citizen, and his California ballot was sitting on his desk, waiting to be filled out and mailed, for free, in its postage-paid envelope. (Like most Californians, he votes by mail.)

I was curious why he was going through so much trouble to vote in Israel. Kariv is a game theorist. Shouldn’t he know better? “In game theory,” he told me, “there is a notion called the pivotal player. A player in a game is pivotal when his actions are both necessary and sufficient to establish something. Game theorists are always asking why people vote, because the likelihood that you are going to be what we call a pivotal voter—a voter that changes the election—is basically zero. Yet still people are participating.”

Game theorists have been arguing about this phenomenon, dubbed “the paradox of voting,” for decades. There is still no consensus on why billions of people routinely cast ballots even though it seems to be irrational. Perhaps the reassuring sense that one is part of a community is the real driver? Others think that the reward of not having to regard oneself, in Kariv’s words, as a “selfish bastard,” is enough of an enticement.

Kariv is a veteran of the Israel Defense Forces and the Shin Bet, Israel’s intelligence agency, but he has been horrified by recent events: “When I see what is going on in the West Bank with the settlers—I would say, with the support of the current government—this is something that I cannot accept.”

“I feel it’s my moral obligation,” Kariv told me. “I would not be able to look myself in the mirror if I didn’t vote. I just think that, if we lose the moral ground, we lose everything.” ♦

#360. Your guide to the California state schools superintendent race: Barrera vs. Shaw

See more from the L.A. Times in Google Search. Set us as preferred

The office of state superintendent of public instruction is classified as nonpartisan, but that’s not how the candidates line up: Sonja Shaw is a conservative Republican whose views align closely with those of the Trump administration; Richard Barrera is a liberal Democrat backed by the California Teachers Assn. and closely aligned with its views.

They are vying for an office with little clear authority and an uncertain future. In June, the Legislature signed off on Gov. Gavin Newsom’s plan to reshape the position. By nearly all accounts, the state superintendent’s power has been reduced — and the authority of the office already was somewhat limited. Many of the new duties have yet to be worked out.

But that doesn’t mean the two candidates are any less motivated. And the California Teachers Assn., as the main source of campaign spending, also is invested in the outcome.

Leaving the office is two-term incumbent Tony Thurmond, who was barred by term limits from serving again and ran unsuccessfully for governor.

California’s general election takes place on Novemer 3. Learn about L.A.’s city and county races as well races for state office.

Although the contest is defined as nonpartisan — and party affiliations won’t be listed on the ballot — the candidates’ affiliations are likely to decide the race, in the view of political analysts.

In the primary, voters appeared to hew closely to political party lines. Shaw finished first with 22.6% of the vote — apparently consolidating the support of conservatives and Republicans. Barrera, who had 20.3%, split a much larger swath of more liberal votes with six other competitive Democrats. Together the Democrats in the primary garnered 67.6% of the vote.

Democratic voters generally are expected to consolidate behind Barrera in the general election and sweep past the smaller number of Republicans. Shaw hopes to beat the odds, however, by appealing to independents and centrist Democrats, especially with positions on culture-war issues that she thinks will resonate in her favor with parents.

The state superintendent always has had a challenging role — with limited authority over school districts, which are locally managed. Instead, the officeholder managed the California Department of Education, which guides local school districts and also provides partial oversight. In that capacity, the state superintendent also worked in close conjunction with the state board of education — appointed by the governor — and often in concert with the governor and Legislature.

But under a new distribution of powers, the next state superintendent will lose control over the education department. Newsom has consolidated authority over education under the governor, and the education department now will be run by an appointee of the governor. The elected superintendent is reimagined as a student “champion” who would analyze and report on the effectiveness of the education system and also take on an advocacy role.

The Times submitted surveys to the candidates. Unedited excerpts from the surveys, in which candidates lay out detailed goals and policy analysis, can be found at the links below.

Shaw, 44, is the president of the Chino Valley Unified Board of Education, overseeing a relatively large school system of 26,000 students in San Bernardino County.

Barrera, 59, is the president of the San Diego Unified Board of Education, which governs the state’s second-largest school system with about 113,000 students. Barrera also serves as a senior adviser to state Supt. Thurmond, whom Shaw has sharply criticized.

Both are parents. Barrera’s children are grown; Shaw’s still are in school.

Shaw broadly characterizes the public education system as failing because of liberal Democratic policies, although she praises the quality of the public school system over which she presides. On the culture-war front, she supports flag bans at schools — which were conceived to target gay pride flags — and book restrictions that target content deemed as sexually obscene.

She became politically activated by the pandemic’s prolonged school closures, COVID vaccine mandates and mask mandates — all of which she opposed.

Despite a consistent policy alignment with the Trump administration, Shaw, a longtime religious conservative and a small business owner, objects to any implication that she is falling into line behind Trump or that she is subservient to his policies.

“I don’t put loyalty behind a person,” Shaw said. “I put my my effort and my love and my everyday sacrifice behind protecting kids, whether it’s this president, the next president or whatever. If they’re on the right side of protecting kids, then, of course, when it comes to the issues, I’m all in. I really honestly hate the division. In my heart, if we just speak on our issues, it’ll bring people together. We could see more eye to eye. We can actually have decent conversations, right?”

At times, Shaw presents a sharp rhetorical style, as when she characterized a September court ruling that placed limits on when school officials would have to notify parents about their child’s gender issues. Shaw believes such notifications should be mandatory. Her first emphatic comment, in an interview and a social media post after the hearing, which she attended, was: “Kangaroo courts. Dishonorable judges.”

Shaw sidesteps discussions about Trump’s immigration enforcement. “Districts should follow the law, communicate clearly with families and keep their attention on the classroom ... That’s where our energy belongs,” she said.

Barrera, in contrast, calls Trump’s immigration raids harmful.

“This assault on our immigrant families by the Trump administration absolutely is a real issue that affects students and affects educators,” he said. “And I’m going to stand up to those assaults, and Californians will continue to stand up to those assaults.”

Barrera defends the quality of public schools and advocates for increased funding and expanding what works from successful campuses to those with poorer academic results.

Barrera sees the superintendent as a convener and organizer to advance initiatives that benefit students — with or without state funding or direction from the governor and Legislature.

As an example, he cited the education department’s effort to promote affordable workforce housing projects on school district property. He also cited efforts to reduce chronic absenteeism, for which the department gathered a working group that has produced guidance for schools.

Barrera has a long career in community and labor organizing and worked as a senior official in two unions. That background, combined with his 18 years on the San Diego school board, where he has been a union ally, made him an ideological fit with CTA. Neither Barrera nor CTA had to compromise values or shift positions to join forces for this campaign.

“I have absolute respect and … a long demonstrated partnership with the unions that represent our educators,” Barrera said, “and believe strongly that unions are democratically elected, governed institutions where the educator voice comes out most powerfully, and so for me, the unions are a partner and will always be a core voice in influencing the way that I make decisions.”

Shaw called education Newsom’s biggest failure as governor.

“On his watch, academic outcomes have declined while the state focused on everything but the basics. When districts tried to course-correct, the response from his administration was litigation instead of support. He signed laws that cut parents out of major decisions in their children’s lives and backed policies that put ideology ahead of student well-being,” she said.

Shaw also is critical of outgoing Supt. Thurmond.

“In one of the wealthiest states in the country,” Shaw said, lagging student achievement “is not a funding problem. It’s a leadership problem. Under his watch, the focus shifted away from academic results and toward political priorities. The state fought parents, defended policies that put girls at a disadvantage and ignored clear signs that students were falling behind.”

Barrera praised Newsom for treating education as a “core priority,” and especially for creating a new public school grade level: transitional kindergarten for 4-year-olds.

However, he criticized Newsom for holding back $3.9 billion in funding this year that, under state law, typically would be directed toward education. Newsom defended the maneuver as justified because of the state’s potentially volatile revenue outlook and the need to provide resources to this year’s budget.

“Even when districts ultimately receive the money, that kind of maneuver complicates cash flow, raises uncertainty and pushes districts toward short-term decisions rather than stable strategies,” Barrera said.

Barrera praised Thurmond for helping to expand community schools, which provide academic support and nonschool services to students and families; for staying focused on whether policies and resources are reaching students with the greatest needs; and for defending students’ civil rights “in a period when many states have moved in the opposite direction.”

Barrera: The aspiration should be to fully inform parents, but the child’s wish should carry the greatest weight, especially as the child grows older.

California law allows sports participation based on gender identity, meaning transgender athletes have a right to participate in girls’ and women’s sports. Recently, the California Interscholastic Federation enacted a new policy for track and field competitions — offering honors and slots in competitions both to transgender athletes and to any biologically female athletes they would have displaced.

However, this approach affects only track and field and not team sports.

Barrera supports state law and agrees with allowing transgender athletes to participate on the teams of their self-identified gender.

“The practical work is implementation: Districts need clear guidance on their obligations, consistent coordination with the relevant athletic governance bodies, and a school climate where students can participate without being targeted,” he said.

For Shaw, this issue is central to her campaign.

“Girls are losing roster spots, medals and scholarships. Some don’t even feel comfortable using their own locker rooms, choosing to change in their cars before games rather than be forced to undress in front of boys. That’s not fairness. That’s failure,” she said.

Shaw demonstrated at a track meet to oppose the participation of a trans athlete — which won her plaudits from supporters who felt she was standing up for girl athletes, but criticism from those who felt she was publicly targeting a child for harassment.

#361. OpenAI brings its frontier models to rival Google Cloud

OpenAI is bringing its AI models and Codex coding tools to Google Cloud, expanding its reach to customers of all three major U.S. cloud providers — including companies that are also among its fiercest AI rivals.

Why it matters: The move underscores a growing recognition that winning the enterprise AI race isn't just about building the smartest models. It's also about making those models available wherever businesses already run their software and store their data.

  • For OpenAI, the agreement offers another way to reach corporate customers without requiring them to establish a separate relationship with the ChatGPT maker.
  • For Google, it means selling a competitor's AI models alongside its own Gemini offerings, potentially capturing business that might otherwise go to rival cloud providers.

Driving the news: Under the agreement announced Wednesday, Google Cloud customers will be able to purchase OpenAI's frontier models through Google's "Model Garden and Marketplace," including by using existing cloud spending commitments.

  • The deal also brings OpenAI models to Google's Antigravity development environment and Gemini Agent.
  • General availability is planned in the coming weeks.

The big picture: The agreement follows OpenAI's expansion onto Amazon Web Services earlier this year, after changes to its Microsoft partnership opened the door to broader cloud distribution.

  • AWS CEO Matt Garman said at the time that customers had long wanted to run OpenAI models where their existing applications and data already resided.
  • Amazon has since expanded the OpenAI models and services available through its Bedrock platform, although neither company has publicly quantified the additional business generated by the arrangement.

What they're saying: Mike Leone, VP and analyst at Moor Insights & Strategy, said the deal is significant for both companies, particularly because many Google Cloud customers want access to OpenAI models without moving their data.

  • "For Google, I read the deal as confidence, because Gemini now has to win those customers with OpenAI sitting right next to it," Leone told Axios.
  • But he doesn't see Google as necessarily hurting Gemini, since customers will still pay Google for its cloud platform, data services and security regardless of which AI models they choose.

Yes, but: Distribution is only part of the equation. OpenAI still has to persuade enterprises to choose its models over those from Google, Anthropic and other competitors, many of which are available through the same cloud platforms.

  • That could make the cloud providers' existing enterprise relationships and infrastructure as important as the models they offer.

Some 86 years ago, Missouri voters enshrined a system for selecting top judges that would remove political influence from the courts. And almost immediately, it came under attack.

Under what has come to be known as the Missouri Plan, lawyers help choose a slate of three candidates from which the governor must make an appointment for the state Supreme Court, appeals courts and, in some populous areas, the circuit courts.

In the first years after adoption, it faced pushback from party bosses who had historically used judicial seats for political patronage. They said it gave too much power to the lawyers. Voters, though, decisively defeated a repeal measure in 1942.

More recently, the conservative activist Leonard Leo, who viewed Missouri as a crucial battleground, tried in 2007 to persuade Republican Gov. Matt Blunt to reject a slate of Supreme Court candidates — part of a broader effort to weaken a judicial selection system that had become a model for some two dozen states around the country.

Blunt resisted and picked one of the three judges from the approved slate.

Then, in 2012, GOP lawmakers put a proposed overhaul before voters that, if approved, would have given the governor more control. Voters, by a 3-to-1 margin, turned back the plan.

Now the attacks are back, driven by a new grievance.

At President Donald Trump’s behest, Republican lawmakers redrew the state’s congressional map in a mid-decade gerrymander. The goal: to give the GOP a shot at winning seven of Missouri’s congressional seats, instead of the six it now holds.

But voters gathered enough signatures to force a referendum on the map and put it on hold. While Secretary of State Denny Hoskins tried to keep the referendum off the ballot, the Missouri Supreme Court overruled him. It ordered the old map restored until voters decide on the new one.

Hoskins, citing a federal court ruling that contradicted the state’s highest court, directed officials to use the new map anyway, prompting Chief Justice Brent Powell to demand that Hoskins appear in court to show why he shouldn’t be held in contempt.

Fourteen Republican lawmakers responded by threatening to impeach “responsible members of this Court” for “willful neglect of duty, incompetency and misconduct” if the justices held Hoskins in contempt; the court did so anyway. Hoskins backed down and instructed local election officials to distribute ballots using the old map; the justices decided not to have him arrested.

The drama played out as Republicans in Missouri and elected officials nationwide have sought to weaken institutions that limit their power. In North Carolina, the Republican-dominated General Assembly stripped authority from Democratic governors. In Wisconsin, Republicans threatened to impeach a newly elected Supreme Court justice to protect gerrymandered maps. And in New York, Democrats bypassed an independent commission to pass a gerrymander that favored them, but the state’s highest court struck it down.

Rather than electing top judges, as Illinois does, or giving the governor primary responsibility for choosing them, like California, Missouri selects its appellate and Supreme Court judges through a commission made up of three lawyers, one elected by lawyers in each appellate district; three non-lawyers appointed by the governor, one from each district; and a state Supreme Court judge selected by the court. After they are appointed, judges face a retention vote at the first general election after completing one year in office, then every 12 years, with voters deciding whether they keep their positions.

In response to the controversy, a number of Missouri Republicans have attacked the system. U.S. Rep. Jason Smith called for the state to switch to electing its Supreme Court judges. Republican Party executive director Miles Ross declared on X, “The Missouri Plan is done.” Dan Seitz, chief of staff to Sen. Brad Hudson, called for replacing it with a system in which the governor could nominate judges directly, subject to Senate confirmation.

State Rep. Cathy Jo Loy, a Carthage Republican who has introduced legislation in each of the past two sessions to replace the Missouri Plan with gubernatorial appointment and Senate confirmation, said she intends to file a new proposal Dec. 1, the first day lawmakers can file bills for next year’s session. Loy said her opposition to the system predates the recent clashes with the Supreme Court. But she acknowledged that those defeats could give the proposal new momentum.

“There will probably be more appetite because there are several senators that feel the same way,” she said.

Republicans, who hold every statewide elected office, supermajorities in both chambers of the legislature and six of the state’s eight congressional seats, seem to have suddenly found the limits of their power. At the ballot box, voters have overwhelmingly rejected major pieces of the party’s agenda, including an effort to weaken their power to bypass the legislature by amending the constitution or putting laws passed by lawmakers to a statewide vote.

Time and again, when elected officials have sought to limit voters’ use of the ballot initiative, the courts have protected voters’ use of direct democracy. Courts repeatedly rewrote ballot summaries prepared by Republican state officials that they found unfair or misleading, including language describing the initiative that ultimately established abortion rights in 2024.

By the end of that year, after the Missouri Supreme Court rejected a last-minute effort by Republican lawmakers and anti-abortion activists to keep the abortion measure off the ballot, Senate President Pro Tem Cindy O’Laughlin was calling to repeal the Missouri Plan. She proposed replacing the system for selecting Supreme Court and appellate judges with a nonpartisan election. But her bill stalled in committee.

Then came Hoskins. The former state representative and senator from west-central Missouri took office as secretary of state in January 2025 and brought a more combative approach to overseeing elections and ballot measures. Hoskins made a bright-red sport coat his sartorial trademark, often posing with a wood baseball bat and speaking about politics as a series of battles and himself as a conservative warrior.

Hoskins didn’t respond to questions sent to his office. He has previously said that he disagreed with any suggestion that he and Republican leaders were defying the will of voters. He hasn’t publicly called for changing how Missouri selects Supreme Court judges, but his office’s official X social media account reposted Smith’s message calling for the end of the Missouri Plan.

Gov. Mike Kehoe also complained, saying in a post on X that “unelected judges” have shown a “lack of respect for the legislative process.” On a radio show the next day, Kehoe acknowledged that changing how judges are selected hadn’t even been on his radar before the ruling.

“But I think it’s definitely something a lot of people are going to start asking about,” he said. “And I’m not sure the judges across our state, whether Supreme Court or other judges, really wanted that on people’s radar screen.”

Kehoe’s office said it would not respond to questions from ProPublica.

Although some Republicans have derided the state Supreme Court as being a bastion of liberal activists, five of the Supreme Court’s seven judges were appointed by Republican governors. Among them is Powell, whom the then-chair of the state GOP hailed as a “good conservative” before Republican Gov. Eric Greitens selected him in 2017.

Powell, who also serves as chair of the judicial selection commission, declined to comment for this story. In remarks on Sept. 17 to the joint annual meeting of the Missouri Bar and the Judicial Conference of Missouri, he warned of “threats from those who seek to detract from the ideals of our republic and to undermine faith in our sacred institutions — including our legal profession and our courts.”

“It is incumbent on all of us as lawyers and judges to rise up and defend our constitutions and the rule of law,” he added.

Whether there’s enough support to repeal the Missouri Plan remains to be seen. Peverill Squire, a professor emeritus of political science at the University of Missouri, said some of the bills that would eliminate the Missouri Plan might go to committee hearings, “but my guess is there probably won’t be enough appetite among enough Republicans to really want to put it on the ballot.”

Any change would ultimately have to be approved by the voters, something Loy acknowledged could be difficult after Republicans’ recent losses at the ballot box.

But the renewed calls have also mobilized the system’s longtime defenders. Ed Dowd, a former U.S. attorney who for two decades has helped lead the Citizens for Missouri Courts, a campaign that advocates for preserving the Missouri Plan, said the group has historically become most active when the system is under threat.

Dowd’s St. Louis-based firm, Dowd Bennett, recently contributed $100,000 to the campaign.

“It’s something that we take very, very seriously and don’t want to take any chances on losing something that we have that works so well,” he said.

#365. Protein shakes for teenagers: Good or bad idea?

Protein products are marketed as a way of building muscle and losing weight — and they're very popular with young people. They also claim to promote healthy growth in children. Is this true? How much protein is healthy?

You can take it as a powdered drink, or in a snack bar. Puddings and certain breads are also promoted as "sources of protein," and protein-enriched products like Kindershake and Teen Balance are specifically designed to appeal to a young people — and it appears to be working.

In a representative survey of almost 1,000 parents by the University of Michigan in 2024, just under half said their children, who were aged 13 to 17, had consumed protein products over the previous year. The survey found that boys were more likely to do so than girls, especially in connection with sport, or to build muscle. Girls took them to balance their diet, and to lose weight.

According to the statistics portal Statista, between March 2024 and March 2025 the fitness protein product market in Germany recorded a huge jump of around 25% in retail revenue.

Proteins are key building blocks for our metabolism. They play an important role in building up and repairing tissue in our muscles, skin and organs. Proteins are the basis for the formation of enzymes that regulate chemical reactions in the body, and of antibodies that are vital for our immune system.

According to the German Nutrition Society (DGE), healthy adults should consume 0.8 grams of protein daily per kilogram of body weight. A woman weighing 65 kilos should eat 52 grams of protein per day, for example.

But they can do this without special protein products. A portion of oat flakes with an almond drink for breakfast, a baked potato with curd cheese for lunch, and two slices of wholemeal bread with peanut butter in the evening — objective achieved.

Young people should consume a little more, as they are still growing. For them, the formula is 0.9 grams of protein per kilo of body weight. A teenager weighing 45 kilos should therefore eat 40.5 grams of protein per day. People over 65 and children aged one to four should have a little more: one gram of protein per kilo of body weight.

Older people tend to eat too little protein, often because their appetite decreases. But a study done in Germany between 2015 and 2017 showed that children and young people there often consume too much. Around half of six- to 17-year-olds ate more than double the recommended daily amount of protein.

Too much protein can be harmful in the long term, according to the DGE. Various studies show that children who eat too much protein, particularly animal protein, often become overweight. Some enter puberty early.

Above all, too much protein can put excessive strain on the kidneys, causing damage. The kidneys have to convert the amino acids that constitute the building blocks of proteins into urea before they are excreted in urine. Damage is particularly likely if a person doesn't drink enough fluids, or has pre-existing kidney damage — but adolescents are also at greater risk.

A person's protein requirement is greater if they are very physically active. Among other things, according to a DGE position paper, proteins can help maintain or build up muscular mass and strength, and support recovery after strain.

The paper states that, depending on physical condition and what they are training for, sportspeople should consume between 1.2 and 2 grams of protein per kilo of body weight, divided into several portions throughout the day. These recommendations only apply to people who train for more than five hours a day.

Proteins are of interest to people who want to lose weight because they are good at satisfying hunger. People usually lose more weight by following a protein-rich diet rather than another. Long-term, though, the most important thing to look at is: How many calories am I consuming, and how many am I burning?

Overall, plant proteins usually contain somewhat fewer essential amino acids than animal protein. "Essential" because the body cannot make them by itself – it has to take them in as food.

However, wholegrain products, pulses, or nuts are also higher in fiber, vitamins, and unsaturated fats – all of which are healthy. The DGE therefore advises that protein intake should be increased primarily with sources of plant protein.

Protein powders and bars are often enriched not only with vitamins and minerals but also with unhelpful ingredients like sweeteners, sugar, additives and flavorings. The German Sport University in Cologne even found doping substances in protein products that were praised for building muscle.

Adolescents in particular might experience an unpleasant side-effect of consuming protein powders. These usually consist of milk proteins. There are various ways in which milk proteins can increase the skin's production of sebum, and this can trigger or exacerbate acne, although a direct link between milk protein products and acne has not been definitively proven.

Specialists are also skeptical about protein shakes, fearing that they could lead to young people eating fewer normal meals, and thus consuming less fiber, micronutrients, and healthy fats.

Protein-enriched foods are usually expensive. In a 2022 study, consumer organizations also found that foods that are naturally high in protein, such as cheese, curds, or soya drinks, often cost more if the protein content has been highlighted by the manufacturer.

Not even sportspeople with a high protein requirement need to supplement their diet with special protein products. The DGE advises that they should aim to satisfy their requirements by optimizing their diet, and says there is no scientific evidence that our bodies require protein-enriched food supplements when put under strain.

However, the experts do say that it may make sense for people to use protein preparations if they have allergies, problems with digestion, chewing, or swallowing, or if their appetite is very low.

#367. A Mother on Trial

The moment I learned that I was pregnant, I began sleeping badly, waking up before daybreak with a feeling of vague dread. It was the summer of 2023. My oldest daughter, now five, had been the result of an ignominious I.V.F. “journey,” and her birth had been followed by years of infertility. In my mind, it was a foregone conclusion that my husband and I would never have another child. Yet there I was, forty-five years old and unmistakably storked.

I felt guilty for not being elated. I tried to describe my ambivalence to my husband: after all we’d been through, I wanted to focus on work; there was no way we could afford it; another child would disrupt our family dynamics. As much as I loved being a mother, I found myself secretly wishing the baby away.

It was in the midst of these discordant emotions that I found her, in an old article in People magazine. She stared at me from the screen. Her eyebrows were arched, making her seem shocked, or possibly recalcitrant. The headline—“N.J. Mom Found Guilty of Killing Toddler Son, Burning Body and Burying Him in Yard”—jolted me. “Nakira Griner reportedly admitted to police that she’d hit the child because he wouldn’t ‘eat nor listen to her,’ ” the writer stated. A neighbor was quoted as saying that Griner was, “like, the most genuine person in the whole world.” The woman went on: “My kids were close with her and her kids were close with me. I just don’t know what to do.”

I read the piece several times, then looked for more articles about this mother of two who had committed the ultimate transgression. A report on CBS News described her as an “aspiring YouTube personality” in Bridgeton, New Jersey. On February 8, 2019, she had called 911 in a panic to say that her toddler son had been kidnapped. Later, during her trial, she admitted through her lawyer to burning and dismembering his body, then hiding it in her back yard. I couldn’t get the image out of my mind—it was horrifying, unfathomable. I remember thinking, Who would do such a thing?

I usually spent my days editing a fashion journal, but, suddenly, my focus was elsewhere. I tracked down her videos on YouTube, in which she shared her love of clothes and designer handbags. In one video, uploaded around six months before her son died, she dutifully explained consignment shopping to her modest follower count and showed off her favorite monogrammed Louis Vuitton bag. I also found pictures of her late son, a toddler with dark, curly hair and a sweet expression.

As my belly grew, and resignation about my pregnancy turned into acceptance and then anticipation, I couldn’t stop thinking about Nakira. Earlier that year, she had been convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to life in prison without the possibility of parole. Nearly every afternoon, I Googled her to see if there had been any new developments in her case.

It wasn’t long before I began researching other stories about women convicted of killing their children. Mothers who beat, shook, shot, drowned, strangled, and stabbed their babies. Mothers who placed their babies in the microwave and turned it on. There were days when I did little else besides read about one harrowing scenario after another, cataloguing them on my computer. I scrutinized the photos of these women. Some glared back at me with a wild, defiant gaze. Others seemed meek, with downcast eyes that refused to meet the camera. I realized that I was looking for something in their eyes, a sign—anything that might explain how a mother could turn against her own child.

I would end up going down a rabbit hole, trying to understand. In the course of my research, I interviewed members of Nakira’s family and several attorneys and investigators who worked on the case. I spoke to sociologists and criminologists, legal scholars and mental-health professionals, all of whom study the intersection between motherhood and violence in one way or another. The experts emphasized that a woman’s childhood often plays a key role: isolation, neglect, abuse, and generational trauma appear in numerous filicide cases. Yet many studies have found that most women who commit filicide have, like Nakira, never been in trouble with the law before. When I discussed the case with Anna Motz, a British forensic psychologist who has worked with violent women in England for more than three decades, she observed, of her experience interviewing women who have been convicted of killing their children, “There’s always a bit of self-preservation. I’m not a monster, I’m not a bad person.”

Nakira’s mug shot showed a young Black woman with dark hair gathered at the nape of her neck, green eyes staring intently into the camera. I studied one photo of her wearing felted reindeer antlers and looking at her son, who is balanced on her hip. She was a mother, like the ones I saw every day at school drop-off, as I kissed and waved goodbye to my daughter in the morning.

The story of Nakira Griner’s crime and her subsequent trial had made the rounds on TikTok. Her case was shocking in the exact way that true-crime buffs appreciate: a nonsensical act; an innocent victim; an unlikely, photogenic perpetrator. But, aside from a dormant Facebook page and the YouTube videos, I couldn’t find much of anything about what her circumstances had been beforehand. I was curious about her past—her relationship with her own mother, what she had been like as a young girl. What had her expectations of motherhood been? And what was it, on that particular day, that caused her to snap?

On impulse, I decided to write to her. Nakira was now an inmate at the Edna Mahan Correctional Facility for Women, in Clinton, New Jersey. Writing an inmate is surprisingly straightforward in the United States—a company called JPay allowed me to correspond with Nakira at thirty-five cents per e-mail.

“Dear Nakira,” I began. “I’m a writer, living in Paris, France.” I ventured: “I would love to talk with you about your life, in and out of prison.” She wrote back the same day: “Um if i am being honest i’ve been approached by many, and i’m almost sure that there wil be more to come. And i am very skeptical about not only you but this whole process entirely.”

I appreciated her shrewdness. I’d read on prison-pen-pal websites that one should be tactful in asking about an inmate’s crime, so I stayed away from the topic. Instead, I asked her about life at E.M.C.F.W. “Prison is soo much better than jail,” she replied. “You can live here, feel the breeze on your skin here, get wet by the rain here, make snowballs here, get a suntan here, touch blades of grass here. Moral of the story I can live here.”

As we began exchanging messages, she asked me at least as many questions as I asked her, including “why this topic and why now.” By this time, I had decided to write about maternal filicide, and I tried to explain my reasons as best I could: “I became a mother myself some years ago, and with it the cultural and societal expectations and pressures of motherhood became very real.” I told her I had realized that rather than interrogating the underlying issues—the kind that might cause a mother to break—we prefer to scapegoat such women. “It’s the woman in the mugshot who is the problem,” I wrote. “She’s the monster. Lock her up and throw away the key. Because any ambivalence I might feel towards my own children, any sense of weariness, despair or ‘mom-rage’ is not at all the same because . . . I am not her.”

Over the following months, we built a kind of rapport. When I told her that I was pregnant, she thanked me for sharing. “I honestly appreciate that,” she wrote. “Because most people including family tend to hide things like that from me assuming anything baby related is a trigger.” When work got busy and I didn’t write to her for a little while, she worried about me (“knowing that you are 8 months pregnant i just want to make sure”). And when I gave birth to a baby girl, she congratulated me.

My eldest daughter turned six. She knew that I was writing to a woman in prison, and she was curious in the oddly specific way that kids can be—not about what Nakira had done (for which I was grateful, because what on earth would I have told her?) but about what she ate, what she wore, whether she was allowed to have stuffies in her cell. I shared my daughter’s questions with Nakira, and she diligently answered each one: she ate in her room; she wore a khaki-colored uniform; and she had a beloved Ike from “South Park” stuffie, made by another inmate.

By then, Nakira had begun sharing details with me about her childhood, her marriage, and her surviving son, whom I’ll call J.J. Nine months old when his brother died and his mother was first sent to jail, he was now the same age as my daughter, and, she said, he spoke to his mom over the phone every day. “When i need to smile i think about the times i currently have with J.J. on the phone & our many conversations,” she wrote to me, in May, 2024. “The terrible but super cute pictures that he sends me.” Then, in July: “The only reason I’m alive today is because J.J. is.”

Little by little, she also told me about Baby Dan, her late son, who had died a month shy of his second birthday. In one e-mail, I mentioned that my two-month-old baby refused to settle down unless I played Enrique Iglesias, and she replied that every time Baby Dan had needed a diaper change, she’d had “to rap a specific Kodak Black song for him called No Flockin or he would loose it.” Nakira told me that she preferred not to think about that time, though—the good memories or the bad. “The pain outweighs it all,” she said.

I never asked her exactly what had happened, and she never offered to tell me. She didn’t “knowingly, purposefully or intentionally” end her son’s life, she wrote at one point. Then, in the same e-mail, “accepting the fact that your child would still be here if it weren’t for you is inexplicably detrimental to the heart.”

I had been corresponding with Nakira for months when my husband and I decided to leave Paris for New York with our children for work reasons. But I knew that the move might also help me get to know Nakira better: in the U.S., I could speak to her on the phone, or perhaps even meet her in person.

In her e-mails, Nakira described a childhood “surrounded by squalor. Abandoned houses, empty syringes & bottles of liquor.” Her mother, Melissa Fuller, grew up in Vineland, New Jersey, and ran away from home at fifteen; she was eventually put in foster care. At seventeen, Melissa met an older, married man in nearby Bridgeton and became pregnant. Nakira James was born in 1994; her younger sister, Lasha Trussell, was born two years later, to a different father. The girls sometimes lived with their grandmother or an aunt, other times with their mother and Lasha’s father, who was a part of the sisters’ lives on and off until he was convicted of armed bank robbery in 2003.

Nakira told me that she was never really sure who her father was. When she was around ten, her mother started seeing a new man, and the family moved in with him; Melissa told Nakira that he was her father. It was Christmastime, and his house was full of gifts. Nakira was confused but decided to focus on “the pretty things.” They lived there for about a year until, one day, Melissa and her boyfriend had an argument; afterward, her mother blurted out that the man wasn’t her father, and she and the girls immediately moved out.

Following that experience, Nakira recalled being constantly on the move. “Me and my sister tallied it,” she told me. “We must’ve lived in at least twenty different places throughout our childhood.” Sometimes the family stayed in their car, but the girls were also left for long stretches with various friends of their mom’s. Nakira’s sister Lasha said, of their upbringing, “I was always confused.” Melissa “never told us when she was leaving, never told us when she’d be back.”

Lasha, a stay-at-home mom of three young children, lives in Florida now. Over the phone, she recollected her mother’s violent temper. “Everything we did, even something small, it always felt very big,” she said. “Like you could go in the fridge and drink a sip of juice that my mom wanted, and she’ll beat you bloody. And it’s for drinking juice, you know.” After remembering a traumatic moment, Lasha would giggle, short and sharp; she called it her “coping mechanism.”

Nakira, too, had vivid memories of their mother’s anger. At some point in elementary school, Nakira told me, her mother found a note from a boy in her pocket, saying how much he liked her. Irate, Melissa punched Nakira in the face, then used makeup to cover the bruises. She coached her daughter to say that she’d taken a snowball to the face, should anyone ask. (No one did.) There were some attempts at normalcy. In one e-mail to me, Nakira recalled “going to the Rite Aid early in the A.M.” with Lasha every day before school. They would brush their teeth and wash their faces in the public rest room while their mom waited in the car. On their way out, they would swipe a snack and a soft drink for breakfast. (Melissa denies her daughters’ accusations of abuse.)

When I called Melissa, she was on break at an Inspira Health hospital in Woodbury, New Jersey, where, at the time, she said that she was working as a supervisor. She told me that the family would move when she “couldn’t keep up on the rent,” and that this had happened only “around four or five times.” “I didn’t have challenges with my kids until they became teen-agers,” she said. “They really didn’t need too much discipline.” She continued, “I would give them that look and they know I’m not playing around.”

Nakira was often late for school or missed it altogether. Although she was a good student, she was self-conscious about her “dirty clothes” and “disheveled hair,” as she later told a forensic assessor. During breaks, she would hide in the girls’ bathroom to avoid other students. She imagined a future where she’d be famous, but, she told me, “most importantly I wanted to be a mother.” “I had so many toy babies & strollers. It was my favorite thing to play, A mom,” she wrote. “So much so that my great aunts had a problem with it surprisingly. And forbade me to keep playing a mom and be happy actually being a child.”

She was nineteen and working as a cashier at Wawa, a local convenience store, when she met her future husband, Daniel Griner, who was a customer. He was twenty-six and on a leave of absence from a nursing school nearby. “It was love at first sight,” she wrote. “I went home and told my mom if this white boy comes in one more time he’ll be my husband.” The couple got married on Valentine’s Day, 2017, and Nakira James, who was by then seven months pregnant, became Nakira Griner. Three weeks later, their son Daniel Joshua Griner, Jr., was born. Everyone called him Baby Dan.

Daniel, Sr., became a nurse at an Inspira Health in Vineland, and the couple began their married life in a two-story clapboard house on a quiet, leafy street about an hour away, in Bridgeton. Nakira had long craved stability, and for a brief, hopeful moment, it looked as if that was what she had: a husband with a good job; a baby boy; and a house of her own, where, she told me, she dreamed of keeping “report cards from when he was in kindergarten,” as there had been in her husband’s childhood home. “I was willing to really endure almost anything to give him that,” she said.

When her son was five months old, Nakira got pregnant again. Her first pregnancy hadn’t been easy: Baby Dan was delivered almost two months early because Nakira developed preeclampsia. Afterward, she told me, she had experienced postpartum anxiety, paranoia, and depression. She was acutely aware that a new mother should have an “overwhelming feeling of love towards the baby,” but she had “just felt numb,” she said. Her symptoms were never officially diagnosed, but she said that they never went away. During her second pregnancy, she told me, she developed a condition called hyperemesis gravidarum, which causes severe nausea and frequent vomiting. After another difficult birth, she found herself home alone with two babies. She wrote to me, describing “extreme paranoia & being set off by my babies cry” during those early days. “I couldn’t understand why it made me feel the way it did,” she added. Still, Nakira kept her struggles to herself. “She put on a good show when she talked to me,” her mother said. “That’s one thing I can say,” she went on. “We can act as though nothing is bothering us, but on the inside we’re dying.”

Nakira attempted to describe the turbulence inside her head to her husband. But Daniel worked twelve-hour shifts; when he got home, he would often stare at his phone, too exhausted to pay attention to his wife’s unravelling. Nakira told me that it was their willful toddler who bore the brunt of her emotions. Baby Dan tested the boundaries, Baby Dan defied his mother, Baby Dan threw tantrums and wouldn’t stop. She asked her husband for help with discipline, but he rarely stepped in, which became a source of contention between them. “I didn’t want to be the bad guy,” she told me. “I didn’t want to be seen as this mean, angry monster.” Nakira recalled times that she raised her voice at Baby Dan, or shouted. When shouting didn’t work, she slapped him. Eventually, she brought out a belt. Afterward, she said, she would feel shame, which would quickly turn into anger again. Now, in hindsight, she saw things differently. “It wasn’t anything he was doing,” she told me. “It was me. It was how I was perceiving what he was doing.”

Nakira still hadn’t told me what happened the day that Baby Dan died; she referred to it as “the incident.” She alluded to “mistakes” she had made, and the fear that led her to hide the events of that day. “I wanted to be better than my mother in every way,” she wrote, “& in every way I was, until I wasn’t.”

The road that leads to the Edna Mahan Correctional Facility for Women is long and desolate. I had taken my bike on the train trip from New York that morning, and, as I pedalled from the station, nondescript greenery flanked the highway under a dull gray sky. I was wearing a long black coat, which flapped in the wind as I rode.

Nakira and I were left alone at a small table in a big room, the prison press officer just out of earshot. The first thing that struck me was her eyes, which she had emphasized with eyeliner and white eyeshadow at the inside corners. She wore her khaki uniform, and her hair was ironed straight, with little curls glued to her forehead. She kept one hand in the pocket of her trousers. “Your accent surprised me,” she said. Was she assessing me as I was her?

We had agreed to speak about “the incident” in person only; the subject was, Nakira observed, “too taboo for the phone and email.” Every call from her was preceded by an automated voice reminding us that our conversation would be “subject to monitoring and recording,” and I knew that every e-mail we exchanged was also screened.

We made small talk about my ride out there, and about her phone call that morning with J.J. “Today, I was met with him telling me that he was sad,” she said. He had to go to the dentist to get a few cavities filled. “But if he brushed his teeth like his father told him to do. . .”

It took some time for Nakira to tell me about the day that Baby Dan died. It had been a regular weekday; her husband left for work early, as he always did, and Nakira was at home alone with the boys. “We were on our way downstairs,” she began, a little haltingly. She was holding J.J. on one hip, and Baby Dan was clutching her other hand. “He doesn’t want to walk so I sling him over onto my hip,” she told me, shifting into the present tense. “But something happens. He leans forward and he slips, like, he falls and he kind of tumbles. And obviously it’s a mad dash downstairs.” At the bottom, she said, “he’s bleeding from his mouth. He has a huge knot forming on his head.” She said that he seemed to be in shock: “I convinced myself that since he wasn’t crying that it was going to be O.K.”

For the rest of the day, Nakira said, she gave him all the snacks he wanted and fussed over him. He threw up, and, after he continued to do so, she told me, she Googled “children falling down stairs” and “symptoms of concussions.” She was looking for confirmation that there was no need to go to the hospital, where people would see bruises “all over his body and his little butt.” She told me that a few days before the fall, Daniel had put Baby Dan over his knee and belted him, as punishment for refusing to eat his dinner. “That was the first and only time that he ever did that, but that left marks,” she said. Nakira was afraid that the doctors and nurses would not believe that Baby Dan had fallen down the stairs, and that she and Daniel would be subjected to what she called “scrutiny, ridicule, the police.”

That evening, Nakira said, Baby Dan fell asleep early. When Daniel got home from work, she didn’t tell him what had happened. She turned the baby monitor on in Baby Dan’s room, but, as the hours passed, she didn’t see him stir.

My palms became clammy in my lap as I sensed what was coming. “When I went in the room the next morning after my husband had gone to work . . . um . . . he was cold,” Nakira said, looking down. “I remember holding . . . I remember holding him and attempting to do CPR even though . . . even though I knew it was too late. And the contents . . . of his stomach . . . coming up . . . in my mouth.”

She was crying, so I offered her some toilet paper from my pocket, which I had hastily ripped off a roll in the prison rest room. She balled it up in her fist and dabbed at her face. Little bits of tissue paper got stuck on her chin, her temple, the tip of her nose.

I had asked her about J.J. “I can’t even tell you what he was doing,” she said. “I remember walking to the bedroom and pulling the gun from underneath the bed.” She told me that she “was going to go with” Baby Dan. “Like, wherever he was, I wanted to be,” she said. Her voice was barely audible. “And that’s when J.J. wailed. He just screamed, like, an unnatural scream. It brought me out of that trance and I went to him. And all he wanted was his bottle.”

Nakira acknowledged that Baby Dan had died because of her. “I carry the weight and I take the blame,” she told me. “Those were my decisions, and my lack of action led to his demise.” But the story that she articulated to me was a crime of omission: the help she never called for, the truth she hid, the lies she told. The other part, the crime of commission—what she did to his body—remained too horrifying to put into words. Only two memories stood out, she told me: the smell of something burning, and the image of herself “digging, clawing at the earth in an effort to hide what I had done.”

In the distance, I spotted a woman dressed as if for a cocktail party, wearing a canary-yellow brocade coat and bright-yellow strappy stiletto sandals. The press officer told me that she was there to introduce a lecture on female empowerment to the inmates.

“There were times that she broke down and cried to me,” Daniel Griner, Sr., had told me over the phone. “I guess I didn’t know what to do or how to help her. I didn’t have the solution. So I kind of ignored it.”

Daniel is tall, with close-set eyes and an affable manner. He lives alone with J.J. now, in a one-story house in a different New Jersey town. A few weeks after my visit with Nakira, I went to see him there. We sat down in his living room, where Fox News played silently in the background on a large flat-screen TV. He fiddled with his Under Armour cap, worn backward, taking it off then putting it back on again whenever the subject turned difficult. Over the phone he had told me that Nakira “didn’t want people to think she couldn’t handle being a mom.” He said, “That was the biggest thing. She didn’t want people to judge her.”

It was a weekday. J.J. was at school, and I had promised to leave before he got home. Daniel told me that J.J. has started asking questions about his mom: Where is she, exactly, and why isn’t she here? Daniel keeps his answers vague. “It’s going to be really hard for me to tell him everything that’s happened,” he said. “I’m scared that it’s going to affect his feelings for his mom, and I don’t want that to happen. ’Cause she loves him.”

We began talking about the day that Nakira was arrested. The night before, Daniel had come home from work at dinnertime. “It was kind of strange because the kids were both in bed already,” he said. Daniel ruminated about Baby Dan. “I could’ve gone in there and kissed him, but she said that he was sleeping and I didn’t want to wake him up,” he told me. “I thought it was kinda strange because I looked at the camera, and, you know, he was, like, in the same position all night.”

In the morning, Daniel left without seeing the boys; later that day, he told me that he had a “regular conversation” with his wife. As he was about to head home for the day, though, he got a missed call from Nakira. He tried calling her back, but she didn’t pick up. “I knew something was wrong,” he said. “She always answers the phone.” Daniel asked his mom to go to the house; when she arrived, the police were there. Baby Dan was missing, they told her, and Nakira was at the station with J.J.

I had asked if I could see the legal files from Nakira’s case, and as I left his house Daniel handed me a big plastic bag from the supermarket Aldi. Inside was a tumble of papers, some moldy, some with partial footprints, leaves and twigs interspersed among the pages. He had kept the files in the back of his truck since Nakira’s conviction.

On the recording of the 911 call from that day, the operator’s steady voice is in sharp contrast to Nakira’s frantic crying. “You’re hiding?” the operator can be heard saying. “Who are you running from?” Officers were sent to the street corner from which Nakira had placed the call. She told them that she had been walking to Walgreens to get Pedialyte, with J.J. strapped to her chest and Baby Dan in a stroller, when someone pushed and kicked her from behind and then, she said, “snatched my stroller and my baby in it.” The officers took her to the station for questioning.

An extensive search was launched for Baby Dan, with about fifty people from the fire and police departments. After several hours, according to the police report, an officer found a partially buried Hello Kitty purse in the back yard of the Griner home, at 21 Woodland Drive. Inside was a tube of MAC lipstick and six layers of white plastic bags containing something that resembled “cooked meat.” On closer examination, the lead investigator saw what appeared to be “the remains of a skull, one foot, and one leg consistent with a child.” The search for Baby Dan was called off.

The next day, February 9th, Nakira was charged with first-degree murder, endangering the welfare of a child, desecrating human remains, and tampering with physical evidence. She was held at Cumberland County Jail in an isolated cell, allowed only a thick, tear-resistant suicide-prevention gown and mattress, and a Styrofoam tray. I thought of a line from one of her earliest e-mails: “The overwhelming feeling of loss makes you either literally insane, suicidal or numb and I at one point have felt all of those things.”

An affidavit drawn up by a detective with the major-crimes unit at the Cumberland County prosecutor’s office stated that Nakira called her husband on March 5th and told him that “she did what she did do” for the sake of him and their son J.J. The detective went on, “She said Daniel Griner Jr. had bruises as a result of Daniel Griner Sr. hitting him with a belt, and that [she] was trying to cover up the injuries.” The detective also noted that Daniel had “admitted to striking Daniel Griner Jr. with a belt earlier in the week.” The police found no evidence that Daniel had inflicted the injury that caused his son’s death or “any evidence that Daniel Griner Sr. participated in the fabricated story about the abduction.”

At first, Nakira was assigned a public defender named Kim Schultz. According to Jill Cohen, a private attorney who represented Nakira during the trial, Schultz tried to build a case that Daniel, Sr., had caused Baby Dan’s death, and that his wife was covering for him. The forensic assessor compiled a psychological evaluation of Nakira, which concluded that she suffered from “Major Depressive Disorder, Recurrent Episode, with peripartum onset, as well as Battered Woman Syndrome.” It pointed to her accounts of being grabbed by the throat, punched in the legs, and psychologically and verbally abused by her husband. (Daniel did not respond to a request for comment on striking Baby Dan with a belt, or on abusing Nakira.) I was taken aback—Nakira had never mentioned Daniel being physically violent—and asked her about these incidents. “I didn’t want to be seen as some type of victim of domestic violence,” she said. “Yes, these things happened, but they didn’t happen in the light that I felt she”—Schultz—“portrayed them.” She had instantly felt wary of her attorney’s strategy. “Her defense of me was going to be to drag my husband down,” she told me. “And I didn’t feel as though that was necessary. Nor did I think it was going to work out in my favor at all.” Daniel used savings and took on extra shifts to afford hiring Cohen.

By the time that the trial began, on December 12, 2022, a fifth charge had been added: creating false public alarm. In the prosecutor Elizabeth Vogelsong-Parvin’s opening statement, she painted an eerie picture. When police officers arrived at the Griner residence, a few details struck them as odd right away: even though it was cold out, the windows were open, the fans were running, and there was, Vogelsong-Parvin said, “a burnt smell in the home.”

Cohen had the unusual task of acknowledging that she was not going to fight four out of the five charges in question. Nakira did do “all those terrible things to cover the evidence,” Cohen told the court in her opening statement. But “the one thing that you can’t find her guilty of, because there’s not going to be enough evidence, is that she murdered, knowingly, purposefully—intended to cause the death of—Daniel Griner, Jr.” Cohen would go on to suggest that Nakira had kicked a toy downstairs and that Baby Dan, in chasing after it, “stumbled down the stairs and fell to the bottom.” Nakira didn’t seek help, the attorney explained, because Baby Dan already had bruises on his body and she was worried about what the authorities would think. “She should have called the doctor, E.M.S., or someone, and she did nothing,” Cohen acknowledged. “And subsequently she found him the next morning and he was dead.” Cohen finished by pointing out what the prosecution didn’t have: “There was no blood found in the home. There was no instrumentality of death.” Cohen told me later, “Believe me, the state looked for all of that. And it didn’t exist.”

Baby Dan’s remains were so deteriorated that the medical examiner, Dr. Peter Mazari, requested that a forensic anthropologist examine the body. The ensuing report, compiled by Evan Bird, stated that fractures were found on Baby Dan’s ribs, cranium, and left arm and leg, and that his body showed signs of “perimortem trauma,” meaning that his injuries had occurred “at or around the time of death.” His skull, the report noted, showed fractures “characteristic of inflicted, non-accidental, blunt force trauma.” Mazari, in his autopsy report, concluded, “Manner of Death: Homicide.”

It became clear early on that the state’s case rested on the findings of the forensic anthropologist and the medical examiner. Both testified as expert witnesses; both concluded that the injuries to Baby Dan’s body “wouldn’t be compatible with a simple fall down stairs,” as Mazari put it. Bird testified for more than four hours. Baby Dan’s skull had been fragmented into approximately seventy-four pieces, he told the jury, and “the skull alone took something like forty hours to put back together.” He explained that, although determining the time of death from bone analysis is less precise than from soft-tissue analysis, the fractures most likely occurred before Baby Dan’s remains were destroyed by the heat of an oven. In her cross-examination, Cohen suggested that the damage to the body could have happened after it was burned, say, while “trying to put it into a handbag.” “Sure,” Bird conceded.

Two of the lead investigators on the case, Cara Kahn and Ryan Breslin, had examined Nakira’s behavior on the day of Baby Dan’s supposed abduction. She had checked Facebook approximately forty-three times, tracked Amazon packages, visited designer-handbag shopping sites, and conducted online searches for where to buy Pedialyte. Vogelsong-Parvin, in her closing argument, contended that Nakira did not act like a grieving parent. She cited a series of calls that Nakira had made from jail to Lasha, Daniel, and other family members about what had happened. “What did you hear?” the prosecutor asked. “Did you hear remorse for a lost child?” The murder, Vogelsong-Parvin acknowledged, could not be proved by any single piece of evidence; but the kidnapping hoax, the destruction of the body, the forensic evidence of multiple instances of blunt-force trauma, and the internet searches added up to a scenario in which homicide was the only reasonable explanation. “Murders are made to look like accidents,” she concluded. “But accidents are not made to look like murder.”

Nakira didn’t testify. Through Cohen, she admitted that she had destroyed Baby Dan’s remains in a blind panic but claimed that she did so only after her son had already died of the injuries he incurred from the fall down the stairs. Cohen remembered the trial as a particularly tough one: Nakira “stuck to her story” and wouldn’t take the twenty-five-year plea deal on offer. “She said that after a few seconds he was perfectly O.K.,” Cohen said, of the fall. “You know, no blood, no nothing.”

Lasha and Daniel had both agreed to testify at the trial. But Lasha had just given birth to her third child and couldn’t make the trip. And Daniel, in the end, couldn’t bring himself to participate. He recollected being under immense pressure from his family, who, once they realized that he and Nakira were back in touch, staged what he called an “intervention.” “They all came to my house,” he said, “and were telling me not to talk to her.” Earlier, he had told me, “They were saying to me that I’ll lose my nursing license and I might lose my job.” In the end, no one testified on Nakira’s behalf.

On January 4, 2023, Nakira’s verdict was announced. In photographs of the courtroom, she appears in dark-gray prison scrubs, her hands shackled to a chain around her waist. I recognized the little curls glued to her forehead. Kahn was there, along with some colleagues from the police department. They sat with Daniel’s family, all wearing pins with Baby Dan’s face on their chests, as the jury found Nakira guilty on all counts. “We all just hugged each other,” Kahn said. “Because, you know, it’s not going to bring Daniel back, but it’s justice.”

Now, three years later, Cohen wondered what she might have done differently: “I’m not sure whether I really explained to her in great detail before negotiating for her that she was looking at life in maximum security without parole.” It was an affecting case, but Cohen thought that Nakira’s demeanor seemed guarded and cold. “I didn’t like her,” she told me at one point. “I guess she didn’t trust me either.” I asked Cohen if she had ever questioned Nakira about her childhood, or her postpartum mental health. “Did I need to put on psychologists to show that she’s, you know, not in her right mind, that she was acting under extreme pressure?” Cohen said. “I guess I could have said that she had postpartum depression and put that on. Truthfully, there might be some merit to it.”

In an appeal, filed shortly after the sentencing, a second public defender argued that “the State had no theory of how Ms. Griner killed [Baby Dan], or her motive.” He noted that “the dispute over whether the extensive bone injuries were the cause of death or the result of Ms. Griner’s decision to destroy his body after his accidental death was the only disputed issue at trial.” And he pointed out that the medical examiner had based his conclusion of homicide not only on scientific evidence but on the fact that “the remains were found ‘desecrated’ and ‘outside of a house,’ which he viewed as ‘highly suspicious.’ ” (“I think the circumstances played a very large role in the ruling of homicide,” the examiner had said during the trial.) The appeal mentioned a 2021 paper from the Journal of Forensic Sciences, in which researchers studied the cognitive bias of forensic work and concluded that “forensic pathologists were more likely to rule ‘homicide’ rather than ‘accident’ for deaths of Black children relative to White children.”

In June of last year, Nakira’s appeal was denied. She has applied for post-conviction relief, a legal process used to dispute a defendant’s sentence or conviction. A different public defender has begun working on her case, and an investigator is reinterviewing her former attorneys, witnesses, and family members. If her application is successful, Nakira will get a new trial; if it isn’t, she told me recently, “all hope is lost.”

Both Kahn and Breslin have potent memories of being on the scene that day. “I was there when the baby was found in the purse, and it was just crazy,” Breslin told me. “I can still picture vividly the flashlight going on to, like, what is that? Oh, my God.” Kahn, who interviewed Nakira at the station, was struck by her affect. “She was emotionless,” Kahn told me. “I just wasn’t picking up on a normal human reaction to somebody that just lost their child.” (Breslin did recall Nakira crying. “I’m pretty confident I remember Cara giving her tissues,” he said.) Kahn couldn’t reconcile Nakira’s behavior with the circumstances. “How do you stay in your home all day while you know that your child is baking in an oven next to you?” she said. “How do you smell that? How do you sit there and Google and check your packages? It’s just unfathomable.” Grief counsellors were brought in to help the officers. “They put me through some P.T.S.D.-specific training to work me through it,” Kahn recalled. “Because, you know, it’s just one of those things you can’t unsee.”

Kahn was reluctant to speak with me at first. She eventually agreed, she said, “because you can’t have a lopsided story.” She was still haunted by the case. “It’s this weight that we all kind of carry around,” she said. “We don’t want to relive it.”

When I initially read about Baby Dan’s death, it had seemed like an isolated tragedy. But, as I dove deeper into the subject of maternal filicide, I learned that roughly every two days a child dies at the hands of his or her mother in America, more than in any other developed country. A study from the Journal of Aggression, Maltreatment & Trauma suggests that the actual rate is likely higher, as a result of inaccurate coroner rulings and the number of victims whose bodies are never discovered.

Earlier this fall, a deadlocked jury in the Lindsay Clancy murder case forced the judge to declare a mistrial. The story of the affluent, white nurse who killed her three young children has become a lightning rod of sorts for discussions about postpartum mental health and about how filicidal mothers are treated by the judicial system. Even before the Clancy trial began, the forensic psychiatrist Susan Hatters-Friedman pointed out to me how quickly the discourse around the case had turned to postpartum psychosis, a rare disorder that affects about one in a thousand new mothers. “There was an outpouring of money and concern and ‘She must have been mentally ill,’ ” Hatters-Friedman said. In contrast, “Black and brown people,” she noted, “tend to be portrayed as bad rather than mad.” Clancy might have been, for some, easy to empathize with because she was supported by her ex-husband, colleagues, family, and friends. She had a network willing to testify and speak to the media on her behalf. Nakira did not. Clancy also had consistent health-care coverage and thus a documented history of her mental-health breakdown. Again, Nakira did not.

The sociologist Martha Smithey observed that to understand crimes like this we have to recognize that they are committed by human beings. “That human being could be me,” she said. “It could be you.” Then she added, “People want to think it’s about murder, and it’s actually about mothering.” Smithey, who has focussed her research on filicide and has uncovered a trend in the criminalization of mothers in low-income households, told me that often the trigger for a mother who kills her young child is a crying baby, who, she said, becomes a “symbol of her failure.”

Almost every expert with whom I spoke emphasized that, although there can be commonalities, each case of maternal filicide is oddly specific. Gwen Adshead, a British forensic psychiatrist, used the term “the bicycle-lock model” to explain the many risk factors that have to align, like numbers on a combination lock, for fatal violence to occur. The last risk factor, according to Adshead, is the relationship between the perpetrator and her victim, and what happens between the two on the day itself. That interaction, she said, is “often key to understanding the homicide.”

What exactly had happened that day between Nakira and Baby Dan was unknowable—I would never be sure of what, if anything, had finally turned the lock. “And, of course, if a mother tells me, ‘My child fell down the stairs,’ I would always wonder, Well did you throw them down the stairs?” Anna Motz, the British forensic psychologist, said. I remembered Motz’s comment about “self-preservation” when Nakira told me that she was still upset by the way that she had been portrayed by the prosecutors. Their description of her Googling and shopping on the day of Baby Dan’s death had especially bothered her. “They made it seem as I just went on about my life with no cares and no worry,” she said. “I was just so out of my mind.”

I had noticed that, although Nakira recounted Baby Dan’s last days in broadly the same way since she was arrested, small details had changed. According to Cohen, Nakira had said that Baby Dan’s fall seemed “minor.” But she had told me that he bled from his mouth and had a “huge knot forming on his head.” During one conversation with me, Nakira observed, “I feel like every time you reflect on a memory, it always kind of changes shape.” Adshead had pointed out that for most perpetrators their “cover story” is, in fact, a life buoy. Its purpose isn’t malevolence or deception, she said, but, rather, “a story you can live with.”

Over the years of writing to, speaking with, and visiting Nakira, I had come to care about her. The remorse she expressed was hard not to empathize with. “My husband lost his son, my child lost his brother & so on & so on & the guilt from that alone is crippling,” she had written in one of her first e-mails. I liked her willingness to reflect on her life, her inquisitiveness, her dry humor. At times, I caught myself thinking back to our first meeting, her hands wiping away tears or protectively hugging herself—the same hands that had shoved Baby Dan into the oven, then stuffed his remains into a Hello Kitty purse. It was jarring.

After Nakira’s conviction, Daniel didn’t speak to her for many months. But, as time passed, he began accepting her phone calls from prison. Eventually, he decided to believe his wife. “I forgave her,” Daniel told me. “I didn’t want to carry that with me for the rest of my life. I wanted to be there for her because she had no one else.” He faltered a moment, then added, “And I still love her.”

Lately, though, things between the couple have become more complicated. Nakira told me recently that Daniel has started dating another woman. On the phone, she tried to be sanguine about it. Daniel will always be “my family,” she said. “I want him to be happy, and I don’t get to control how that looks.”

Nakira knows that access to J.J., to life outside, depends on her husband’s graciousness. “He doesn’t have to financially support me, he doesn’t have to allow me to be a part of J.J.’s life,” she said. When we first began corresponding, Nakira spoke to her son every day; now she said that months had passed since their last call. “I’m just going to wait it out,” she told me, “because I have no other choice.” ♦

#370. Executives are more likely to trust AI over themselves

Executives are twice as likely as rank-and-file workers to trust AI over their own judgment, per a survey out Wednesday morning.

Why it matters: It's a worrying development. Leaders are hired for their experience and knowledge.

  • Outsourcing that to AI raises the risk of making decisions that don't line up with reality, which then reverberate across companies and beyond.

By the numbers: 32.8% of executives said that they trust AI more than their own judgment, according to the survey from Charter, a workplace research and media company.

  • That's twice the rate for workers, or individual contributors. Just 16.3% of individual contributors said the same.
  • The divide shows up at the level below executives — 34.8% of managers said they trust AI more than their own judgment.

Zoom out: "Cognitive offloading" is a term for using tools like AI to reduce mental effort.

  • "Executives—despite having the most experience—are doing it more than anyone," the report's co-authors wrote.

The fine print: Charter in August surveyed 5,002 U.S. office workers, at companies with at least 100 employees, who said they use AI at work, including 2,952 individual contributors, 1,547 managers and 503 executives — including vice presidents, C-suite executives, owners and founders.

Between the lines: Most executives are under a lot of pressure to know the answers to a lot of questions really quickly — and AI can help with that at least in the short term, says report co-author Brian Elliott, a former Slack executive who is now executive-in-residence at Charter and co-wrote the report.

  • AI "lends itself very well to the 'seldom wrong, never in doubt' tendency that some executives have," he says.
  • "If you feel like you have to fake it until you make it as an executive, then AI is certainly going to feed those tendencies because you can appear to be really smart, really quick."

Zoom in: He described tech executives who use AI to build product prototypes over a weekend, then expect their teams to quickly turn those into functioning products — without appreciating the more complicated work involved.

  • "AI is really good at giving you the executive summary. It's not necessarily as good at giving you the very specific answer to the problem you've got today, which is more often what an individual contributor faces."

Reality check: The Charter survey measures executives' self-reported trust in AI, but not whether they actually follow its advice when making consequential decisions.

  • The report's findings were overall positive about AI's potential in the workplace. 42.7% of AI users said it improved both their work and personal lives.

Friction point: A key tension for workers is the threat of AI job loss, the researchers observed in separate qualitative interviews with 91 more workers.

  • The better workers become with AI, the more they realize how much can be done without them, the survey found.
  • There's a "persistent tension."

The bottom line: Executives are mostly free from that tension, says Elliott.

  • "The odds that an AI CEO shows up and takes over a company are excruciatingly low."

#372. These Women Were Trying to Escape Domestic Abuse. ICE Detained Them Instead.

Investigative Journalism in the Public Interest

ProPublica is a nonprofit, investigative newsroom that exposes corruption. We report in all 50 states and partner with local newsrooms. Our work spurs real-world impact and has received numerous awards, including nine Pulitzer Prizes.

How Abortion Bans Lead to Preventable Deaths

How Recent Arrivals at the Border Have Changed the Country and Its Attitudes

ProPublica is a nonprofit newsroom that investigates abuses of power. Sign up for Dispatches, a newsletter that spotlights wrongdoing around the country, to receive our stories in your inbox every week.

These highlights were written by the reporters and editors who worked on this story.

They were victims, not abusers. That’s what they told police officers, attorneys, visitors and anyone else who would listen. It didn’t matter. The women were all taken to a sprawling immigration detention center in the woods of west Michigan and handed thin jumpsuits — red, to mark them as a high security risk.

Inside the North Lake Processing Center, they talked about the men whose calls to police landed them there. The boyfriends or husbands who threatened to get them deported and cut them off from their children. The men, or sometimes women, who hit them, pulled their hair or choked them, bruising their throats.

They didn’t know where to go for help before, but now, in the confines of a cold cellblock in a former prison, the women helped one another. If one spoke to somebody on the outside who could connect them with advice, legal aid or money for the commissary, she’d pass around a tablet used for calls so others could tell their stories.

Migladys Bermudez, an immigration attorney in southeast Michigan, remembers a call in May from a Peruvian woman detained at North Lake. Her name was Pilar, and she’d heard about Bermudez from a fellow detainee who had been a client. Pilar told Bermudez about the boyfriend she tried to escape before her arrest, when she used makeup to cover the marks she said he left on her face. But he had accused her of being the abuser, resulting in her arrest.

Pilar handed the tablet to a Cuban woman named Leya. Over the echoes of the other detainees’ chatter, she described how her boyfriend insulted her, isolated her and gave her a black eye. Then, after grabbing him by the arm during an argument, she was the one behind bars, separated from their nursing daughter. On video calls, the girl didn’t seem to recognize her mother anymore. She stopped calling her “Mamá.”

Bermudez was taken aback. All three women in that chain — the former client, Pilar and Leya — said they were trying to get out of abusive relationships when they were arrested by local police on domestic violence allegations. In all three cases, the charges were dropped or never filed at all. The U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement detained the first woman during a routine check-in a few months after her charges were dropped. The others were handed over to ICE by local officials.

It’s long been intimidating for immigrants who suffered abuse to call 911, regardless of who was in the White House. They knew — and their abusers knew — that their immigration status made them vulnerable. But previous administrations treated victims as just that, people who needed help, not as targets for deportation. Bipartisan coalitions in Congress have long supported the Violence Against Women Act, which creates a pathway toward legal status for immigrants who are victims of domestic violence or other serious crimes.

Now, under President Donald Trump, all bets are off. On inauguration day, his administration rescinded guidelines that largely prevented immigration agents from making arrests at sensitive locations — including domestic violence shelters and hospitals. About a week later, the administration eliminated key protections granted to VAWA applicants and other crime victims. For the first time in decades, immigration authorities could disregard those protections and more freely arrest, detain and deport immigrant victims.

Life became more harrowing in other ways for immigrants who were victims of domestic violence. Federal immigration agents made arrests outside specialized domestic violence courts. And the administration persuaded more and more local police and jails to carry out the president’s mass deportation campaign, sweeping still more immigrants into custody.

Even communities without these so-called 287(g) agreements are helping the government round people up. In west Michigan’s Kent County, where Pilar and Leya were arrested, the county jail turned over more immigrants to ICE in the first six months of 2026 than it did during the entire Biden administration.

Advocates across the country say they’re seeing signs of an alarming increase in the numbers of domestic violence victims who are detained and deported. And many immigrants are staying in abusive relationships, fearful of calling for help or even stepping outside their homes.

“It’s just probably the most dangerous time for immigrant women right now who are victims of domestic violence,” said Bermudez, who took on Leya’s immigration case.

The arrest and detention of domestic violence victims is, in part, an unanticipated consequence of an effort meant to keep them safe. In much of the country, law enforcement officers are encouraged — or even required — to make an arrest when they have probable cause to believe that a domestic violence assault has taken place. Sometimes victims are mistaken as the abuser and taken into custody. Then, if they’re immigrants, they can be turned over to ICE — even if they were never criminally charged.

There’s no comprehensive way to track how often this happens or how many domestic violence victims have been detained by ICE or deported. But ProPublica spoke with a half-dozen alleged victims who were locked up together in a single facility, North Lake, and reviewed police records about others who were there, too. Their stories offer a picture of why Congress created programs like VAWA in the first place, and what’s at stake when those guardrails are taken away.

A coalition of immigrant survivors and advocates have sued the Trump administration, saying changes to how immigrant victims are treated not only undermined public safety, but also broke promises enshrined in federal law and policy.

“We were seeing our clients be more scared, be systematically targeted, be arrested, and deported very quickly,” said Kathleen Rivas, supervising attorney of the survivors team at Public Counsel, a law firm that’s involved in the litigation.

In May, a federal judge ordered the government to restore protections while the case played out. The Trump administration has appealed the order.

White House spokesperson Lauren Bis said in a statement that ICE is targeting the “worst of the worst.” The real story, she added, is the “American women whose lives have been taken by illegal alien criminals.” A spokesperson for the Department of Homeland Security said in a statement that “we will never apologize for deporting illegal aliens, because that’s how we save lives.”

But Leya, Pilar and other immigrants we interviewed have no criminal records. And advocates said communities are less safe now because immigrants are afraid to call police when their partners hurt them. This gives more ammunition to the threats of their abusers, said Casey Carter Swegman, director of public policy at the Tahirih Justice Center, a national nonprofit that advocates for immigrant survivors of domestic violence, sexual assault and human trafficking.

“Certainly more immigrant survivors are feeling like the violence and abuse they experience behind the closed door is somehow more tolerable than the violence and abuse they could experience if they tried to leave,” she said.

The first time her boyfriend’s fist slammed into her face was in July 2025, Leya recalled. They were living in Florida with his relatives. Only a month before, Leya gave birth to their daughter.

One night, she said, he heard her laugh in the kitchen with his mother and a man his mother was dating. Leya’s boyfriend flew into a jealous rage, she said, and punched her a few feet from their sleeping baby.

Leya, a recent immigrant from Cuba, had applied for status and was in active immigration proceedings. She said he was a permanent resident, with permission to work in the U.S. and better command of English. She felt trapped.

Soon after the Florida arrest, Leya and her boyfriend moved to the city of Wyoming, Michigan, a suburb of Grand Rapids that’s a magnet for immigrants who come for jobs in manufacturing and food processing. The unpretentious city is dotted with temporary staffing agencies, money-wiring businesses and Mexican restaurants.

Leya said their relationship worsened. Her boyfriend pushed her, pulled her hair and broke her phone, she said. He made her delete her social media accounts and isolated her from friends and family. They fought about money and talked about breaking up. One night in April, during an argument, he called the police.

“I’m having problems with my wife,” he said in Spanish, according to a recording of the call. The operator asked if there had been any violence. “No, no, no. None,” he said. “Just a discussion.”

He told officers who came to their apartment complex that Leya got angry because he had talked to an ex-girlfriend, according to body camera footage and a police report. When he tried to leave, he said, Leya pushed him and grabbed his arm. Then she attempted to hang up his phone when he dialed 911.

He said he wasn’t injured, though he accused her of hitting him several times a few days earlier.

Officers interviewed his brother, who mentioned the arrest in Florida, according to bodycam footage. “They had a cycle of problems where she called the police for abuse and he was detained for three days,” he said in Spanish. “My brother is trying to avoid having that happen again.”

The police report doesn’t mention the earlier arrest.

Footage of the interview with Leya was redacted due to state privacy laws, in part because she was interviewed inside their residence, unlike her boyfriend, who was interviewed outside. According to the police report, she “minimized” the argument and said there was no physical contact. She acknowledged trying to hang up the phone.

In an interview with ProPublica, Leya said they argued when she learned he sent money to his ex-girlfriend, who is the mother of his son. At the same time, she said, he had told her he couldn’t afford to buy diapers for their daughter. She said she has never hit him.

Officers arrested Leya on suspicion of domestic assault and interfering with a 911 call. They didn’t arrest her boyfriend or seek charges against him. Leya said she didn’t tell officers she was a victim of abuse because she thought she’d be able to tell a judge.

The Wyoming Police Department’s written policy encourages officers to make an arrest in incidents involving domestic violence “when there is reasonable cause to believe an assault was committed.”

Arrests demonstrate that “violence in the home is recognized as a serious crime,” according to Michigan’s law enforcement training manual on domestic violence. This approach is common across the country. Twenty-four states require an arrest when there’s probable cause of a domestic assault.

But domestic violence scenes can be chaotic, with both partners accusing each other of being the aggressor. It can be difficult for officers to understand what happened, said Erin Ross, a former domestic violence prosecutor who works at the national nonprofit Vera Institute of Justice. Sometimes, the wrong person ends up in handcuffs.

“The complexity of DV situations can thus lead to survivors being criminalized by the very system meant to protect them,” Ross and her co-authors wrote in a 2025 report about the effects of arrests on domestic violence survivors.

The Vera report doesn’t touch on immigration. But Ross and a dozen other domestic violence advocates across the country told ProPublica that immigrants who don’t speak English or understand their rights are, under the Trump administration, especially vulnerable to an arrest that can have irreversible consequences.

ProPublica reviewed hundreds of habeas corpus petitions filed nationwide since January 2025, in which immigrants challenged their ICE detention on constitutional grounds. We found dozens of petitioners who were arrested on suspicion of domestic violence and turned over to ICE, even though they were never charged or their charges were dropped. Using jail and police records from Florida and Michigan, as well as news reports, we identified even more cases.

In their petitions, several immigrants said that officers mistook them for the aggressor. The underlying arrest records show that officers often had to make close calls in messy cases where it was unclear who was the victim and who was the aggressor. Either way, under Trump, there’s little grace for immigrants.

In one case in Florida, a state where sheriff’s offices cooperate with immigration authorities, a deputy came upon a Venezuelan couple arguing in the street in November 2025. In his report, the deputy said the woman pulled at her husband’s pants, necklace and arm, leaving marks on his body. The woman said her husband had taken her keys and phone. She didn’t want to be left stranded, she said, so she ran after him.

She was charged with misdemeanor battery; her husband wasn’t charged. The woman was turned over to ICE while the case was pending. Prosecutors later dropped charges. The sheriff’s report didn’t mention any allegations of prior domestic violence, but the woman later said that, during their marriage, her husband had beaten her and threatened to kill her. In her habeas corpus petition, she said she was “afraid to report the abuse to the police.”

The woman was released in May, but during the five months she was incarcerated, her husband took their daughters out of state, her attorney told ProPublica. She still hasn’t seen the girls in person.

For Leya, it was a shock to find herself handcuffed, sitting in the back of a police cruiser, headed toward the Kent County jail. She was silent during the 15-minute ride, unable to make sense of what had just happened: “I had suffered from domestic violence,” she said. “But the one who ended up in trouble was me.”

The next day, a detective asked the prosecutor’s office to file charges against her. Prosecutors declined to do so, saying there was insufficient evidence, there were no injuries and Leya hadn’t successfully interfered with the phone call.

Leya had no criminal history, apart from this arrest, according to a DHS record. But her fingerprints had been run against state and federal databases, flagging ICE officials who could match them with her prints from when she arrived in the U.S. In addition, Leya said, a jail official asked about her status, and she told him she was waiting for an immigration court hearing.

ICE issued a detainer, essentially asking the jail to hold Leya until agents could pick her up. Kent County officials honored their request. A day later, Leya was at the detention center in the woods.

She cried in despair over her daughter. They’d never been separated. The 80 miles between Wyoming and North Lake seemed infinite. Her breasts ached from the drying milk. Leya wondered whether she’d be deported and if their separation would be permanent.

From a narrow window in her cell, she saw people outside holding signs, protesting ICE. She could hear their chants in English. Though she had trouble understanding them, she felt comforted by the care of strangers.

Inside, she familiarized herself with the routines. She spent most of her time in a large, open room with other women in red. Cameras eyed them as they talked about their children and upcoming court dates. They shared contact information for relatives in their home countries in case one of them got deported and had nowhere to go. To pass the hours, they watched telenovelas, played rounds of Uno and pieced together jigsaw puzzles of forests and farms.

Leya grew close to a few women from Latin America and Eastern Europe who said they, too, had been in abusive relationships. Several had called police for help, records show. A Honduran woman said her partner tried to choke her. A Dominican woman said her husband, a U.S. citizen, hit her, pulled her hair and chased her with a stick.

Then there was Pilar, Leya’s first cellmate. The asylum seeker from Peru had also been arrested on suspicion of domestic violence in the city of Wyoming.

Pilar, her boyfriend and her teenage daughter called 911 five times in the 15 months before her arrest, records show. On one occasion, Pilar’s daughter said she worried that the boyfriend would hurt her mom; according to bodycam footage, Pilar assured officers that everything was fine. On another, the man said he experienced mental abuse and wanted to find a shelter. On yet another, Pilar said she just wanted him to leave.

None of these calls ended in an arrest. Officers ensured that the boyfriend had another place to stay when he sought help, and they told Pilar to call back if he tried to return and hurt her, according to the footage. They advised the couple to go to civil court for future disputes over money and property.

In an interview with ProPublica from North Lake, Pilar, 48, said that her boyfriend took financial advantage of her and physically assaulted her. And in her habeas corpus petition, she described the relationship as one of “physical, mental, psychological, economic abuse.” Her sister took a photo of the bruises around Pilar’s neck and feared the worst. “I would tell her to leave that man,” she said.

Pilar said she was ashamed to go to her job at a food-processing plant with bruises on her face. She wondered what her co-workers would think. She distanced herself from relatives and friends. Pilar said she never told the police the full story because she worried about the possible repercussions — not for herself, but for her boyfriend. She didn’t want him to be deported. Despite everything, she loved him and felt he loved her.

One night in October 2025, her boyfriend drove up to their apartment complex in a rental car. The couple argued in the parking lot. He called 911.

“I need police,” he said over and over in English.

Pilar called moments later: “This man is leaving my house and he’s not giving me my keys,” she screamed in Spanish. “This man has hurt me. He has scratched my whole arm.”

When officers arrived, they separated the couple. The boyfriend said that Pilar slapped his head, grabbed his shirt and tried to pull him out of the car while he was in the driver’s seat, according to bodycam footage and the police report. One side of his T-shirt was torn. He said he never assaulted Pilar and any injuries were her own fault. “I’ve never hit her,” he said in Spanish.

He told officers that he’d become a U.S. permanent resident two months earlier and that Pilar was jealous. “She is making this up because she knows she can get papers,” he said.

Pilar insisted she was the victim. She showed officers the scratch marks on her arm and said her boyfriend was to blame. She begged them to make him return the apartment keys, according to bodycam footage and a police report. “I’ve been trying to kick him out for a while and he won’t leave, and he doesn’t want to help pay the rent,” she said in Spanish.

An officer asked how her boyfriend’s shirt got ripped. Pilar said she grabbed onto him when he started driving away in order to avoid being dragged by the car. The officer asked her several more times about the ripped shirt. Pilar told him she wanted to speak to a lawyer.

In their report, police noted that the boyfriend had called 911 first. His shirt was ripped. Pilar’s wasn’t. If the boyfriend had thrown her down, the officer wrote, then her shirt should have been ripped because he was “much taller and heavier (around 80lbs more).”

Her boyfriend wasn’t arrested. Pilar was.

Wyoming Public Safety Chief Kip Snyder declined to speak about the arrests of Pilar or Leya. In an email to ProPublica, he said officers don’t “participate in enforcing federal immigration laws. We encourage trust and cooperation with all residents and aim to ensure that everyone feels safe reporting crimes or seeking assistance.”

Shortly after Pilar’s arrest, a police detective asked the prosecutor’s office to file domestic violence charges against both her and her boyfriend.

Prosecutors declined to file any charges, citing insufficient evidence. Pilar wasn’t released. Apart from this arrest, she had no criminal history, according to DHS records. She also had a pending asylum case.

Still, ICE put a detainer on her and jail officials turned her over.

A few years earlier, Kent County Sheriff Michelle Lajoye-Young said a handoff like this wouldn’t happen.

In 2019, she announced that deputies would stop automatically honoring detainers and instead require warrants signed by a federal judge or magistrate. The move came after a U.S. citizen — a Marine combat veteran — was transferred from her jail into ICE custody.

“There needed to be a judicial oversight inserted into that process to give us the assurance that somebody wasn’t erroneously detained without due process,” Lajoye-Young said around that time.

Trump called Kent a “sanctuary jurisdiction.” The label haunted the county, which in the 2016 election voted for Trump. A national think tank that aims to reduce immigration put Kent County on its list of sanctuary communities, while Republican state lawmakers tried to compel its cooperation with federal authorities.

Yet it’s unclear whether Lajoye-Young ever delivered on her promise. ProPublica requested copies of all department policies, procedures, memos and instructions on how it handles ICE detainers since 2018. None said a judicial warrant was needed. ProPublica also asked for copies of judicial warrants for Pilar and Leya. The county said no such records exist.

Lajoye-Young, who retired last week, declined interview requests. Sgt. Scott Dietrich, a spokesperson for the sheriff’s office, didn’t directly answer questions about whether the policy was ever implemented. But in a statement he said that detainers are “handled under applicable law and established policy, consistent with our commitment to public safety, due process, and fair and impartial enforcement.”

Gema Lowe, an organizer with the local chapter of an immigrant rights group, said the sheriff had backtracked on her promise and wasn’t being forthright. “I don’t understand why they’re being secretive,” she said.

The shift deepened the mistrust among immigrants in a county with one of the state’s fastest-growing immigrant populations. Lowe, an immigrant herself from Mexico, said she advises victims to think twice before reporting domestic violence to the police because of the risk of arrest and deportation, while pointing them to other local resources like shelters.

She and others said the stakes are especially high now. ICE arrests have skyrocketed under Trump. Nobody is off limits, including immigrants with pending asylum claims and no criminal records.

And more local authorities are helping ICE make arrests. In January 2025, fewer than 150 state and local law enforcement agencies had signed 287(g) agreements with DHS to help carry out the president’s deportation agenda. That number has ballooned to more than 2,600 today.

“Partnerships with law enforcement are critical to having the resources we need to arrest criminal illegal aliens across the country,” a DHS spokesperson said. “We have had tremendous success when local law enforcement works together with us.”

Kent County doesn’t have a 287(g) agreement. Still, the jail hands over the vast majority of people with detainers to ICE, records show. Dozens of people who were turned over to ICE this year had their criminal charges dropped, or, like Pilar and Leya, were never charged in the first place.

Dietrich said the dismissal of a criminal charge “does not, by itself, mean the original arrest lacked probable cause.” The sheriff’s office also doesn’t “determine whether criminal charges are ultimately prosecuted, nor does it determine whether an individual is deported,” he said.

DHS told ProPublica in a statement that both Leya and Pilar “illegally entered the country” in 2022 and that both “will receive due process” in their immigration proceedings.

Kent County Prosecutor Chris Becker said he hadn’t heard of victims getting detained by ICE after calling the police. In his experience, he said, more perpetrators with active cases get deported.

Becker said he tries to get the message out that his team serves all victims, regardless of their immigration status. He’s aware that immigrants are reluctant to seek help. His own staff has told him that victims are afraid to testify or file restraining orders. They don’t want their partners — often the breadwinners and the fathers of their children — to be deported.

“There’s been a chilling impact to women coming forward in domestic violence situations due to what’s going on,” Becker said.

As the months passed at North Lake, Leya watched as women she befriended started to leave.

Some were deported, including at least two Mexican women who were also arrested in Kent County on suspicion of domestic violence. Others were released. New women took their cells. Some told Leya that they, too, had been arrested after 911 calls about domestic violence.

Leya fought for her freedom. She met with volunteers from Kent County, who raised money to hire her lawyers and pay for a required physical exam and fees. Even her ex-boyfriend, the one who she said gave her a black eye, wrote a letter on her behalf to help her case, calling her “a calm person.” He explained that he had called the police after their argument “escalated” and that “problems are situations that can happen in any relationship.”

Leya, he added, “deserves a second opportunity to remain in this country and be close to our daughter, as our baby truly needs her very much.” While Leya was locked up, their little girl celebrated her first birthday, got her first teeth and took her first steps.

At virtual hearings, Leya sat with her arms crossed, her dark, wavy hair neatly braided in cornrows and wearing an oversized coat over her red uniform. She tried to follow the discussion of people who seemed to have her life in their hands.

In late August, a judge granted Leya bond. While her immigration case played out, she could be free.

It was a profound relief. At the same time, it felt awful to leave behind her friends. It seemed like some of them were never getting out. Pilar was approaching a year in detention.

Four months after her arrest, Leya walked out of the old prison. She thought about how she would live on her own now and support her daughter.

She promised herself she wouldn’t go back to the man who hurt her.

They were victims, not abusers. That’s what they told police officers, attorneys, visitors and anyone else who would listen. It didn’t matter. The women were all taken to a sprawling immigration detention center in the woods of west Michigan and handed thin jumpsuits — red, to mark them as a high security risk.

Inside the North Lake Processing Center, they talked about the men whose calls to police landed them there. The boyfriends or husbands who threatened to get them deported and cut them off from their children. The men, or sometimes women, who hit them, pulled their hair or choked them, bruising their throats.

They didn’t know where to go for help before, but now, in the confines of a cold cellblock in a former prison, the women helped one another. If one spoke to somebody on the outside who could connect them with advice, legal aid or money for the commissary, she’d pass around a tablet used for calls so others could tell their stories.

Migladys Bermudez, an immigration attorney in southeast Michigan, remembers a call in May from a Peruvian woman detained at North Lake. Her name was Pilar, and she’d heard about Bermudez from a fellow detainee who had been a client. Pilar told Bermudez about the boyfriend she tried to escape before her arrest, when she used makeup to cover the marks she said he left on her face. But he had accused her of being the abuser, resulting in her arrest.

Pilar handed the tablet to a Cuban woman named Leya. Over the echoes of the other detainees’ chatter, she described how her boyfriend insulted her, isolated her and gave her a black eye. Then, after grabbing him by the arm during an argument, she was the one behind bars, separated from their nursing daughter. On video calls, the girl didn’t seem to recognize her mother anymore. She stopped calling her “Mamá.”

Bermudez was taken aback. All three women in that chain — the former client, Pilar and Leya — said they were trying to get out of abusive relationships when they were arrested by local police on domestic violence allegations. In all three cases, the charges were dropped or never filed at all. The U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement detained the first woman during a routine check-in a few months after her charges were dropped. The others were handed over to ICE by local officials.

It’s long been intimidating for immigrants who suffered abuse to call 911, regardless of who was in the White House. They knew — and their abusers knew — that their immigration status made them vulnerable. But previous administrations treated victims as just that, people who needed help, not as targets for deportation. Bipartisan coalitions in Congress have long supported the Violence Against Women Act, which creates a pathway toward legal status for immigrants who are victims of domestic violence or other serious crimes.

Now, under President Donald Trump, all bets are off. On inauguration day, his administration rescinded guidelines that largely prevented immigration agents from making arrests at sensitive locations — including domestic violence shelters and hospitals. About a week later, the administration eliminated key protections granted to VAWA applicants and other crime victims. For the first time in decades, immigration authorities could disregard those protections and more freely arrest, detain and deport immigrant victims.

Life became more harrowing in other ways for immigrants who were victims of domestic violence. Federal immigration agents made arrests outside specialized domestic violence courts. And the administration persuaded more and more local police and jails to carry out the president’s mass deportation campaign, sweeping still more immigrants into custody.

Even communities without these so-called 287(g) agreements are helping the government round people up. In west Michigan’s Kent County, where Pilar and Leya were arrested, the county jail turned over more immigrants to ICE in the first six months of 2026 than it did during the entire Biden administration.

Advocates across the country say they’re seeing signs of an alarming increase in the numbers of domestic violence victims who are detained and deported. And many immigrants are staying in abusive relationships, fearful of calling for help or even stepping outside their homes.

“It’s just probably the most dangerous time for immigrant women right now who are victims of domestic violence,” said Bermudez, who took on Leya’s immigration case.

The arrest and detention of domestic violence victims is, in part, an unanticipated consequence of an effort meant to keep them safe. In much of the country, law enforcement officers are encouraged — or even required — to make an arrest when they have probable cause to believe that a domestic violence assault has taken place. Sometimes victims are mistaken as the abuser and taken into custody. Then, if they’re immigrants, they can be turned over to ICE — even if they were never criminally charged.

There’s no comprehensive way to track how often this happens or how many domestic violence victims have been detained by ICE or deported. But ProPublica spoke with a half-dozen alleged victims who were locked up together in a single facility, North Lake, and reviewed police records about others who were there, too. Their stories offer a picture of why Congress created programs like VAWA in the first place, and what’s at stake when those guardrails are taken away.

A coalition of immigrant survivors and advocates have sued the Trump administration, saying changes to how immigrant victims are treated not only undermined public safety, but also broke promises enshrined in federal law and policy.

“We were seeing our clients be more scared, be systematically targeted, be arrested, and deported very quickly,” said Kathleen Rivas, supervising attorney of the survivors team at Public Counsel, a law firm that’s involved in the litigation.

In May, a federal judge ordered the government to restore protections while the case played out. The Trump administration has appealed the order.

White House spokesperson Lauren Bis said in a statement that ICE is targeting the “worst of the worst.” The real story, she added, is the “American women whose lives have been taken by illegal alien criminals.” A spokesperson for the Department of Homeland Security said in a statement that “we will never apologize for deporting illegal aliens, because that’s how we save lives.”

But Leya, Pilar and other immigrants we interviewed have no criminal records. And advocates said communities are less safe now because immigrants are afraid to call police when their partners hurt them. This gives more ammunition to the threats of their abusers, said Casey Carter Swegman, director of public policy at the Tahirih Justice Center, a national nonprofit that advocates for immigrant survivors of domestic violence, sexual assault and human trafficking.

But her fingerprints had been run against state and federal databases, flagging ICE officials who could match them with her prints from when she arrived in the U.S. In addition, Leya said, a jail official asked about her status, and she told him she was waiting for an immigration court hearing.

ICE issued a detainer, essentially asking the jail to hold Leya until agents could pick her up. Kent County officials honored their request. A day later, Leya was at the detention center in the woods.

She cried in despair over her daughter. They’d never been separated. The 80 miles between Wyoming and North Lake seemed infinite. Her breasts ached from the drying milk. Leya wondered whether she’d be deported and if their separation would be permanent.

From a narrow window in her cell, she saw people outside holding signs, protesting ICE. She could hear their chants in English. Though she had trouble understanding them, she felt comforted by the care of strangers.

Inside, she familiarized herself with the routines. She spent most of her time in a large, open room with other women in red. Cameras eyed them as they talked about their children and upcoming court dates. They shared contact information for relatives in their home countries in case one of them got deported and had nowhere to go. To pass the hours, they watched telenovelas, played rounds of Uno and pieced together jigsaw puzzles of forests and farms.

Leya grew close to a few women from Latin America and Eastern Europe who said they, too, had been in abusive relationships. Several had called police for help, records show. A Honduran woman said her partner tried to choke her. A Dominican woman said her husband, a U.S. citizen, hit her, pulled her hair and chased her with a stick.

Then there was Pilar, Leya’s first cellmate. The asylum seeker from Peru had also been arrested on suspicion of domestic violence in the city of Wyoming.

Pilar, her boyfriend and her teenage daughter called 911 five times in the 15 months before her arrest, records show. On one occasion, Pilar’s daughter said she worried that the boyfriend would hurt her mom; according to bodycam footage, Pilar assured officers that everything was fine. On another, the man said he experienced mental abuse and wanted to find a shelter. On yet another, Pilar said she just wanted him to leave.

None of these calls ended in an arrest. Officers ensured that the boyfriend had another place to stay when he sought help, and they told Pilar to call back if he tried to return and hurt her, according to the footage. They advised the couple to go to civil court for future disputes over money and property.

In an interview with ProPublica from North Lake, Pilar, 48, said that her boyfriend took financial advantage of her and physically assaulted her. And in her habeas corpus petition, she described the relationship as one of “physical, mental, psychological, economic abuse.” Her sister took a photo of the bruises around Pilar’s neck and feared the worst. “I would tell her to leave that man,” she said.

Pilar said she was ashamed to go to her job at a food-processing plant with bruises on her face. She wondered what her co-workers would think. She distanced herself from relatives and friends. Pilar said she never told the police the full story because she worried about the possible repercussions — not for herself, but for her boyfriend. She didn’t want him to be deported. Despite everything, she loved him and felt he loved her.

One night in October 2025, her boyfriend drove up to their apartment complex in a rental car. The couple argued in the parking lot. He called 911.

“I need police,” he said over and over in English.

Pilar called moments later: “This man is leaving my house and he’s not giving me my keys,” she screamed in Spanish. “This man has hurt me. He has scratched my whole arm.”

When officers arrived, they separated the couple. The boyfriend said that Pilar slapped his head, grabbed his shirt and tried to pull him out of the car while he was in the driver’s seat, according to bodycam footage and the police report. One side of his T-shirt was torn. He said he never assaulted Pilar and any injuries were her own fault. “I’ve never hit her,” he said in Spanish.

He told officers that he’d become a U.S. permanent resident two months earlier and that Pilar was jealous. “She is making this up because she knows she can get papers,” he said.

Pilar insisted she was the victim. She showed officers the scratch marks on her arm and said her boyfriend was to blame. She begged them to make him return the apartment keys, according to bodycam footage and a police report. “I’ve been trying to kick him out for a while and he won’t leave, and he doesn’t want to help pay the rent,” she said in Spanish.

An officer asked how her boyfriend’s shirt got ripped. Pilar said she grabbed onto him when he started driving away in order to avoid being dragged by the car. The officer asked her several more times about the ripped shirt. Pilar told him she wanted to speak to a lawyer.

In their report, police noted that the boyfriend had called 911 first. His shirt was ripped. Pilar’s wasn’t. If the boyfriend had thrown her down, the officer wrote, then her shirt should have been ripped because he was “much taller and heavier (around 80lbs more).”

Her boyfriend wasn’t arrested. Pilar was.

Wyoming Public Safety Chief Kip Snyder declined to speak about the arrests of Pilar or Leya. In an email to ProPublica, he said officers don’t “participate in enforcing federal immigration laws. We encourage trust and cooperation with all residents and aim to ensure that everyone feels safe reporting crimes or seeking assistance.”

Shortly after Pilar’s arrest, a police detective asked the prosecutor’s office to file domestic violence charges against both her and her boyfriend.

Prosecutors declined to file any charges, citing insufficient evidence. Pilar wasn’t released. Apart from this arrest, she had no criminal history, according to DHS records. She also had a pending asylum case.

Still, ICE put a detainer on her and jail officials turned her over.

A few years earlier, Kent County Sheriff Michelle Lajoye-Young said a handoff like this wouldn’t happen.

In 2019, she announced that deputies would stop automatically honoring detainers and instead require warrants signed by a federal judge or magistrate. The move came after a U.S. citizen — a Marine combat veteran — was transferred from her jail into ICE custody.

“There needed to be a judicial oversight inserted into that process to give us the assurance that somebody wasn’t erroneously detained without due process,” Lajoye-Young said around that time.

Trump called Kent a “sanctuary jurisdiction.” The label haunted the county, which in the 2016 election voted for Trump. A national think tank that aims to reduce immigration put Kent County on its list of sanctuary communities, while Republican state lawmakers tried to compel its cooperation with federal authorities.

Yet it’s unclear whether Lajoye-Young ever delivered on her promise. ProPublica requested copies of all department policies, procedures, memos and instructions on how it handles ICE detainers since 2018. None said a judicial warrant was needed. ProPublica also asked for copies of judicial warrants for Pilar and Leya. The county said no such records exist.

Lajoye-Young, who retired last week, declined interview requests. Sgt. Scott Dietrich, a spokesperson for the sheriff’s office, didn’t directly answer questions about whether the policy was ever implemented. But in a statement he said that detainers are “handled under applicable law and established policy, consistent with our commitment to public safety, due process, and fair and impartial enforcement.”

Gema Lowe, an organizer with the local chapter of an immigrant rights group, said the sheriff had backtracked on her promise and wasn’t being forthright. “I don’t understand why they’re being secretive,” she said.

The shift deepened the mistrust among immigrants in a county with one of the state’s fastest-growing immigrant populations. Lowe, an immigrant herself from Mexico, said she advises victims to think twice before reporting domestic violence to the police because of the risk of arrest and deportation, while pointing them to other local resources like shelters.

She and others said the stakes are especially high now. ICE arrests have skyrocketed under Trump. Nobody is off limits, including immigrants with pending asylum claims and no criminal records.

And more local authorities are helping ICE make arrests. In January 2025, fewer than 150 state and local law enforcement agencies had signed 287(g) agreements with DHS to help carry out the president’s deportation agenda. That number has ballooned to more than 2,600 today.

“Partnerships with law enforcement are critical to having the resources we need to arrest criminal illegal aliens across the country,” a DHS spokesperson said. “We have had tremendous success when local law enforcement works together with us.”

Kent County doesn’t have a 287(g) agreement. Still, the jail hands over the vast majority of people with detainers to ICE, records show. Dozens of people who were turned over to ICE this year had their criminal charges dropped, or, like Pilar and Leya, were never charged in the first place.

Dietrich said the dismissal of a criminal charge “does not, by itself, mean the original arrest lacked probable cause.” The sheriff’s office also doesn’t “determine whether criminal charges are ultimately prosecuted, nor does it determine whether an individual is deported,” he said.

DHS told ProPublica in a statement that both Leya and Pilar “illegally entered the country” in 2022 and that both “will receive due process” in their immigration proceedings.

Kent County Prosecutor Chris Becker said he hadn’t heard of victims getting detained by ICE after calling the police. In his experience, he said, more perpetrators with active cases get deported.

Becker said he tries to get the message out that his team serves all victims, regardless of their immigration status. He’s aware that immigrants are reluctant to seek help. His own staff has told him that victims are afraid to testify or file restraining orders. They don’t want their partners — often the breadwinners and the fathers of their children — to be deported.

“There’s been a chilling impact to women coming forward in domestic violence situations due to what’s going on,” Becker said.

As the months passed at North Lake, Leya watched as women she befriended started to leave.

Some were deported, including at least two Mexican women who were also arrested in Kent County on suspicion of domestic violence. Others were released. New women took their cells. Some told Leya that they, too, had been arrested after 911 calls about domestic violence.

Leya fought for her freedom. She met with volunteers from Kent County, who raised money to hire her lawyers and pay for a required physical exam and fees. Even her ex-boyfriend, the one who she said gave her a black eye, wrote a letter on her behalf to help her case, calling her “a calm person.” He explained that he had called the police after their argument “escalated” and that “problems are situations that can happen in any relationship.”

Leya, he added, “deserves a second opportunity to remain in this country and be close to our daughter, as our baby truly needs her very much.” While Leya was locked up, their little girl celebrated her first birthday, got her first teeth and took her first steps.

At virtual hearings, Leya sat with her arms crossed, her dark, wavy hair neatly braided in cornrows and wearing an oversized coat over her red uniform. She tried to follow the discussion of people who seemed to have her life in their hands.

In late August, a judge granted Leya bond. While her immigration case played out, she could be free.

It was a profound relief. At the same time, it felt awful to leave behind her friends. It seemed like some of them were never getting out. Pilar was approaching a year in detention.

Four months after her arrest, Leya walked out of the old prison. She thought about how she would live on her own now and support her daughter.

She promised herself she wouldn’t go back to the man who hurt her.

This measure would change the way recall elections are conducted in California. Under this proposed constitutional amendment, during a recall election, voters would decide solely whether a politician should be removed from their elected position. Under current law, voters make two separate decisions during a recall election: whether to remove the subject of the recall from office and, if they are booted, which candidate running to replace them should fill the position. Under an existing state appropriations restraint, also known as the Gann Limit, lawmakers cannot spend more in the state budget than an amount determined by a formula that takes annual tax proceeds, changes to the population and cost of living into consideration.

Tax revenue above the limit must be divided between schools and refunds to taxpayers. More recent carve-outs in the law also allow leaders to direct excess funds to infrastructure and local government.

The limit applies to most other appropriations of tax revenue, including money that lawmakers tuck away into the “rainy day” fund and other reserves.

California voters also have capped the amount of money lawmakers can set aside in the rainy day fund at 10% of general fund proceeds in a given year.

The proposed changes would exempt deposits into the rainy day fund and a short-term reserve, called the Projected Surplus Temporary Holding Account, from the state appropriations limit. The cap on the rainy day fund would grow from 10% of general fund tax revenue to 20%.

In effect, the measure would allow the governor and the Legislature to put more money into budget reserves, creating another option to avoid triggering rebates.

For example, if the spending limit is $400 billion for the year, and revenue hits $410 billion, the measure would give lawmakers the option to deposit the $10 billion above the spending limit into the rainy day fund, direct the money to infrastructure or give it to local governments, instead of returning the funding to taxpayers and increasing funding for schools.

Newsom, legislative Democrats and the California Democratic Party say the changes will allow the state to save more when the economy is growing and use the funds to lessen cuts to vital programs during a recession or stock market crash.

Reform California, a political action committee started by state Sen. Carl DeMaio (R-San Diego), and legislative Republicans argue the proposal will allow Democrats to spend more money and hold on to revenue that otherwise would go back to taxpayers.

Since taking office, Newsom has argued that it doesn’t make sense for deposits into a savings account to count as spending under state law.

California’s budget revenue also is vulnerable to dramatic and at times unpredictable swings from year to year based on stock market activity. Newsom has said the state should have more flexibility during times of surplus to deposit money into the reserve accounts that lawmakers could then use during deficits to prevent big cuts to social services.

Statewide voters originally adopted the constitutional spending limit during an antitax wave in the late 1970s. Subsequent changes weakened the measure with exemptions to the limit for different kinds of appropriations, such as spending on infrastructure, and made it easier for lawmakers to avoid exceeding the limit and triggering rebates.

Why it matters: While AI giants race to build AI tools that can respond quickly to hacking or other malicious online threats, strict guardrails on their most powerful models lock out defenders mid-investigation, leaving them scrambling for alternatives at critical moments.

What's happening: OpenCode, an open-source AI coding agent, said it was compromised over the weekend, allowing attackers to obtain API keys belonging to some customers, including keys for OpenAI, Anthropic and Google services.

  • OpenCode said the breach was "related to a large scale distillation attack that impacted 1.4% of OpenCode Console users."
  • OpenCode co-creator Dax Raad said attackers had been targeting its systems continuously for 48 hours, forcing the company to keep its own AI agents running around the clock to respond.
  • OpenAI, Google and Anthropic have said they worked with affected customers to revoke exposed API keys and address unauthorized usage charges.

Zoom in: Raad said on X that frontier AI models initially helped investigate the breach but then "started refusing."

  • The models' safety restrictions reportedly began blocking requests as the investigation turned to potentially malicious code, even though OpenCode was trying to defend its own systems.
  • OpenCode then gained access to OpenAI's Daybreak Blue program, which gives vetted cybersecurity defenders access to models with fewer restrictions.
  • "We definitely need to be more proactive and we've made so many improvements over the last few days," Raad wrote on X Monday.

Between the lines: Cybersecurity teams will have to figure out how to balance the need for faster AI-powered defenses with restrictions designed to keep those same capabilities out of attackers' hands, Chris Jones, chief technology officer at Nightwing, told Axios.
