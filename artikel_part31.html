Paramount on Tuesday finalized its takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, completing a hostile acquisition that was months in the making and almost faltered at several hurdles.

The takeover cost a total of around $81 billion (roughly €72 billion), or almost $111 billion including the Warner Bros. debts passed on as part of the deal.

The merger brings together two of the oldest US filmmaking studios and will form a new giant called Skydance Corporation.

The new name Skydance hails from billionaire investor David Ellison, who first bought out Paramount and its subsidiaries and then moved on to target the much larger fish that is Warner Bros.

The bid faced stiff competition in a bidding war with Netflix, and later scrutiny from US regulators and politicians, but ultimately went through.

"Today is a historic day, not just for Skydance but for our entire industry," said Ellison, whose family has ties to US President Donald Trump.

Skydance shares began trading on the New York Stock Exchange on Tuesday under the ticker SKYD, while Warner Bros. Discovery shareholders will receive about $31 per share.

The deal closed less than a week after a federal judge in California approved a settlement between Paramount and 12 US states that had sued to block the transaction on antitrust grounds.

As well as the two giant film studios, Paramount Pictures and Warner Bros. Pictures, the deal also brings together the CBS News and CNN news networks, and the HBO and CBS television brands. Ellison says he plans to merge the subscription streaming platforms Paramount+ and HBO Max.

Ellison will serve as chairman and CEO of the new company. Former Mattel boss Ynon Kreiz is operating as co-CEO in charge of day-to-day operations.

Skydance Corp. looks set to have a total revenue in the region of $70 billion, based on the performance to date of its component parts. It says it's targeting cost savings of around $6 billion within three years as it takes advantage of overlaps created by the merger.

#1508. The Genius of Charli XCX

On her new album, Music, Fashion, Film, the musician embraces rock.

The last time a Charli XCX album appeared—her sixth studio album, by the evocative title of Brat—the Club was ascendant. By that, I mean not a specific club but that quasi-mythological space, known to humankind for at least a century, where you dance away your emotional burdens into the wee hours of the morning. Here, as a Russian clubgoer once famously observed, “The ladies look good, the alcohol is flowing. There is much pain in the world, but not in this room.” That summer, in the not-so-distant year of 2024, Kamala Harris was projected to win the upcoming presidential election, fans and marketers declared Charli’s album to be the sound of the season, and “Brat Summer” was born, screaming, into a world that promised to be a little less chaotic.

Of course, we know how 2024 ended. Two years later, at the end of another record-breaking summer, Charli has given us something new: Music, Fashion, Film, an album whose title seems to gesture toward carefree living. But that would be too easy, I think. The album appears in the second year of another interminable Trump presidency, full of the nothing-signifying sound and fury that his return to office has wrought, and it sounds nothing like its predecessor. Of course, our sociopolitical vibes are different these days as well.

But, thank God, so is the music. Despite featuring a couple of the same producers who appeared on Brat—A.G. Cook and Finn Keane—the album is vastly different: It sounds, in a word, like rock. Its title also hints at something else: Charli’s renewed focus on her creative ventures outside of music. In addition to being the Club’s princess, she’s one of the faces of Saint Laurent and an actress appearing in more and more independent films. That Charli is less well-known in these domains appears to have given her some productive creative anxiety, pushing her music in new directions in the process. “I think the dance floor is dead / So now we’re making rock music,” she sings in the title track. And the rest of the album lives up to that promise.

As an artist, Charli XCX has always been great at making meaning out of the prevailing cultural currents: Brat Summer only happened because the public finally caught up with her. Which is what is fascinating about a track like “SS26,” one of Music, Fashion, Film’s singles and among the best songs on the album. Here’s the chorus, which finds Charli dispensing a terminal diagnosis for an uncertain world:

﻿Spring, Summer ’26When the world is gonna end, no hope for any of itYeah, we’re walkin’ on a runway that goes straight to hellNothing’s gonna save us, not music, fashion, or film.

It’s unusually straightforward songwriting, and it’s not hard to feel that Charli is correct once again: As governments around the world continue their inexorable rightward slide; as Europe, Canada, and the western United States are consumed by intense wildfires; as the American president continues to send fuel prices spiraling globally, it’s getting harder to deny that hell is real and that, as a great writer once said, it is other people.

But with that last line in mind (“Nothing’s gonna save us, not music, fashion, or film”), it is, I think, easier to read the thrust of the new album: that our outlets of escape can’t save us. A little later in the song, Charli has some even sharper words for the culture. “Think my politics could work as a press strategy / And my heritage could give me quite the USP [unique selling point, in marketing-speak] / Can’t hide the fact that I’d rather take the easy road,” she sings. Me too, girl. If escaping into art is no longer enough, what is? Charli doesn’t give an answer for the rest of us, but she does provide one for herself, the last line of the verse: “Yeah, I think I’ll be all right if I look good in the clothes.” Obviously, she doesn’t believe it. But sung as sweetly as it is, over a pop-punkish bass line, a digital snare hit, and a tastefully minimal guitar, the song goes down easy.

Music, Fashion, Film gets darker as it goes on. To a set of grunge-y guitars that could come straight from a late-aughts alt-rock album, backed by MPC﻿-style digital beats, Charli gives us songs about her bedroom-pop-star days (“2007”), about how she can buy everything but a new life (“Card Declined”), and about her fear of ruining the best relationship in her life because of her own bad self (“I’m Afraid”). That’s a lot of self-disclosure, and in other hands it might feel maudlin—too vulnerable, too eager—but here the feelings are finely worked. It’s emotional vulnerability as a necessity, not as a marketing strategy (though it could be that too, of course).

“Do I wanna make music?” Charli asks in the middle of “Camera,” a song that’s largely about contemplating a career change. “Am I being fuckin’ stupid / If I try to be a girl on the screen when I’m turning thirty-four? / But all I know is how it makes me feel.” It’s refreshingly honest; she’s a narrator you can trust.

Still, the music’s fun, and I’m a sucker for depressing lyrics set to fun music. The tunes are spare in places, with seemingly simple instrumentation—but you can tell that a lot of thought went into the arrangements. Cook and Keane do a lot with what feels like a little, and it’s magic. Another notable thing: Charli sings without AutoTune, in a voice that’s minimally processed. It’s another sign that she’s moving forward.

Plus these songs have an outsize life and energy to them. Music, Fashion, Film isn’t all interiority: “2007” is a romp through the past, a reminder of how much fun it was to make music in your bedroom before the fame set in. “Magic Metal Montana,” on the other hand, swaps bouncy lyrics for poignant ones, as it’s a song to her friend and collaborator A.G. Cook. Its sweetest moment comes when she says, “I think you taught me, I think I taught you / You believe me, I believe you”—an expression of trust you don’t find in most love songs. And “Rock Music” is a fun night out with friends, with everyone having a good time as they feel their way through the moment.

If there’s a thesis statement on Music, Fashion, Film, it’s probably “No One Lasts Forever,” which is a reminder from Charli—to herself, to the world—that no matter what happens, no matter how much things change, no state of affairs can last forever, and none of us do either. The song is billed as “featuring David Cronenberg” because it has an extended snippet from an interview that she did with the famous body-horror director about a near-death experience he had; it’s the only featured appearance on the album.

“As I was lying there on the sidewalk,” Cronenberg says, “I did not think about art at all. I—I thought about people, I thought about life…. No, I was not thinking about art as endurance, as immortality. We can talk about immortal artists, and it can turn out that, yes, some artists have created art that has really survived thousands of years…. And that’s fantastic. But experientially, for them, they are dead, they are gone, they are not experiencing that…. I really feel, and hear me assuring you, that oblivion is freedom.”

It’s a funny lesson to throw in at the end of what is ostensibly a pop album, but somehow it works. Oblivion is freedom, yes—from obligation, from the pressures of fame, from everything annoying that we can’t change but that nevertheless makes us who we are. The Club is dead—Charli says so herself. Perhaps it’s time to accept the things we can’t escape, about our lives and about ourselves. Perhaps it’s time to chart a new way forward.

Bijan Stephen is a music critic for The Nation. He lives in New York and his other work has appeared in The New Yorker, The New Republic, Esquire, and elsewhere.

#1514. Nobel Prize in literature: How the winner is secretly chosen

Can Xue is considered one of this year's favorites; Margaret Atwood and Salman Rushdie are other strong contenders. But what goes on behind the scenes in the selection of the Nobel Prize in literature?

On Thursday, October 8, at around 1:00 p.m. CEST, all eyes in the literary world will be set on Stockholm.

That's when the new permanent secretary of the Swedish Academy, Ingrid Carlberg, will hold a press conference to announce the winner of the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature, marking the end of a lengthy selection process that officially began a year earlier.

The Swedish Academy is the body responsible for awarding the prize. Its 18 members consist of writers, professors and intellectuals.

In September of the previous year, the Academy's Nobel Committee sends forms to hundreds of individuals and organizations eligible to submit nominations for the Nobel Prize in literature.

According to the Nobel Prize website, these include professors of literature and linguistics at universities and colleges, previous Nobel laureates in literature and the heads of writers' associations that represent the literary community in their respective countries. Anyone from one of these groups who has not received a form may also submit a nomination.

"The list can reach 200 names per year of nominated authors," explains Gisele Sapiro, research director at France's National Centre for Scientific Research. She studies how the Nobel Prize, as a global institution, influences the international circulation of literature, power dynamics in the literary world and translation markets.

"The committee can also add names itself, such as authors who were nominated in previous years," she says.

In the spring, the Nobel Committee then selects five names through a multistage process and presents them to the Academy. Over the summer, Academy members read the nominees' works and discuss them. The Academy always announces the decision in early October.

As for the criteria the Academy uses to make its decision, it prefers to keep them to itself.

The public doesn't learn which nominees missed out until 50 years later — that's how long the Academy waits before releasing the list.

Sometimes decades pass between an author's first nomination and actually receiving the prize. Danish writer Johannes V. Jensen, who won the Nobel Prize in literature in 1944, waited 40 years. Doris Lessing received the award in 2007 after 35 years, while Thomas Mann, who won in 1929, had to wait 27 years.

In his will, Alfred Nobel, the prize's founder, stipulated that it should be awarded to the person who had produced "the most outstanding work in an idealistic direction." That leaves plenty of room for interpretation.

According to Gisele Sapiro, the Academy aims to honor ambitious, influential literature that has, for example, given rise to a new literary movement. "At the same time, the work has to be universal, being able to transcend its own culture," she says.

Translations can be one indication of this. "I noted in my research, however, that all the laureates since 1990 had been translated into English, French and German — except for Abdulrazak Gurnah [the Tanzanian-British 2021 Nobel laureate], who had not been translated into German before winning — meaning that I think these three languages are the most important," Sapiro explains.

Sapiro says it is also a major advantage if nominees have already won other major literary awards, such as the International Man Booker Prize, the Cervantes Prize or the Büchner Prize.

Politics can also influence the Swedish Academy's decisions.

On several occasions, the prize has gone to writers who openly opposed the regimes in their home countries, such as Belarusian writer Svetlana Alexievich in 2015. She is an outspoken critic of authoritarian President Alexander Lukashenko.

Genre, on the other hand, does not matter. In the past, laureates have been honored not only for novels but also for poetry and plays. In 2016, singer-songwriter Bob Dylan received the prize for his song lyrics.

So far, only 33 of the 122 laureates have come from outside Europe. There is also a clear gender imbalance: only in the past eight years have men and women alternated as winners, significantly increasing women's share of the total number of laureates to 14.9%.

#1515. Inside the Supermassive Debate Over the James Webb Telescope’s ‘Little Red Dots’

The original version of this story appeared in Quanta Magazine.

Astronomers built the James Webb Space Telescope to pick up faint light from the first billion years after the Big Bang, a chaotic era when vast swaths of hydrogen and helium gas gathered into the chains of galaxies we see today. Even in the telescope’s first images, astronomers could see a whole zoo of mysterious smears of light.

One batch of objects proved especially difficult to interpret. They glowed blindingly bright, emitting red light with long wavelengths and shining as brilliantly as a whole galaxy. They were tiny, spanning just a pixel. And they were everywhere. A couple appear in almost every image Webb takes. In 2023, researchers started calling them “little red dots.” Astronomers have repeatedly pointed Webb toward the little red dots, wringing precious new information from these pixels of light.

Initially, researchers thought the dots looked kind of like galaxies. Later, they concluded that little red dots look more like the supermassive black holes that sit at the heart of most galaxies. These monstrous masses are themselves dark, but their formidable gravity violently vacuums up gas and other nearby matter, generating rings of hot, swirling detritus that completely outshine the stars around them.

Then, in the spring of 2025, two teams of astronomers simultaneously announced observations of a pair of little red dots that were unlike all the rest. In fact, they were unlike any object ever seen.

“In all the millions of [observations] we’ve taken with ground-based telescopes,” said Anna de Graaff, a researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy in Heidelberg, Germany, and head of one group, “there’s nothing that looks like these sources.”

The two teams of astronomers propose that they are looking at a new astronomical object: a topsy-turvy lump of hydrogen that shines with the light of billions of suns while hiding a black hole deep in its core. They call it a “black hole star.”

In a paper posted in September, astronomers took this analysis a step further. They argued that the Webb telescope is witnessing the births of supermassive black holes inside the cores of colossal stars. “There is a fundamentally new phenomenon afoot,” said Rohan Naidu, an astronomer at the University of Hawai’i.

But not everyone agrees with this bold interpretation. It has sparked a flurry of follow-up research and reignited a fierce debate over the nature of these peculiar pinpricks of light. “The field has gotten very polarized,” said Anna-Christina Eilers, an astrophysicist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology who studies little red dots.

When all you can see is a speck, it’s hard to tell what you’re looking at. All you know about it is its color and brightness. Astronomers first argued that little red dots were distant galaxies on the cosmic horizon, mainly because of their brightness. But galaxies that bright would have to be huge—and there was no known way for them to grow so big in just hundreds of millions of years. Astronomers dubbed them “universe breakers” for the way they seemed to demolish the standard cosmic timeline.

Then they took a closer look. De Graaff led one survey, called Red Unknowns: Bright Infrared Extragalactic Survey (Rubies), and Naidu co-led another survey, called Mirage or Miracle (MOM). These were two of a wave of surveys that trained the Webb telescope on distant objects, including little red dots, for hours at a time. They tabulated precisely what shades of light were coming from each dot and how bright the shades were. This detailed color breakdown, known as a spectrum, told astronomers a far more detailed story than the initial observations had. Different atoms shine in subtly different hues, so the spectrum provided a sense of what the object’s particles were doing.

The bombshell discovery in the little-red-dot spectra was that the colors of hydrogen were smeared out across multiple shades. Usually, seeing such an effect means you’re looking straight at an exposed black hole. Black holes whip hydrogen clouds around them at furious rates, with the clouds emitting slightly different colors depending on their speed. The net effect is that instead of seeing just the hue of hydrogen, you see a range of colors called a broad line. The wider this range, the faster the fastest hydrogen clouds are flying—and the more massive the black hole.

Many astronomers concluded that big black holes dotted the universe, washing out the light of the stars in their host galaxies. As black holes, the little red dots would appear red because dust—grainy stuff much more complicated than gas—was blocking their blue light.

Yet they still seemed weird. Most supermassive black holes flicker as they gulp down chunky streams of gas around them. They also beam powerful x-rays across the universe. Most little red dots seemed to be doing neither of these things.

But that didn’t trouble astronomers much; they expected to see some strangeness during the pandemonium of the early universe. And at least the black holes weren’t breaking any cosmological theories.

Then, in the spring of 2025, de Graaff’s and Naidu’s teams unveiled the two strangest dots yet.

What made these two little red dots exceptional was how red they were. Webb picked up almost no light in the bluer hues of their spectra. And at a particular shade of red, the colors abruptly got much, much brighter. This feature, known as a Balmer break, is something you see when looking at a hot ball of hydrogen gas—typically, certain types of stars or galaxies, which are made of many stars. Deep in a star’s core, nuclear fusion pumps out heat and light, which slowly filters up to the star’s surface. There, hydrogen atoms can become energized in a way that blocks bluer light and lets through only redder light. These red colors have a hump-shaped spectrum that reveals the overall temperature of the star’s surface.

But the new little red dots couldn’t literally be stars—they were way too bright. And they didn’t look much like black holes either. Black holes have an assortment of ringlike structures of different temperatures. They don’t typically produce a Balmer break, or the red, hump-shaped curve indicative of a stellar surface burning at a uniform 5,000 or so degrees Kelvin.

Naidu and de Graaff concluded that they were looking at the first examples of something combining the vigor of a black hole with the outward appearance of a star: a black hole star.

From the outside, a black hole star would appear as a huge agglomeration of hydrogen gas. If our sun were replaced with a black hole star, it would extend a dozen times farther than the orbit of Pluto. Out toward the edge, the star would boil unstably, sloughing off outer layers and explosively ejecting mass. “It’s going to be a very messy system where stuff is being blown out and falling back in,” de Graaff said. “I wouldn’t want to come too close.”

Deep in the center, invisible to the outside world, the star would be powered by a black hole. This black hole would pull gas around it, dramatically heating it and pushing light and energy outward, which would keep the outer layers of hydrogen from collapsing inward. In this way, the black hole would form the “engine” of the star, analogous to the fusion-powered core of our sun. Moving outward, material swirling around the black hole would beam out a range of colors that would slowly make their way toward the surface.

And as with certain stars, the hydrogen near the surface would stop the bluer light while letting the redder light pass through. The end result would be a gassy surface shining as brightly as a more exposed black hole but with the Balmer break and smooth red hump of a 5,000-kelvin star, de Graaff and Naidu theorized. As a bonus, the gas “cocoon” would also block x-rays, and the gas wouldn’t flicker much—which would explain two mysteries surrounding other little red dots.

But what about the broad lines, supposedly caused by hydrogen swirling fast around a black hole? Another group provided a possible explanation.

That group, which included Vadim Rusakov, an astronomer at the University of Manchester, had been scrutinizing the broad lines of the best-observed little red dots. Broad lines take the shape of a sharp mountain peak. But Rusakov and collaborators noticed that in many cases, these mountains sloped slightly more gently than would be expected if they came from fast-moving gas around a black hole. So they suggested that instead of coming from rotating gas, much of the spread of the hydrogen colors could come from light scattering off electrons.

They digitally removed the effect of this electron-induced smudging from their data, Rusakov said. After that, the broad lines stopped looking quite so broad and started looking more like light passing through a sluggishly churning shell of hydrogen gas in a particular state, similar to what you’d expect to see from a black hole star. The three teams—de Graaff’s, Naidu’s, and Rusakov’s — posted their findings on March 20, 2025—“black hole star date,” as some of the researchers called it.

Black hole stars could represent a new stage in the development of a supermassive black hole: First, a black hole would form in the center of a shell of hydrogen, together with a baby galaxy of normal stars around it. Then, over time, the black hole would eat its way out of its cocoon, gaining mass as it cleared the hydrogen gas away.

“We are seeing the seed,” Naidu said. “This is the birth of potentially every massive black hole in the universe.”

The black hole star enthusiasts appeal to Occam’s razor, arguing that their theory gives the simplest accounting of these two little red dots, and perhaps of little red dots in general. But simple is subjective, and astronomers have spent the last year in a lively debate about what’s really going on.

Even years after the discovery of the first little red dot, not much about them is settled. Dale Kocevski, an astrophysicist at Colby College, recalls leading a discussion about them at an April 2026 conference in Aspen, Colorado. He started by recapping what he hoped would be an uncontroversial idea about their trace amounts of blue light. “The group erupted into argument, and we couldn’t even get past the first bullet point,” he said.

Many astronomers still argue that little red dots are traditional black holes—even the new duo. “The data is really compelling,” said Roberto Maiolino of the University of Cambridge. “I’m a little bit more dubious about the interpretation.”

For each point in favor of black hole stars, Maiolino fires off a quick rebuttal. The lack of flicker? In the early universe, black holes may have had a steadier food supply and may therefore have been tidier eaters. The lack of x-rays? Standard galactic black holes are ringed by a thick doughnut of gas and dust, which can block most x-rays. He sees no reason to suspect the little red dots of being anything other than standard supermassive black holes.

The redness of the new objects is striking, he said, and he agrees that it means there must be a ton of gas between the black hole and us. But that gas could come in the form of the doughnut, or as puffy clouds that fill in patches of the black hole’s sky, as opposed to the shell of gas around a black hole star. Maiolino agrees that electron scattering likely contributes to broadening the lines in the spectra of some of the little red dots. But electron scattering also smears hydrogen lines from supermassive black holes, he said.

Maiolino and his collaborator, Piero Madau of the University of California, Santa Cruz, argue that the redness of the dots comes mainly from the angle at which we see them. The reddest dots are those that we happen to see edge on, their gassy doughnuts blocking our view. Webb also sees some “little blue dots.” These could be the same exposed black holes, viewed top-down, Maiolino and Madau pointed out in spring 2026. They also appeal to Occam’s razor—in this case arguing that black holes are a simpler explanation for little dots of all colors.

At this stage either theory—black hole or black hole star—could match what the Webb telescope has seen. “I don’t think that there is a compelling reason to prefer one or the other,” said Mauro Giavalisco, an astronomer at the University of Massachusetts, Amherst, who has spent much of his career interpreting the spectra of distant galaxies.

To test their interpretations, astronomers need a clearer picture of how black hole stars might form and how exactly they expect them to look.

Over the last few years, Mitchell Begelman has been teaching a graduate student seminar on little red dots at the University of Colorado, Boulder. It’s kept him reading the firehose of papers coming out on the subject. In these mysterious objects that weren’t quite stars and weren’t quite black holes, he recognized a ghost from his past: the quasi-star. “Suddenly the switch flipped, and I realized that this is what quasi-stars should look like,” Begelman said.

Begelman had proposed the existence of quasi-stars back in 2006, along with Marta Volonteri and Martin Rees, to explain observations of what looked like impossibly massive black holes. Their quasi-star theory offers one way to make a black hole star: The core of a vast gas cloud collapses to directly form a black hole, gathering the remainder of the cloud around it.

In 2025, Begelman and his collaborator Jason Dexter applied the quasi-star model to the little red dots. They estimated that quasi-stars could quickly assemble themselves in a few million years before settling into a more mature form that would look just like little red dots. They would do this for tens of millions of years—lasting long enough for Webb to spot them.

In 2026, Giavalisco worked with a team to flesh out the quasi-star model as an origin for black hole stars, which he finds to be a natural way of explaining how little red dots could mask the signs of a feeding black hole. He points out that our sun performs the same trick, hiding its explosive fusion, just on a much smaller scale. “We have billions and billions of hydrogen bombs exploding every second, and yet we see none of them,” he said.

Giavalisco and his collaborators found that their new model of a quasi-star fit the spectra of de Graaf’s and Naidu’s objects even better than the initial models had. He thinks the quasi-star theory is a plausible explanation for the little red dots but remains open to other ideas. “I just want to know the truth,” he said.

In the meantime, researchers are starting to search for another distinguishing pattern: If little red dots are black hole stars formed in the early universe, then they should grow rarer over time as they each break free from their shells and reveal their inner black holes.

In an August 2026 census of both red and blue dots broken up into different eras, Kocevski of Colby College and his collaborators found exactly that pattern. In the data, as the universe approaches 2 billion to 3 billion years of age, the little red dots seem to vanish—preliminary evidence that little red dots, as black hole stars, might really be a puberty-like phase for many supermassive black holes.

Theorists are already working out what that puberty-like phase might be like. In another analysis, posted on September 8, Naidu, de Graaff, Eilers, and their collaborators tested out an assortment of techniques for deducing the mass of a black hole “seed” inside a black hole star. These hidden black holes seemed to be far less massive than standard, exposed black holes. The scientists propose that Webb is catching supermassive black hole stars—up to 1 million times the mass of the sun—in the act of incubating the universe’s first big black holes.

Some researchers, including Kocevski, still aren’t sure. Kocevski suspects that the universe is a messy place, and that some little red dots are truly as starlike as the black hole star camp is arguing. Others, he thinks, will be more like standard black holes, as the other camp argues. “I have a sneaking suspicion that we’re both right,” he said.

#1516. My Family’s Dark, Hidden History With Institutionalization

My great-great-grandfather was the namesake of an infamous institution for the disabled. From long-neglected records of the property, the lives of forgotten people were uncovered.

On the Fernald campus. From left to right: Walter Jr, Walter Sr., Tom (the eventual father of Barbara and Winton), Helen (my great-grandmother), and Katherine.

There is a single name across the front of my family gravestone: FERNALD. A simple slab tucked beneath a low-hanging spruce, it would be easy to miss among the grand monuments and lush gardens surrounding it at Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge, Massachusetts. But for many people in the state, the name it bears can still incite a shiver of terror. It was once invoked by teachers and parents as a threat to misbehaving children: “Keep it up and I’ll send you to the Fernald.”

The Walter E. Fernald State School and Hospital, known to locals as the Fernald, sits on a hill in Waltham, Massachusetts, a few miles down the road from my family’s plot. Now an abandoned heap of earth and brick, from its founding in 1848 until its closure in 2014, the Fernald was the oldest institution for intellectually and developmentally disabled people in the Western Hemisphere. For decades, thousands of disabled, neglected, or “behaviorally troubled” children were brought there; many never left. In the mid-20th century, it became one of the nation’s most infamous institutions after journalists, activists, and lawyers brought to light the squalid conditions inside the Fernald, its neglect of patients, and even the medical experimentation, sponsored by corporations like Quaker Oats, that took place there.

In the spring of 2024, I stood before the family stone as my grandma was being buried. She had died a year earlier, and her name was being added to a long list on the back of the stone which began with that of our patriarch: “Walter E. Fernald.” For 37 years, he was the superintendent of the institution that was then called the Massachusetts School for Idiotic and Feeble-Minded Youth. When he died, 100 years before his granddaughter did, flags flew at half-mast across the state, The Boston Globe wrote that his life inspired “something approaching to awe,” and the institution he had run took his name—an honor that ultimately cemented his dishonor.

My grandma had been the family archivist: Cabinets in her home office overflowed with yellowed newspaper clippings and family trees printed from her 1992 Macintosh. I never paid much attention when she extolled our Mayflower origins or showed me photographs of scowling ladies who were apparently related to me. But in September 2022, early in my senior year of college, while I was mapping out a thesis about my older brother’s hospitalizations with a brain illness, I remembered the occasional conversations I had had with her about her famous grandfather. A quick Google search sent a shock of shame up my spine—there were a slew of stories about abuse at the institution he had once run, but also damning information about the man himself. He was labeled a “eugenicist,” a “sterilizer,” a “monster.”

This revelation led me to drive up to my grandma’s nursing home in Northampton, Massachusetts, to ask her about him. Her health was declining at this point, but she was adamant that I had it all wrong: Walter Fernald was not a monster; he was a remarkable man who would visit every single resident each night before bedtime and taught service to others  above all. She insisted that there were no “horror stories” to tell, no matter how much I “seem to want them.” I kept replaying that last point over in my head as I drove back to campus, past the fast fading leaves. Did I want them?

All I knew was that my obsession with Fernald’s legacy only grew. I was lucky that my grandma was such a fastidious record-keeper. I went through her archives, which had been tossed into bankers boxes and were sitting outside in the barn at her old house, and spoke to local historians, but I was only able to view a few of the tens of thousands of records belonging to Fernald that were held in the state’s archives. I could only access the small number that had the patient names redacted due to a draconian Massachusetts law that restricted access to institutional medical records indefinitely—rather than the standard 50 years as specified in the federal Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA). That meant that the Fernald records from 1848 were treated the same as those from 2014.

What I was able to uncover complicated both the condemnations of Walter Fernald that I found online and my grandma’s unequivocal praise. He was undoubtedly a fierce supporter of what was then a radical idea: that disabled children should be offered treatment and education rather than being abandoned or neglected by their families. Through his work at the institution and in local school districts, he created the foundations of what we now call special education. But while he was opposed to the practice of forcibly sterilizing residents, he promoted eugenicist ideas about their potential criminality and, for a period of his career, called for their lifelong segregation from the surrounding community. These ideas turned his Waltham institution into the archetype for a national state hospital system—hundreds of sister institutions were established from Maine to California that were designed to permanently warehouse the disabled under the guise of “public safety.” This system—now often forgotten, its abandoned institutions the backdrop to haunted houses or misremembered as asylums (which served the mentally ill and typically had short-term, rather than indefinite, stays)—was among the largest public investments in the country from the late 19th to the mid-20th century. At its peak in 1967, it held nearly 200,000 people, very often against their will; Walter Fernald was one of its original masterminds.

I never got to share what I had learned with my grandma. She died a couple months after my visit. Would she have been disappointed by who her grandfather really was? Vindicated? In denial? I only knew she would have been proud of me for trying to understand our family history. Now, as she was being interred beside Walter, I recognized all the names on the back of the stone. I knew the story. I remembered.

But as I stepped back from the gravestone, I felt my foot drop onto stone instead of grass. I looked down and saw I was standing on a barely visible mud-caked slab with a name I didn’t recognize: “Barbara M. Fernald 1921–1935.” I called to my mother, “Ma, who is this?” She came over and together we scraped the mud off with our soles. She didn’t know, perhaps a cousin of my grandmother’s who had died young. What had kept her name off the central stone? Beside Barbara’s stone I noticed an even smaller square with no words on it—a nameless grave.

Who was Barbara? Who lay beneath that bare slab? Soon after my grandmother’s funeral, Alex Green, a fellow researcher I had met through my work, contacted me. Alex, a lecturer in public policy at Harvard University who was then finishing a book about my great-great-grandfather, had commiserated with my struggles with the state’s exceptionally restrictive records law. He had sued the state to get access to records from Fernald that were over 100 years old and, in order to view them, agreed to have his notes checked each day to make sure he wasn’t recording confidential patient information. He was so frustrated by the obfuscation that he persuaded the Legislature to create a Special Commission on State Institutions to study, among other things, the state’s actions with regard to records and offer recommendations for ways to improve access to them. Signed into law in July 2022, the 15-person commission—the nation’s first disability rights-led human rights inquiry into the legacy of institutionalization—met for the first time in June 2023.

“This may be a bit of a shock,” he now told me, “but I am fairly certain Fernald had two grandchildren who died in institutions for children with intellectual disabilities.” He had come across offhand references to them in his research. Tom Fernald, my grandmother’s uncle, had two children who both had PKU, a now-treatable genetic condition that causes toxic buildup of the amino acid phenylalanine and leads to progressive brain damage. Barbara Fernald had died at age 13 at a private facility in Pennsylvania in March 1935. Her younger brother Winton had been admitted to Fernald’s sister school, the Wrentham State School, where he died at age 21 in June of 1946. That was all Alex had.

The private facility in Pennsylvania was long closed, and according to the local historical society, its records had almost certainly been destroyed or lost. But I knew Winton’s records must be out there, in some basement archive, but that the state was not going to give me access to them, even though I was related to him and he was long dead.

This restriction was not only absurd but hypocritical. I knew, despite the state’s supposed emphasis on protecting patients’ identities, that thousands of records from as recent as the 1990s had been left on the abandoned grounds of the Fernald for hordes of trespassers to steal and vandalize. I knew it because I’d seen the documents myself.

A couple months after my grandmother’s death, on a cold January morning in 2023, I sat in a car outside the metal gate leading to abandoned acres of the Fernald. I watched Bryan Parcival, a local photographer and Fernald historian I’d befriended through my research, jostle the gate’s lock. “If this doesn’t work we’ll have to hike on,” he said as he shook and twisted the key. With a sudden crack, the lock opened and fell to the ground. He pushed open the groaning gates and hopped back into the driver’s seat. “I got this key five years ago when the state had me take photos of this place. The guy who gave it to me, some maintenance man, died a few weeks later, so no one knows I have this.” He lifted the little silver key up to my face and smiled. “You ready?” he asked, and without waiting for my reply, he drove us past the “No Trespassing” signs and onto the property no one in my family had set foot on in decades.

I followed Bryan’s footsteps as he darted across the buckling wood floors into the Asylum building, which once served as the institution’s main hospital. Mountains of rusted wheelchairs lay crumpled on their sides beside wooden restraint chairs with thick black ropes. Spilling from cabinets was a stream of papers that lay beside books with broken spines, children’s drawings of butterflies, sheet music for “Ode to Joy,” forks, knives, and spoons. I picked up one of the loose papers, a patient record from 1995 titled “Psychotropic Medical Treatment Plan” with a person’s name, birthday, medications, symptoms, and a diagnosis of “chronic schizophrenia” clearly visible. It was one of thousands like it that dotted the floor.

Of course, the campus was technically off-limits. But in 2020 and 2021, the cameras Bryan had set up around the property recorded more than 5,300 incidents of trespassing, during which buildings were frequently damaged and records were stolen, urinated on, and graffitied. One video showed people shooting pornography in the abandoned library that had once housed one of the largest collections of disability literature in the nation. He shared all of this with the City of Waltham, which has owned the property since the institution’s closure. Nothing happened. A police spokesperson told me that they were unable to secure the property due to its size. Urban explorers shared their adventures openly on social media. A local teenager told me it was a rite of passage for kids in the city to visit the campus and that he personally had multiple boxes of patient records in his room that he had swiped. “Do you want to see them?” he asked.

I wrote an article in early 2024 for The Boston Globe about these blatant violations of both HIPAA and the state’s own more restrictive records law that had taken place with the knowledge of the state and the city. The Massachusetts Department of Developmental Services (DDS) blamed the state’s Division of Capital Asset Management and Maintenance (DCAMM) for failing to remove the records in 2014. The DCAMM responded by shifting the blame back to the DDS. Meanwhile, Waltham’s mayor stayed mum. The article changed little for the campus itself. Over the past two years, some of its oldest buildings have been destroyed by arson, and the town has opened a park on a corner of the property that includes miniature golf and a train. The state did remove thousands of records from the campus. According to Caitlin Ramos, the curator at the Massachusetts State Archives, they were extremely damaged and were ultimately all destroyed by the DDS.

But the article did have one major effect: People who were desperate to get some information about loved ones who had died at the Fernald began to reach out to me. One had heard that his sister had choked to death on peanut butter while she wasn’t being properly supervised, another wanted to track down a beloved aunt who had vanished during his childhood, and another had recently discovered that he had a cousin he had never been told about, and he wanted to find his unmarked grave and buy a proper headstone. The state had previously rebuffed them all, telling them it was to protect the privacy of the patients. What privacy was there on the moldy floors of the Fernald?

One of those who had been denied access was Susan Ruderman, a 64-year-old fifth-generation Bostonian who had long known that her mother, Betty, had a missing sibling. In January 1933, when Betty was 4, her half-brother didn’t come home after school. “Where’s Larry?” she recalled her older brother asking. Their mother turned to the two children and barked, “Never speak of him again!”

Despite her mother’s admonition, Betty occasionally mentioned her long-lost sibling to her daughter. In 2019, years after Betty’s death, Susan became concerned that she might be a carrier of PKU and decided to track down what happened to the lost boy, whose name was all she had to go by. Combing through the 1940 US Census, she found an entry for Lawrence Barry. He was listed as a resident of the Fernald. A request to the state archives for his records led to a typical bureaucratic response: She was told that she needed to hire a lawyer, go to court to prove lineage, and be appointed guardian of his estate. It was too much; she begrudgingly let Larry slip back into the well of silence where he’d sat for decades.

But in May 2025, the Special Commission on State Institutions that Alex had helped start and which he served as vice chair of, released its final report on the decades of mistreatment and disregard for records and graves at the Fernald and other state institutions. In response, in November of that year, Governor Maura Healey signed a law that opened the state’s institutional records to families and researchers 75 years after their creation or, if still held by the DDS or the Department of Mental Health, 50 years after the death of the patient. Alex estimates that 75 to 100 million formerly hidden records were found, received, and opened to the public and that more than 1,000 people have been able to access the story of their loved ones. (The state archive did not immediately keep track of access to institutional records under the new law, but between July 1 and October 13 of this year, 143 people received records; the vast majority are family members of former patients.) The new law also temporarily paused the destruction of documents, a de facto admission that the state had been destroying them. Massachusetts, once one of the most restrictive states for access to institutional records, overnight became one of the most transparent.

Susan made a new request and received Larry’s records within days. There was no evidence of PKU in Larry’s records, but what they did reveal was that he was admitted to the Fernald in 1933 at the age of 8 as a “neglected child,” and that he was determined to escape. A medical note dated November 17, 1933, a few months after he was brought to the Fernald, indicated that he was constantly getting into fights and sprinting from the campus into the surrounding neighborhoods. According to the records, after he was brought back on one occasion, he said, “[I’m] posted anyway, so what’s the use of being good?” The file documents years of constant escapes from wards, infirmaries, haylofts, and, once, from a bean patch on the school’s farm. He stole bikes, made it to train stations, and was caught by local residents and police in towns near and far. He was continually being brought back. His yearly files often ended with the same two sentences: “He had no visitors and no correspondence. Clothing is free.”

After an escape when he was 18, the institution gave up on tracking him down. The file continues with notes that the Fernald staff occasionally received phone calls reporting that he was in and out of prisons and other institutions. The final accessible record was from January 1951, when a Waltham police officer questioned a suspicious-looking man at the employee residence at the Fernald; the man claimed that he was a sailor from California and thought the place was a hotel. In fact, he was Larry Barry, returning to the place he hated so much that he’d escaped a hundred times but that was also the only home he’d ever known. The unnamed staff member who wrote the note told the police officer that Larry was not a patient anymore and “they were free to deal with him as they saw fit.” Larry was taken away by the police.

While many questions are left unanswered, Susan says this access was an unbelievable gift—an opportunity to finally get a glimpse of a family member who had been relegated to oblivion since that cold January day in 1933. She is determined to find out what happened to him after he was taken by the Waltham police in 1951, to find his grave, and, if necessary, give it a proper stone.

Just as it did for Susan and the dozens of other people who reached out to me for help, the change in the law meant I could access not only Walter Fernald’s records but also those of his grandson. Within a matter of days, I received Winton’s records from the DDS in an e-mail. It included his autopsy report, which gave him a mental age of 2 years and 10 months and a diagnosis of “imbecile,” a series of medical and psychological notes from his admission at the age of 1 to his death 20 years later, and two photos: one at age 7, standing in a field with a mischievous smile on his face, and another at age 20, in an oversize suit with a large white handkerchief protruding awkwardly from the chest pocket, staring grimly down against a black background. The records show that Winton was constantly shredding his clothes and, as he grew older, smashing his fists into windows. He often had to get stitches on his knuckles. He occasionally had visitors, but his parents, perhaps because of the trauma of institutionalizing two of their children, moved to Florida shortly after his birth and apparently rarely visited Massachusetts.

Wrentham was founded and run by one of Walter Fernald’s closest protégés, Dr. George Wallace. It is very likely that this is why Winton was brought there, and perhaps, though the records don’t reflect this, he received special attention because of his last name. But after Wallace’s sudden death in 1930, Wrentham, like most institutions in the period coinciding with the Great Depression and then the Second World War, had scant funding and dangerously few employees. One staff member per 50 residents was common. Whatever special attention he might have had was likely gone—it is no wonder he kept smashing his fist into windows.

In the final pages of his records, I found proof that Winton was indeed buried under that small stone square at the family plot at Mount Auburn. The files have no mention of his grandfather, the man who had bought that plot, whose name had been bestowed on the institution a few miles down the road, and who had helped create the very system of institutionalization in which his grandson had died. No, it just says Winton died with his mother at his bedside, that he was cremated and buried beside his sister, Barbara, laid to rest 10 years earlier. Unlike her, he did not get a name.

These records cannot do much. Written in the institution’s removed hand, they cannot truly tell his experience, what he was like, what he thought or felt or desired. His life was just one drop in a great ocean of forgotten lives. But is that so special? Despite our best attempts to archive the past, to keep the records from rotting, from potter’s fields to Mount Auburn, from institutions to families, isn’t forgetting the one inevitable thing?

But even so, why do I keep looking at the sad photographs of this faraway boy? What do I want from these “horror stories”? Why do I keep reading and rereading the records, especially returning to the rare moments when he speaks? Such as the January 3, 1941, examination in which he kept repeating, “So glad I’m going home” and “I hate this damn dump.” Or on November 12, 1935, when he kept begging the nurse, “Turn light on, Miss. It’s dark, Miss.” The game, the report says, was an “obsession with him and it was practically impossible to get him to stop.” He jumped up and down and up and down, flicking the light on and off and on and off, answering questions at random, until he finally declared, “No more.”

Oliver Egger is a writer and editor based in New Haven, Connecticut.

HOPE, Ark. — For Senate candidate Hallie Shoffner, the most important political label she wears isn't that she's a Democrat. Or that she's not a Republican. Instead, it's that she is an Arkansan.

As a lunchtime crowd ate at Tailgater's Burger Company Friday, Shoffner decried what she called a historically unproductive Congress driven by extreme voices in each party and pitched herself to receptive — if skeptical — voters as a candidate who would better serve their needs.

But in the final days of the 2026 midterm election, Shoffner and other Arkansas Democrats are crisscrossing the state with a message that seeks to capitalize on the Trump administration's unpopularity, stubbornly high prices and anti-incumbency vibes.

"We're the sleeper race," Shoffner told NPR. "I mean, we're the race that no one's noticed and that no one is going to see coming."

Republicans have taken some notice, pre-emptively pouring millions of dollars into the Senate race, the Little Rock-based 2nd Congressional District and robust get-out-the vote efforts to try and stem the rising tide of dissatisfaction with the party.

But even in a midterm cycle where Senate races in states like Kansas, Iowa, Ohio and Texas are viewed as tossups, and where the landscape of potentially competitive House races stretches deep into conservative territory, Arkansas seems a bridge too far for election forecasters.

Arkansas' 2nd Congressional District is considered "Likely Republican," according to the Cook Political Report, and its Senate race is "Solid Republican."

At an outdoor coffee shop in Arkadelphia, Vanilla Hannah said she was there to support Shoffner and talk about her own reelection bid to be a local justice of the peace. "When it comes down to basic needs or whatever, the 'D' or the 'R' after your name don't mean a thing, because we're all suffering and all having the same issues and everybody's ready for a change," she said. #1550. How Many Billions Would It Take to Hold Meta Accountable?

Earlier this week, a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay a $25 billion fine for violating consumer protection laws dating back to the Cambridge Analytica scandal. $25 billion is a massive fine, the largest Meta has ever faced, but it still had us folks at WIRED wondering: Would Meta even feel the pain?

A quick recap of the case: Filed in 2021, the suit alleged that the company violated the state’s Unfair Practices Act by lying to Meta users about the way it handled their data. (Later, New Mexico’s attorney general also made claims against Meta’s content moderation policies.) In September, a jury ruled in favor of the state. Then came the multibillion-dollar penalty.

“This ruling, and the Attorney General's approach to enforcement, makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico,” C.J. Mahoney, Meta’s chief legal officer, said in a statement. “At its core, this lawsuit seeks to impose a tax on speech to enforce new rules for what people can say online. We are confident we will prevail on appeal.”

Meta’s legal troubles have proliferated so much this year that it’s hard to keep track of them. Earlier this year in New Mexico, a jury ruled that the social media company should pay $375 million for violating the state’s Unfair Practices Act. That trial was related to child safety. A second phase of the case resulted in Meta being ordered to pay $567 million.

Meanwhile, a Los Angeles County superior court found Meta—and Google-owned YouTube—liable for harming a user’s mental health with the addictive design features of their apps. The plaintiff, a young woman, was awarded $6 million; Meta is on the hook for the bulk of that payment. Then in August, Meta settled a landmark federal case brought against the company by the majority of US states. This suit, once again, centered on addictive features in its apps that were alleged to be harmful to kids. As part of that settlement, Meta will pay the states between $17 and $18 billion over 10 years.

Added up, that’s a lot of billions. And yet: It’s still just the cost of doing business for Meta. Reuters reports that in the most recent New Mexico trial, the state’s attorneys originally asked Judge Francis Mathew to award the state between $35 and $45 billion. That would’ve been significant for the state, but would that extra $20 billion have kneecapped Meta? Nope.

Even if Meta was forced to pay the total maximum fine of the New Mexico ruling—a whopping $219 billion, or $5,000 across 43 million violations, reduced by the judge to $25 billion—the company would have felt only a mild sting. As of writing, Meta is worth around $1.7 trillion. Its stock took a hit on Thursday, but that was due to a confluence of factors, like Meta’s earnings report on Wednesday, the company’s massive capital expenditures on AI, and AI stocks dragging overall.

At this point, with no immediate monetary damage to Meta, it would take an accumulation of events to force some kind of change, legal experts tell me. For one, Meta has a clear perception problem: Juries are ruling against it, protesters have railed against it, even its own employees have been more vocal about the company’s internal policies.

Every time Meta doesn’t settle a case, the evidence presented during trial can be scrutinized by the public and used in future cases against Meta, says Corban Rhodes, partner at DiCello Levitt, the outside law firm representing New Mexico in this case. “This trial included more than 100 exhibits that we were able to put into public record, things that had been kept secret for more than a decade since Cambridge Analytica,” Rhodes told WIRED.

One such item is a 2018 email that then COO Sheryl Sandberg sent to a few colleagues days after the Cambridge Analytica scandal broke. Sandberg wrote, “The single biggest issue we face—and I really need everyone to know this—is that there were a MILLION developers with access to the data. We are going to have many, many, many more moments like this in the next month and year.”

There are early signals that private insurers may start balking at having to cover the cost of a tech company’s legal defenses. Some experts are suggesting that insurance companies could even end up being de facto governance for AI, as they “inject responsibility into how AI is designed, developed, and deployed as a condition for obtaining insurance.” Meta learned this firsthand in March, when a Delaware judge ruled that insurers do not have a duty to defend Meta against the literal thousands of lawsuits being brought against it.

There’s no well-defined number at which Meta suddenly becomes vulnerable—the company is unlikely to ever fold from legal penalties alone. But problems could crop up if shareholders or board members stop seeing the company as some inanimate entity and start to focus on the individual directors or officers who have been making bad decisions, says Brian Quinn, a law professor at Boston College Law School who studies corporate law, M&A, and transaction law. Like, say, the people who keep appearing in lawsuits.

“The way that indemnification law is written right now, the fact that a person in a company was found to have violated a law doesn’t necessarily make them liable. It can be believed that they were acting in good faith, or in the best interest of their company,” Quinn says. “But problems start to arise if shareholders say, wait a minute, there’s not only evidence that this director or operator is violating the law, but it has appeared in the same set of facts seven times now.”

After a huge accumulation of these kinds of results, Quinn says, some angry shareholders might stand up and say the corporation should no longer be able to indemnify those directors. That’s when you might see change, because Meta’s executives would no longer be as protected.

Finally, since litigation is often behind the curve on society-disrupting technology, Meta’s legal woes are likely far from over—especially as it wades deeper into AI. “Regulators and courts have for a long time been focused on the impact of social media 1.0 and 2.0, and I think we’re moving on from that era,” says Rhodes. “I expect the next wave of litigation will be focused on AI.”
