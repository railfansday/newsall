#1112. Why Chinese tech brands are outpacing Western rivals from EVs to robot vacuums

From action cameras and smartphones to EVs and robot vacuums, Chinese brands are gaining ground worldwide, despite privacy and security concerns. What's driving their rise, and can European rivals keep up?

Do you remember the days when GoPro was synonymous with action cameras?

At its peak in 2015, the US company reigned supreme with around 6.6 million units sold that year and a market share of over 70%. Today, the pioneer is fighting for survival.

GoPro's biggest rivals, DJI and Insta360, entered the action camera market in 2019 and 2021, respectively. Today, they account for more than 90% of global smart handheld camera sales.

Alexander Hannemann, who regularly uses action cameras to film his motorcycle rides, says he switched from GoPro to Insta360 around two years ago.

"The quality is much better than a GoPro, even when I'm speeding up and going into corners at like 80 kilometers per hour," he told DW.

GoPro's case is not an exception. Since around 2010, many Chinese brands have not only caught up but surpassed once-dominant Western rivals across the consumer technology industry, from smart TVs to robot vacuums.

Gone are the days when "Made in China" stood for cheap, mass-produced products.

"China is still making affordable products today," says Chris Pereira, a Canadian entrepreneur and author with over 20 years of experience in China. "But the products are also high quality."

This combination has led to well over half of new consumer tech products globally coming from Chinese suppliers, Pereira adds.

BYD, Xpeng and other Chinese car manufactures sold almost two-thirds of all electric cars last year, surpassing incumbents like Tesla. In robot vacuums, Chinese brands dominate the global market, leaving former pioneer US company iRobot struggling to compete.

So why do Chinese companies increasingly outperform their Western rivals?

Pereira, whose consultancy iMpact helps Chinese companies gain traction overseas, attributes China's success to fierce domestic competition in cities like Shenzhen, Hangzhou and Shanghai; highly integrated supply chains and a can-do business culture that allows companies to innovate; and respond to problems quickly.

Other key reasons for China's competitive edge include unparalleled industrial automation, the strategic use of AI in manufacturing, responsiveness from regulators as well as relatively cheap labor, which together leads to significantly lower production costs and much faster innovation cycles than in the world's most advanced economies.

Joey Cheng, who moved to the US for his PhD studies over 20 years ago, is familiar with both the American consumer and the Chinese producer sides.

While his startup is based in the US, almost all the manufacturing is done in Shenzhen, where a large share of the world's hardware and consumer electronics are made.

"The supply chain in Shenzhen is basically inevitable for cost cutting and quick product development," Cheng says. "Especially as a startup you must get the product out quickly, or you'll get blown out of the water."

Although consumers are flocking to Chinese companies, some reservations persist.

A recent poll found that more than 90% of Europeans do not trust Chinese technology companies with their data, while about 80% distrust US firms. Germans expressed the highest level of mistrust.

Nadine, a German travel blogger who uses DJI and Insta360 products, says she wouldn't use any Asian products at home.

"My home robot vacuum is from a German company for precisely this reason: So the data remains compliant with German regulations. After all, manufacturers can connect directly to the camera," she said, referring to camera and audio hacking incidents involving robot vacuums.

Still, Pereira says that Chinese companies expanding abroad have to pay a "trust premium."

"This trust deficit means that they need to be extra competitive in terms of product and price in order for the market to choose them," he says, adding that the gap can be narrowed by a presence in overseas markets like BYD's first European passenger-car plant, expected to open in Hungary later this year.

Chinese companies say they comply with EU data protection rules. However, domestic security laws in the US and China can require firms to hand over data to authorities, and German officials note limited oversight of whether data is transferred from European servers abroad.

In addition to privacy concerns, Chinese consumer tech firms have come under scrutiny over espionage and other risks, most recently regarding connected devices like EVs.

In light of security concerns, trade deficits and China's overall dominance, the US and several other Western countries have hit Chinese imports and companies with tariffs, blacklists and strict requirements. In late July, the Trump administration banned the import of humanoid robots manufactured outside the US.

A representative of Chinese robot maker Primebot told DW that all Chinese consumer tech companies are feeling the effects.

"We might seek cooperation with a local US company. Another potential solution is building a factory there," he said.

European countries and the EU are also weighing more tariffs against Chinese companies.

Chris Pereira foresees that the tariffs will accelerate two trends among Chinese companies: Moving more manufacturing overseas and an increasing focus on challenging top-tier Western brands with premium goods.

"Chinese officials and companies recognize that manufacturing needs to be localized to a certain degree. One reason is to assuage the challenges of exporting, another is to create trust locally," Pereira sums up.

"The other trend is that once duties wipe out the price advantage at the bottom, the margin left is at the top, so Chinese brands are increasingly competing directly with established Western names, where trust matters most."

"Chinese regulators tell you very clearly and quickly what you can do. That speed helps companies iterate and succeed overseas," Pereira says.

#1113. What’s It Like Inside a Data Center?

Northern Virginia is laden with structures that house data for Fortune 500 companies. I went inside just one of those monstrosities that bothers its neighbors.

Northern Virginia is laden with structures that house data for Fortune 500 companies. I went inside just one of those monstrosities that bothers its neighbors.

The sun was beating down as I made my way through a quiet community in Ashburn, Virginia. The roads were wide and lined with cars; there was a nice central playground with no one playing in it; the houses seemed monstrously large; and there were ample trees dotting the clean-cut lawns of the neighborhood.

In so many ways, it felt like your typical US suburb, until you looked past the thick wall of trees that defined its boundaries. The white walls of a large facility peeked out from above the deep green foliage. It ran on in the distance, the large houses making it impossible to see its full extent. Towering above it a little farther away were the high-voltage transmission lines that fed it with electricity. The trees acted like a shield, allowing those who lived in the community not to think about what stood on their doorstep and the growing demands that came from the surrounding industrial expansion. I’m talking, of course, about hyperscale data centers.

No location on the entire planet has more data centers than Virginia. The state holds more than 13 percent of global hyperscale capacity—double that of Beijing, which comes in second. There were nearly 600 data centers within the state’s boundaries in 2025, about 150 of which were hyperscale—the monstrously large variety that have become much more common since the advent of generative AI—and most of them were clustered in an area in the north of the state around Ashburn, which has since been dubbed “Data Center Alley.”

Data centers are unavoidable in Northern Virginia. When I sat on a bench next to a neighborhood lake, a hyperscale data center owned by Microsoft stared back at me from the opposite bank. As I drove through industrial parks, they were dotted throughout. When I pulled into a shopping area to grab a bite to eat, facilities that powered Amazon Web Services loomed behind the strip mall. I could even feel their influence in the street names: Cloud Plaza, Performance Circle, and, worst of all, Growth Mindset Lane. We can thank Microsoft for that one.

In short, they were everywhere when I visited in 2025, and following them wherever they set down were the high-voltage power lines needed to keep them running. Data centers were remaking the fabric of a whole region; there seemed to be no end in sight for their expansion. They had become a major industry, and neither worries over local resource demands nor growing concern from residents seemed able to stop the ambitions of some of the most powerful companies on the planet.

It’s hard to generalize about the data centers themselves. They come in many shapes and sizes, operate at various scales—from rooms in a nondescript building to entire parks of warehouse-sized facilities crammed with thousands of servers apiece—and seem to be getting bigger with every passing year. Some of them exist to serve the companies operating them; others exist as places where many different groups can rent space for servers of their own. Certain facilities are cooled with air-conditioning, while others depend more on water-based cooling, and even the way those systems are implemented can differ. I guess what I’m trying to say is that one description of a data center will never cover the gamut: There is some standardization, but there is also a lot of room for operators to put their own stamp on how their facility or network of locations is going to work.

The interiors of some Google data centers have clusters of brightly colored tubes running throughout them—echoing the primary colors present in the company’s branding—while the average Microsoft or Amazon facility might appear more utilitarian. Digital Realty is one of the largest data-center operators in the world. The company has two major lines of business: It builds entire facilities that then get leased to other companies, including the major cloud providers, but it also offers colocation services. Those are facilities where Digital Realty runs the data center, renting space in racks to other companies that they can then fill with their own servers. I stopped to walk the length of one of its facilities in Northern Virginia. The simplest way to describe it is to say that it looks like a massive box. The company’s logo was visible near the upper portion of the building, and it had some different shades of gray and blue across its exterior.

The data center seemed quiet that weekend afternoon, though even on a weekday there weren’t likely to be many people there. Data centers don’t need many employees to keep them clean, secure, and running; the bulk of the jobs they provide are created in the construction phase and mostly disappear once that’s over. The parking lot at the front of the building was surrounded by a large black fence that went around the entire property. I wasn’t trying to get in, but I did wonder if there was a security officer behind a desk somewhere inside the building peering at me through the video feed of one of the facility’s many security cameras. Data centers are notoriously well-guarded, to keep their data safe and to assuage the worries of their clients.

There wasn’t much to see as I walked along the fence, the spikes at the top of each pole warning me not to try anything rash. It took nearly five minutes to walk from one end of the building to the other, and this wasn’t even one of the bigger ones I’d seen that day. At the far end, a little beyond the fence, I walked onto a wooden platform where I could make out a serene pond obscured by tall trees and plants. The chirps and trills of birds and insects were just barely audible over the buzz of the air-conditioning system on the facility’s roof. The pond appeared impossible to access as its lifeless neighbor encroached upon it.

When I pulled into an outdoor shopping mall, I parked across from the shell of a structure that was soon to become a data center. Some tech companies will take over an existing facility and convert it into a data center, but most end up being built from scratch on vast tracts of newly cleared land. Towering outer walls of concrete reached far above even the retail stores that surrounded me. Each one was held up with steel braces as they waited for the installation of roof trusses that would be used to bind the various pieces together. After that, it would all be sealed against the elements with some utilitarian exterior cladding, then fitted with the cabling, temperature-regulation systems, power backups, and everything else needed to get thousands of servers running and wired to the Internet.

It’s not always easy to know when a data center is slated for a community before it starts rising out of the ground—or, when residents do get that information, who is actually behind it. Consider Lowell Charter Township, which sits about 20 miles outside Grand Rapids, Michigan. In 2025, its residents learned that a new data center was planned for their area, and they saw their local leaders moving fast to rezone the land and get any barriers out of the way to make it happen. Yet no one would reveal which company would ultimately run the facility.

The public was not happy, and near the end of the year so many angry residents crowded into a planning-commission meeting that officials were forced to put the project on ice. It was only then that Microsoft finally came out from behind the curtain to reveal itself as the proprietor.

Situations like this are not uncommon, though they rarely end in the outcome that the residents of Lowell achieved. Major tech firms use shell companies to hide their involvement in data-center projects until it’s too late for locals to stop them, just as Google did in The Dalles, Oregon, when it used the name “Design LLC.” Amazon has gone by “Razor5 LLC” in Indiana, and Meta deployed “Sidecat LLC” in Ohio and “Degas LLC” in Wisconsin.

Tech companies need a lot of data centers to fuel their ambitions. They do not need nearly as many offices where engineers create the products that justify the need for data centers. When I saw that shell of a data center in Virginia, I was also seeing the phase in which it would provide the most employment. During construction, a data-center project tends to employ over 1,000 people—even more for the monstrously large hyperscale facilities—but once they’re done, the employment numbers tend to drop into the dozens. These are not high-paying engineering jobs: Many of the workers are doing security, cleaning, or conducting maintenance on the systems. Is that really worth hundreds of thousands, if not millions, of dollars per job in tax breaks and incentives? A growing number of Virginians didn’t think so.

The rush to build out data centers in response to the generative-AI boom has led to a renewed rush for land, this time led by the companies competing in the AI race or trying to profit from powering it. With time of the essence, access to electricity has become one of the defining metrics of a site’s viability. Companies can’t wait years for capacity to be built or grid connections to be made. In some cases, that competitive pressure has led companies to bypass some of the demands they typically make of local governments—as was the case when xAI, Elon Musk’s AI company, did not demand property-tax abatements from Memphis, Tennessee. Musk wanted the data center up and running as quickly as possible. But the construction boom has also lifted some surprising companies along with it. Construction firms clearly benefit from all the additional projects they get hired to complete, while electricity companies and computer-hardware manufacturers find there’s a much greater demand for what they’re selling.

Communities like the one I visited in Virginia were feeling the brunt of the AI boom. As the data centers got closer, some property owners even started begging government officials to rezone their land for industrial use so they could sell and move away from it all. In Northern Virginia, the signs of that opposition are increasingly common. I spotted large banners hanging from gazebos along a central stretch of road in Loudoun County, warning that towering high-voltage transmission lines would slash the values of people’s homes. “Who pays? Loudoun families,” the banners read. “Who profits? Data centers and Dominion”—the local electricity provider and utility.

Residents also have to deal with tremors through their homes during the construction period, followed by the incessant hum of air-conditioning units once the center is up and running. Some people have to contend with the bright lights that stay on all night to illuminate the exterior of the facilities. It’s easy to dismiss some of these concerns as typical NIMBY complaints, but most Virginians were not initially opposed to data centers. For a long time, there wasn’t much of a concerted campaign to stop data centers at all. That only began to change as they started encroaching on communities and people experienced the tangible impacts in their lives.

In early 2026, a cold snap hit many parts of the United States. Once the grid was stressed, data centers in Virginia were given permission to run fully on their diesel generators, with little regard for the air-quality impacts of burning all that fuel. They did not have to “reduce their consumption, take some of the AI offline,” said Chris Miller, the president of the Piedmont Environmental Council. Instead, the decision was made “to expose the general public to an increased level of public-health hazard” from the burning of all that diesel fuel. It’s no wonder that as data-center construction ramped up in 2024 and ’25, opposition began growing around it.

Between 2024, when I’d first spoken to Miller, and early 2026, when we reconnected, there had been what he described as a night-and-day shift. People were feeling the effects of data centers near their communities, and they were also getting confirmation that what they were feeling was real. Over that time, a lot more data became available on the facilities’ water use and electricity demands, and media reports shed further light on how they were contributing to higher electricity prices in places like Virginia. People were finally waking up to what was going on around them.

When you’re stuck on the outside of a data center, it’s easy to imagine the interior as sinister: ominous, towering black boxes filled with servers whose small lights and indicators give off an eerie glow in the dimmed light of a chilly server room. But the truth is that they’re typically quite mundane and utilitarian spaces. In bigger facilities, the floors can be concrete, but many others have elevated floors covered with removable tiles, while fluorescent lights keep things well lit—at least when humans are inside. Miles of cables running overhead and down into the racks enable rapid data transfer and the servers’ ultimate connection to the wider Internet.

There’s no question that the sensitive data held inside must be protected, and the extent of the effort to do so must be visible to clients. But it’s not a form of theater that ends at the door. Stringent security practices are present throughout the facility, beginning with the people who are allowed through the door. I had a hard time getting inside a major data center: The most recognizable companies rejected my requests, if they responded to them at all. Several of the experts I spoke with told me that’s typical. Since the generative-AI boom has increased the scrutiny placed on data centers, getting inside their walls has become much more difficult.

When I finally visited a data center for a tour, the extent of the security measures struck me more than anything else. Security officials already had my name when I arrived at the gate. Once I got through the door, I needed to check in and hand over my passport before I could go any further. No photos were allowed. As we made our way through the facility, it became clear that you couldn’t simply walk through any of the doors. Each one of them had its own digital lock, meaning the executive escorting me had to tap us into room after room as he explained how the different parts of the facility worked.

When you enter the server room of a data center, the warm air produced by all of the computer hardware envelops you, but far more striking is the incessant metallic hum that feels like an auditory assault—the product of all the servers and whirring fans in the room. Rows of tall lockers (called “racks”) fill the facility, each containing dozens of long, thin servers. Depending on the size of the data center, there can be hundreds, thousands, or hundreds of thousands of servers throughout the building. The server racks stand seven feet high, with bundles of colored cables connecting to the hardware and running along the back of each rack. When the servers are cooled with air-conditioning, you can feel the difference in the heat they produce simply by standing on either side of a rack.

Standing inside a data center, I couldn’t help but think of the vast network of supply chains that make them possible. The Internet has extended to touch every corner of the world, but so too do the trade routes whose finished goods eventually sit within the facility’s racks. The many thousands of servers overwhelmingly arrive from factories in Asia, with supply chains that snake out to mines across the world where the minerals and metals essential for their production originate.

The Internet can seem like an immaterial thing when we log on to social-media platforms or do Google searches that send us to any number of websites. But structuring data centers as real-estate-investment vehicles required defining and treating the network in a wholly different way. It’s an important element of the business of the Internet that often gets ignored, but it has significant implications for the Web itself. Those corporate structures shape the incentives that drive the companies ultimately building out the foundations of the digital world.

THE BERING SEA — The net rose from the heaving Bering Sea swells, packed so tightly with Alaska pollock that their heads squeezed through the webbing.

As the catch spilled onto the deck, other dead marine life came with it: a skate, crabs, jellyfish and, among the most consequential, salmon — the fish at the center of a growing political and cultural fight over industrial trawling in Alaska.

“This is a wild capture fishery so you do run into your nontarget species, and a lot of people have a problem with that,” said Maverick Blake, the boat’s captain. “But this is what it takes to put seafood on the table.”

Blake redirected the boat toward another fishing ground where he hoped to encounter fewer salmon.

Alaska pollock is the world’s second-largest fishery. It is the fish inside U.S. McDonald’s Filet-O-Fish sandwiches and is also turned into fish sticks and imitation crab. The fishery uses methods that it — and McDonald’s — say are aimed at minimizing environmental impacts, but the nets that catch pollock catch other marine life too.

Bycatch — marine life caught unintentionally while fishers target something else — makes up 2% of the pollock fishery’s total catch. At the scale of a fishery that harvests billions of pounds of pollock annually, that can mean hundreds of thousands of salmon. Over the last 10 years, Bering Sea pollock trawlers caught an average of about 18,000 Chinook and 288,000 other salmon a year, nearly all of them chum.

Those catches have become especially contentious as salmon runs have collapsed across western Alaska, where dozens of Native communities have relied on salmon for generations. The backlash has spread to smaller commercial and charter fishers who say they, too, are bearing the costs of declining stocks. And trawling and bycatch have become prominent issues in Alaska’s midterm elections, with candidates for governor and in the state’s closely watched U.S. Senate contest sparring over how aggressively to restrict the industry.

In 2024, Alaska and Canada instated a seven-year ban on Chinook fishing along the Yukon River — one full salmon life cycle — to give depleted populations a chance to rebuild. The Kuskokwim River has faced repeated Chinook and chum closures too, while other Yukon salmon fisheries have also been restricted.

Scientists point to warming waters and changing ocean conditions, not trawl bycatch, as the primary causes of the declines. Warmer oceans can also increase the risk of salmon bycatch by changing where salmon and pollock overlap, according to a 2025 study.

But critics argue bycatch is one of the few sources of fish mortality managers can directly control, and even small losses matter when runs are depleted.

“Each fish is critical to communities along the Yukon River,” said Andrew Seitz, professor of fisheries at the University of Alaska Fairbanks. “The people who feel that they bear the brunt of the conservation measures are people along the river.”

During the four days the Associated Press was on the F/V Progress, the vessel caught some 210 chum salmon. Blake, who has spent 13 years in the pollock industry, said avoiding salmon is a central part of his job. He said the search for waters with fewer salmon burned nearly 3,000 gallons of fuel. Had he stayed put, he said, the boat could have filled its holds in half a day with far less.

“The subsistence users see this huge machine industry out here harvesting in the ocean and I can understand their frustration,” he said. “I would be upset if somebody told me that I wasn’t allowed to fish like my ancestors had done for thousands of years before.”

Still, Blake and industry representatives call it one of the best managed fisheries in the world. Captains stay in contact with other vessels throughout each trip to share information about salmon concentrations, while the fleet follows weekly closure areas based on reports from the fishing grounds. Pollock vessels carry salmon excluders, devices that help salmon find a way out of the net.

Every salmon caught by the Bering Sea pollock fleet is counted. Trawlers are monitored by observers or cameras, as are deliveries at shoreside processing plants.

“The amount of science and management effort that goes into improving it is basically unprecedented anywhere,” said Matt Tinning, chief executive of the At-Sea Processors Assn., which represents five Alaska pollock companies. “The system is designed to really incentivize salmon avoidance every day, all the time.”

At 9 a.m. on a July morning in Rampart, an Alaska Native village, about a dozen boats sat moored along the Yukon River. In years past, families would have been pulling nets and carrying salmon to their smokehouses.

The Yukon flows some 1,250 miles across Alaska before emptying into the Bering Sea. Salmon born in its tributaries migrate to the ocean, spend years feeding at sea, then return to spawn, linking villages like Rampart to the same waters where the pollock fleet fishes.

“We have this big void in our lives right now,” said Brooke Woods, a Koyukon Alaska Native, mother of six and commissioner for the Yukon River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission. “We’ve never had a generation that hasn’t had the opportunity to learn and be taught by elders and parents, and that has such a big impact on our youth.”

Rampart has no grocery store, and reaching Fairbanks takes about five hours by boat and road. Families stock up when they travel and have long relied on fishing and hunting to fill their freezers.

Managers briefly opened chum fishing around Rampart this summer, but only with selective gear such as dip nets and beach seines. The Woods family didn’t have that equipment.

“You think back in history how they tried to destroy us,” Janet Woods said. “Taking our language, our ceremonies, and now our culture, and it’s been forever a fight for us just to hang on to what very little we have.”

Fish from across the North Pacific mix in the Bering Sea, so a salmon caught in a trawl net could have come from western Alaska, the Pacific Northwest or as far away as Russia or Japan.

NOAA has long used genetic testing to trace salmon bycatch. Since 2024, the Bristol Bay Science and Research Institute has also produced weekly chum bycatch estimates from summer to fall to give the fleet faster information about where western Alaska salmon are showing up.

The results support one of the industry’s main arguments: Most chum caught by pollock trawlers are not from western Alaska. In recent years, western Alaska fish made up an average of about 17% of chum and half of Chinook bycatch.

But even a small share can mean tens of thousands of salmon. In 2021, for example, 9% of chum bycatch came from western Alaska, which equated to more than 51,000 salmon, according to NOAA.

In February, after hearing testimony from more than 170 people, the North Pacific Fishery Management Council, which sets many of the rules for the Bering Sea fleet, recommended a cap of 45,000 western Alaska chum bycatch. Critics said the limit was too high.

Pollock is big business in Alaska. More than 3 billion pounds were landed from the Bering Sea and Gulf of Alaska in 2024, worth about $432 million, according to NOAA. Although the fishery supports thousands of Alaska jobs, critics note most of the economic benefit flows outside the state, with many of the largest vessels owned by Seattle-area companies.

“We’re trying to play this balancing game” between helping conserve western Alaska salmon for subsistence communities and maintaining “a very valuable economic fishery,” said Abby Duffy, who manages the Bristol Bay institute’s genetics lab in Dutch Harbor.

Small commercial halibut and crab fishers as well as charter operators have echoed the grievances heard along the Yukon. They too have faced shrinking catch limits and closures as stocks decline, while groundfish trawlers continue to catch both species as bycatch.

In 2025 alone, Alaska trawl fisheries discarded some 96 million pounds of bycatch, according to the North Pacific Fishery Management Council. That’s roughly equivalent to 100 fully loaded Boeing 747-8s.

“It’s extremely frustrating,” said Linda Behnken, a commercial fisher and executive director of the Alaska Longline Fishermen’s Association.

But the larger concern, she said, is what trawling does to the seafloor.

“The habitat damage is really insidious and long lasting,” she said.

Pollock vessels use pelagic, or midwater, trawls that are designed to fish in the water column rather than along the seafloor the way bottom trawls do. But pollock often swim close to the seafloor, so the nets make substantial bottom contact. That contact was evident during the AP’s trip aboard the F/V Progress, when Tanner crabs and basket stars — animals that live on the seafloor — came up in the net.

Meanwhile, some of the same areas that are closed to bottom trawling for the protection of coral, sponges, crab and other marine life are open to pelagic trawlers.

“So they’re dragging in the same areas, defeating the whole purpose of these habitat conservation areas,” said Ben Enticknap, fisheries campaign director at Oceana.

Anti-trawl sentiment has attracted Democrats, Republicans, commercial and sport fishers, and Alaska Native communities — constituencies that don’t often line up on the same side of resource debates.

At least half the original candidates in Alaska’s crowded governor’s race took positions critical of trawling or bycatch as contenders across the political spectrum called for tighter restrictions. Several gubernatorial candidates campaigned specifically on using those appointments to shift the council away from trawl interests.

In the U.S. Senate race, Democrat Mary Peltola has called for an end to large factory trawlers, while Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan has introduced legislation aimed at reducing bycatch and keeping trawl gear off the seafloor.

Blake hopes his son will one day fish beside him on the trawler. Brooke Woods hopes her son will one day set a salmon net with her.

“It’s frightening for me to not know if I’ll be able to pass on the same knowledge that my mother, grandmother and parents had,” Woods said. “I’ve heard elders say that if we lose our salmon, that assimilation has worked.”

Serhii Kuznietsov is accused of attacking civilian infrastructure in the 2022 sabotage of the Russian gas pipeline to Germany. His lawyers called Nord Stream a military target, and said their client is sworn to secrecy.

The trial of a former Ukrainian special forces officer, Serhii Kuznietsov, opened in the northern German city of Hamburg on Wednesday amid considerable public and press interest.

Kuznietsov is accused of leading the September 2022 sabotage operation that put the undersea Nord Stream 1 and 2 gas pipelines linking Russian to Germany out of action. The pipelines remain inactive four years later, with no sign of plans to ever reactivate them as Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine heads into its fifth winter.

Prosecutors accuse the Ukrainian of participation in a war crime by targeting civilian infrastructure, but his defense lawyers on Wednesday argued that the case should be dismissed entirely on multiple grounds.

A team of three lawyers representing Kuznietsov questioned the premise of the trial in court.

One of Kuznietsov's lawyers, Nicola Canestrini, argued that it was not valid to try the officer on war crimes charges for an attack on civilian infrastructure.

He argued that the pipeline, which provided half of Germany's natural gas imports prior to Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, was ultimately a "Russian weapon," given the importance of energy revenues for funding Moscow's military.

"Is it unlawful to destroy an aggressor's infrastructure without harming any human beings?" Canestrini asked the court.

After the charges were read out, the judge asked Kuznietsov if he wanted to comment, and he said that he did not.

His lawyers argue that this silence extends beyond not speaking in court and say this should be the main grounds for dismissal.

They say Kuznietsov is sworn to secrecy by his home government in Ukraine and so is unable to discuss his case or potential exonerating evidence with anyone, including his legal representatives.

"He is not permitted to speak. His two immediate superiors told him that he does not have authorization to make any statements — not even to his lawyers," defense attorney Jes Meyer-Lohkamp told the court. In German law, Meyer-Lohkamp argued: "Anyone who cannot speak out cannot be convicted."

DW's Matthew Moore was on hand in the court in northern Germany on Wednesday.

There is huge interest in this court case, Moore reported. No sooner had the court doors opened at 07:30 local time in Hamburg than the first journalists, observers and protesters began arriving.

A small pro-Ukraine protest held up a banner saying, "Russia is a terrorist." One participant from Germany told Moore they wanted to remind the world of "the war crimes taking place against the Ukrainian people."

Inside, a Ukrainian student told DW that he and others had made the journey from Kyiv to show support for the defendant, Serhii Kuznietsov.

"Serhii Kuznietsov is a hero," the student said. In the eyes of the German prosecutors, it is another matter. They accuse him of carrying out a war crime. If found guilty of his part in the gas pipeline bombing, Kuznietsov faces a lengthy prison sentence.

The case has been highly politically awkward for Germany — a staunch supporter of Ukraine despite investigators pursuing a trail that led to Kuznietsov and other suspects.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has repeatedly denied any Ukrainian involvement in the operation, and had also resisted Kuznietsov's extradition to Germany after his arrest in Italy last year, arguing that there was no case to answer.

German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul declined to comment on the trial on Wednesday, saying that the most important thing for now was for the case to proceed with no political interference or restrictions.

"We will deal with any outcome, and we will, of course, take it into account in our political calculations," he told a press conference in Berlin.

#1137. Israel’s Extremist Election

The run-up to the vote on Tuesday is exposing the façade of Israeli democracy—and the fealty of the country’s so-called opposition when it comes to matters of Jewish supremacy.

The façade of Israeli democracy has been exposed countless times over the years, but perhaps never quite so brazenly as inside the Supreme Court in Jerusalem three weeks ago. There, an esteemed panel of justices dispensed with decades of precedent by refusing to overturn the Central Elections Committee’s decision to disqualify a Palestinian candidate—this time Balad leader Sami Abu Shehadeh—from standing in next Tuesday’s election.

The sole basis for his disqualification was an article published on October 8, 2023, in which Abu Shehadeh described the previous day’s Hamas-led attack on Israel as a “significant historical event.” The court deemed this to have constituted support for armed struggle against Israel, forcing him to drop out of the race.

Such a move would have been damning enough in isolation. But it cannot be understood without considering the fact that Israeli law also permits the disqualification of candidates for incitement to racism—yet Jewish candidates who have endorsed dropping a nuclear bomb on Gaza, called for the “total annihilation” of its cities, stated that “there should be one sentence for everyone there: death,” and demanded that “all of Lebanon must burn” remain free to run. Go figure.

This election is not, however, a story only about the racism of Israel’s extreme right. Every single Jewish party in the Knesset supported Abu Shehadeh’s disqualification, exposing the fealty of Israel’s so-called opposition when it comes to matters of Jewish supremacy. Only the Palestinian parties of the Joint List (comprising Hadash, Ta’al, and Abu Shehadeh’s Balad) offer any real resistance to the policies of apartheid, ethnic cleansing, and genocide that have accelerated dramatically under Israel’s outgoing government.

Paradoxically, it is the Joint List and the Islamist party Ra’am that hold the key to the opposition’s chances of dethroning Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. The polls could not be clearer: Without these Arab parties, the anti-Netanyahu bloc will not reach the 61 mandates required for a Knesset majority. With their support, it could topple Netanyahu—whose own bloc of loyalists looks set to fall significantly short of a majority—virtually overnight.

But on the eve of the election, the anti-Netanyahu bloc appears incapable of doing what is required to send Israel’s longest-serving prime minister to his political grave (and, perhaps, his prison cell). Racism, it turns out, is not only a moral deficiency; it is also terrible politics. And rather than win with the support of Arab parties, Israel’s opposition is preparing to lose without them.

The previous election, in late 2022, ended a political stalemate that had brought Israelis to the polls five times in less than four years—a prolonged referendum on Netanyahu that saw him briefly lose the premiership in the fourth cycle before regaining it in the fifth. Tuesday’s vote takes place on much the same terrain, pitting a pro-Netanyahu coalition of far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties against a looser alliance of centrist and right-wing parties seeking to depose him.

In addition to Netanyahu’s political future, the main issues on the ballot include the cost of living, the conscription of ultra-Orthodox Jews into the military, a state inquiry into the security failings surrounding October 7, and Israel’s deteriorating standing abroad. The policies causing that deterioration remain conspicuously absent from the conversation, save for the near-universal denunciation of the new documentary NAZA.

The pro-Netanyahu bloc comprises the parties of the current government—Likud, Otzma Yehudit, the Religious Zionist Party, Shas, and United Torah Judaism—plus two additional figures from the extreme right hoping to join them. The first is Ofer Winter, a retired senior military commander leading the new party Amcha Yisrael, whose entire election platform revolves around expelling Palestinians from Gaza. (Winter is polling on the cusp of the electoral threshold of 3.25 percent; if he falls below it, all of his votes will go to waste, giving a boost to the opposition.)

The second is Moshe Feiglin, one of the most bizarre men in Israeli politics, whose libertarian party Zehut is running on a joint ticket with Bezalel Smotrich’s Religious Zionist Party. Feiglin’s greatest hits include “Every baby in Gaza is an enemy,” “Kill everything that moves,” and, essentially, Hitler was right but about the wrong people. (Likud and the Religious Zionist Party–Zehut have signed a surplus-vote agreement, meaning votes for Netanyahu may end up strengthening Feiglin and Smotrich, and vice versa.)

The opposing bloc of anti-Netanyahu parties is led this time by former Israeli army chief Gadi Eisenkot, who heads the newcomer party Yashar. He is credited with developing the Dahiyeh Doctrine, a military strategy honed in Lebanon and premised on the wholesale destruction of civilian infrastructure in the hope of turning local residents against Hezbollah—foreshadowing the doctrine of annihilation unleashed in Gaza over the past three years.

Eisenkot was party to that too: He served in Netanyahu’s war cabinet during the deadliest months of Israel’s onslaught, joining almost immediately after October 7 before resigning in June 2024 over the prime minister’s obstruction of a hostage deal.

So what is he offering? Eisenkot has repeatedly vowed that no Palestinian state will arise under his premiership, even attacking Netanyahu for having previously supported a two-state solution. He is in favor of building new Jewish settlements in Area C, the 60 percent of the West Bank under complete Israeli control, and does not rule out construction in the particularly controversial E1 zone.

In Gaza, Lebanon, and Syria, he wants to keep at least some of the land that Israel has militarily occupied under the outgoing government. And he recently stated that National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir “probably did good things in prisons”—where over 100 Palestinians have died since October 7 as a result of systematic torture, medical neglect, and food deprivation.

The other leaders of Eisenkot’s would-be coalition include former settler council leader Naftali Bennett (head of Together), who wants to annex Area C of the West Bank; Avigdor Liberman (Yisrael Beitenu), Netanyahu’s former defense minister, who declared in November 2023, “There are no innocents in Gaza”; and Yair Golan (the Democrats), a former deputy military chief, who advocated starving Gaza’s entire population until Hamas released the hostages. (The Reservists–New Economic Party, a new center-right alliance focused on enforcing the draft in Haredi communities and fixing the cost of living, would also likely join the anti-Netanyahu bloc if it manages to cross the electoral threshold.)

What sets these figures apart from the current government, however, is primarily their desire to dial down the volume of global outrage against Israel.

To that end, Eisenkot has promised to crack down on “Jewish terrorism” in the West Bank (albeit because it is “harming the settlement enterprise”), and would likely seek to advance Trump’s 20-point plan for Gaza where Netanyahu has stalled it. Proposals to expel 2 million Palestinians from the Strip would no longer be on the table. Eisenkot would also halt the government’s assault on Israel’s judiciary, and may pour greater resources into tackling the mafia groups that have been allowed to run rampant within Palestinian communities inside Israel.

There is just one problem: They do not have the votes.

The most favorable polls are giving the anti-Netanyahu bloc a seat count in the low-to-mid-50s (out of the Knesset’s 120), leaving it around 10 seats short of a majority. Luckily for them, there is an easy way to make up the difference. Unluckily for them, it involves sitting with—or at least relying on the votes of —Arab parties, which the bloc’s leaders have almost unanimously ruled out.

There will be two Arab parties in the next Knesset representing Israel’s Palestinian citizens: the Joint List (polling at around seven to nine seats) and Ra’am (around five or six seats). While the Joint List is in every sense a Palestinian political force, Ra’am leader Mansour Abbas has sought to downplay his party’s Palestinian identity, focusing on the fight against organized crime in Arab communities while speaking less about the West Bank and Gaza. He has brought in a Jewish lawmaker as his number two and distanced the party from its roots in the Islamic Movement, hoping to broaden his appeal among the anti-Netanyahu bloc. It has not worked.

In 2021, Ra’am became the first independent Arab party to join an Israeli government, led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid. But the move was fraught with tension, and the government imploded after just one year. Upon the government’s collapse, Netanyahu—who had courted Ra’am himself during the previous election campaign—proclaimed that “the experiment failed.” Four years on, with much of Israeli society gripped by a racist fervor that has reached new levels in the wake of October 7, the chances of a repeat appear extremely slim.

Ahead of Tuesday’s vote, Golan, the anti-Netanyahu bloc’s most left-wing leader, has said he would sit with Ra’am but not the Joint List (which would not join a governing coalition anyway). Liberman voted to disqualify both parties from the race altogether, and has repeatedly ruled out sitting with either. Bennett is refusing to sit with them too, while Eisenkot told a closed meeting of Bedouin leaders that he wants to include Ra’am in his government, then denied it, and has subsequently maintained that neither Arab party will be part of his coalition.

There is, however, another option here: a minority government propped up by the votes of Arab parties from outside the coalition, known in electoral parlance as a “safety net.” Such an arrangement would be highly unstable, but it is what enabled Yitzhak Rabin to sign the Oslo Accords into law in the 1990s. The Joint List is willing to play this role if it means toppling Netanyahu, and Eisenkot has hinted that he views this option favorably. But Liberman and Bennett have both dismissed it out of hand.

Barring any major shock in Tuesday’s results, then, neither bloc appears capable of forming a viable government in its current configuration—unless several of these leaders break their promises, which would certainly not be a first for Israeli politics. If they want to avoid triggering another election, both sides will need to get creative.

The most straightforward option would be to try to poach defectors from the opposing bloc. Netanyahu is reportedly eyeing up Bennett’s faction in the event of a deadlock (though Bennett’s campaign centers on enforcing the draft in ultra-Orthodox communities, thus making a coalition with Haredi parties seem implausible), while Eisenkot hopes to lure deserters from Netanyahu’s Likud. But acquiring more than a handful of defectors would be a mammoth task.

For his part, President Isaac Herzog, who has the unenviable job of overseeing the coalition-building process, is trying to engineer another possibility: a “national unity” government bringing together Netanyahu, Eisenkot, and whichever other parties would join them. A majority of the public favors this over going back to the polls a few months later.

Should Netanyahu conclude that a unity government is his only route to remaining prime minister, even under a rotation agreement with Eisenkot, it would surely appeal to him—especially if he gets the first turn as prime minister and can then collapse the government before having to relinquish power to Eisenkot. Yet, while some members of Eisenkot’s party appear open to this scenario, Eisenkot himself has, at least for now, ruled it out unequivocally. Meanwhile, Liberman, who might seem a likely candidate to join a unity government, has declared that he won’t sit with Netanyahu “come hell or high water.”

In short, we’re in for a messy few weeks, or perhaps months, as these various scenarios are gamed out and written off. What remains, if nobody succeeds in assembling a new government, is another election early next year, with Netanyahu remaining as caretaker prime minister in the interim.

Crucially, however, there is a lot that can be done with a Knesset majority even without forming a government. If the anti-Netanyahu bloc, the Joint List, and Ra’am together control more than 60 seats after Tuesday, they could vote to replace the Knesset speaker and immediately begin passing legislation independently of the government.

They could repeal the outgoing government’s judicial overhaul laws; establish a state inquiry commission to investigate the security failings of October 7, which this government has strongly resisted; and even pass a law preventing someone under indictment from serving as prime minister, effectively ending Netanyahu’s political career.

These plans are already being drawn up, although there are doubts as to whether the bloc can maintain its unity in the face of the inevitable onslaught of pressure from Netanyahu and his acolytes. And without forming a new government, the countdown to the next election will have already begun.
