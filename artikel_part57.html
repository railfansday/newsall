#2804. Dandelion Magic Trick- Make Stems Curl in Seconds

Love or loathe them, dandelions are a common feature in spring. One day, there are just a few, then suddenly they seem to be everywhere. A combination of day length and increasing temperatures leads to a massive synchronised bloom in April or May. It might be tempting to get rid of the pesky weeds, but leave them if you can, as they are an important early source of nectar for bees and butterflies.

Dandelions are also great for science experiments! This simple dandelion curling STEM challenge is a fantastic demonstration of osmosis and a fun science trick.

Carefully use the scissors to cut the dandelion stem in half.

Place the dandelion in a glass of water and watch as the stem strips curl tightly into ringlets.

Try it again, but this time cut the stem into 4 separate strips.

Try using warmer and colder water to see if the speed of the curling changes.

The inside of a dandelion stem contains thin-walled parenchyma cells, which absorb water quickly through a process called osmosis. They expand and become turgid as they absorb water.

The outside of the stem is waxy and rigid, it can’t easily expand and doesn’t absorb water.

As the inner cells stretch and swell, the outside of the stem is forced to curve, resulting in springy curls.

Caution – take care around the white milky sap inside the stem. It can be sticky and may cause a slight irritation.

#2805. Short on Land, Japan Is Building Solar Farms on Old Golf Courses

The Kanoya Osaki Solar Hills solar plant in Kagoshima Prefecture, Japan, was built on a former golf course. Kyocera

Mountainous, densely populated Japan has little land available for large solar farms, so developers are erecting arrays on many of the now disused golf courses that proliferated during the country’s 1980s economic boom.

A Bloomberg analysis found that 9 percent of Japan’s utility-scale solar capacity is installed on former golf courses. The courses, which were once a symbol of Japan’s economic ascendance, have declined in popularity, leading many to close down.

After the 2011 Fukushima nuclear disaster, public outcry spurred Japan to close down dozens of nuclear reactors. To compensate for the loss of these power plants, which once supplied a third of Japan’s electricity, the government began offering generous incentives to solar developers, leading to a wave of new large installations.

Some developers have sought to build solar projects on abandoned farms, including in Fukushima, which has seen massive arrays installed on farmland that was rendered unusable by radioactive contamination from the damaged nuclear plant. But in many cases, large solar farms have been erected in the foothills outside Japanese cities. Such projects have displaced forests and upped the risk of landslides, fueling public backlash.

Japan’s old golf courses provide a better option, not only because they skirt opposition, but because the land is generally cheap and flat, and these sites are already connected to the power grid. At least 10 percent of utility-scale capacity that has been announced or is currently under construction is on golf courses, Bloomberg found.

#2806. A revolutionary new microscope could reveal the hidden machinery of life

Almost a century ago, a breakthrough in microscopy gave scientists an entirely new way to examine the inside of cells. Known as phase contrast, the technique made previously faint or nearly invisible cellular structures much easier to distinguish. Its importance was recognized with a Nobel Prize in 1953.

Now, physicists at the University of California, Berkeley, have brought that same principle to electron microscopy, a technology capable of magnifying objects roughly 10,000 times more than conventional light microscopes.

The researchers have developed a device called a laser phase plate that could significantly improve cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM). This powerful imaging method has transformed the study of protein structures and helped speed up drug discovery over the past decade. Even so, it has a major limitation: Most human proteins are so small that obtaining clear images of them remains extremely difficult.

The new laser technology could help overcome that obstacle, potentially allowing scientists to image proteins as small as one-third the size of those that challenge current microscopes.

An even greater impact could come from improvements to cryoelectron tomography (cryo-ET). This newer approach combines images captured from multiple angles to construct three-dimensional views of proteins and other molecules. Unlike techniques that study isolated molecules in a solution, cryo-ET can reveal how proteins are arranged and interact within their natural cellular surroundings.

“Cryo-EM has become the new, fastest-growing method for resolving the structure of biological macromolecules, and cryo-ET is expected to show how these molecules work together in their natural, cellular context,” said Holger Müller, a UC Berkeley professor of physics and faculty scientist at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory who led the development effort. “But because of signal-to-noise limitations, the majority of human and animal proteins are too small to be analyzed by these methods. The increase in signal-to-noise ratio provided by this laser phase plate is expected to overcome these important limitations.”

The breakthrough relies on what the researchers describe as the world's most intense focused continuous-wave laser. Rather than simply illuminating a specimen, the laser interacts directly with the microscope's electron beam, altering its phase to increase image contrast.

This adjustment makes it easier to distinguish small molecules, including hemoglobin, from their surroundings. It could also improve the visibility of structures inside cells, such as the nucleus and mitochondria.

“With cryo-ET, we're looking at small, very complicated cellular material that’s incredibly crowded inside the cell,” said Bridget Carragher, founding technical director of imaging at Biohub in Redwood City, California. “It’s like a forest of trees, and you’re trying to find one leaf on one tree in there. Cryo-ET needs a dramatic step forward in contrast, so we can start to see what's going on inside the cell. That's what the laser phase plate promises to give us.”

With financial support from Biohub, Müller acquired an advanced cryo-EM instrument and equipped it with the new laser phase plate. He named the resulting microscope Theia, after the ancient Greek Titaness associated with light and radiance.

Meanwhile, Carragher is directing the construction of a related microscope at Biohub's imaging facility in Redwood City. Her team's instrument uses two lasers rather than one, building on theoretical research conducted by Müller and his collaborators.

The two laser beams are arranged at right angles to one another and each operates at approximately half the power. This configuration helps prevent components from burning out while also reducing optical distortions that could interfere with image quality.

Both research teams are working with Thermo Fisher Scientific, the leading manufacturer of cryo-EM instruments.

“Theia is the Formula 1 microscope,” Müller said. “It has extra electron optics that give it better resolution than the standard cryo-EM, even without the laser. With the addition of the laser phase plate, we hope that it really becomes the world's best instrument overall.”

The Berkeley team's latest images and technical findings are presented in the journal Science. Details of Biohub's two-laser design are available in a recently posted preprint.

To understand why the new technology matters, it helps to look at a longstanding challenge in biological imaging.

Because plant and animal cells consist largely of water, they are relatively transparent when viewed under an ordinary light microscope. Although this transparency might seem beneficial, it creates a problem. Small cellular structures, including mitochondria and nuclei, scatter very little light and therefore appear only slightly darker than the surrounding material.

Scientists have traditionally addressed this problem by adding stains to create greater contrast. However, staining also kills the cells.

In 1930, Dutch physicist Frits Zernike realized that light passing through biological material changes in more ways than simply becoming brighter or dimmer. When light scatters inside a cell, it also experiences a tiny delay that changes its phase, or the timing of its wave peaks.

Although the human eye cannot directly detect this phase shift, Zernike discovered that it could be converted into a visible difference in brightness. By shifting the phase of the unscattered light by 90 degrees, he made subtle cellular features stand out more clearly against their background when the light reached the viewer's eye.

This innovation became the foundation of phase-contrast microscopy, earning Zernike the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physics.

By the early 1940s, scientists had already recognized how useful phase contrast was for studying cells. They began exploring whether the same idea might improve electron microscopes, which use electrons instead of light to examine structures as small as individual proteins.

Unfortunately, early attempts encountered serious technical difficulties. Devices designed to alter the phase of electron beams often weakened the beam too much, introduced image instability, or reduced resolution.

A possible solution emerged in 2010, when Müller and Robert Glaeser, now a UC Berkeley professor emeritus of molecular and cell biology, proposed using an intense laser to produce the necessary phase shift. Their approach offered a way to improve contrast without sacrificing electron beam intensity.

Glaeser has played an important role in developing cryo-EM, which offers a potentially simpler way to determine molecular structures than traditional X-ray crystallography.

X-ray crystallography requires researchers to grow crystals of the molecules they want to study and obtain access to an intense X-ray source. Cryo-EM avoids the need to crystallize molecules, but it presents difficulties of its own.

One of the biggest problems is that an electron beam can heat and eventually damage the specimen being examined. This limits how much information can be collected and how detailed the resulting images can become. Covering specimens with metal can reduce damage and improve contrast, but it also makes the images less sharp.

During the 1960s, researchers began investigating whether freezing biological samples could protect them from radiation damage.

Glaeser demonstrated that cooling specimens helped preserve them during imaging. He also proposed using a less intense electron beam to examine thousands of frozen molecules at once, further limiting the damage.

Because the molecules would be positioned at different angles, computers could analyze and combine their images to reconstruct a highly detailed molecular structure.

These advances helped establish the foundations of modern cryo-EM, whose developers received the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. During their acceptance remarks, the Nobel laureates acknowledged Glaeser's contributions.

The Nobel Committee praised the technology, explaining that cryo-EM “both simplifies and improves the imaging of biomolecules. This method has moved biochemistry into a new era.”

Turning the laser phase plate concept into working technology required years of engineering.

Following their 2010 proposal, Müller spent 15 years pursuing the idea, with part of the research supported by the National Institutes of Health.

The first major challenge was generating an extraordinarily intense laser beam concentrated into a tiny area. The researchers needed enough light intensity to change the phase of an electron beam by 90 degrees.

It took a decade to develop the necessary system.

Their solution was to confine a continuous laser beam inside a spherical chamber lined with mirrors. As the light repeatedly reflected through the cavity more than 10,000 times, its intensity increased dramatically while the beam became focused into an extremely small spot.

“It’s 75 kilowatts focused to a few microns,” Müller said. “That’s more powerful than what you use for welding. It's more power than a military laser. It builds up the brightest continuous laser focus ever.”

The team first demonstrated that the concept worked by installing a laser phase plate in one of Glaeser's older electron microscopes.

Additional funding from Biohub then made it possible to acquire a customized, state-of-the-art Thermo Fisher Krios cryo-EM microscope and modify it to accommodate the laser technology.

In their latest research, the scientists tested the upgraded microscope using six different samples that varied in size and preparation method. The laser consistently improved image resolution, with particularly important gains for specimens that are difficult to image using standard methods.

“For the most challenging cases — small particles, bad specimens — the laser produces a very considerable advantage,” Müller said.

To demonstrate the new microscope's capabilities, the researchers reconstructed images of two different proteins.

The first was aldolase, a protein found in muscle that modern cryo-EM systems can already image relatively easily. The second was hemoglobin, the protein responsible for carrying oxygen through the bloodstream.

Hemoglobin is much smaller and lies near the lower size limit of proteins that conventional cryo-EM instruments can reliably analyze.

Using the laser phase plate, the researchers improved the resolution of both protein structures. The improvement was especially pronounced for hemoglobin, highlighting the technology's potential to overcome the difficulties associated with smaller molecules.

“The bottom line is, if you have a large protein and a really good sample — a fresh one or one frozen without bubbles, for example — you may not need the phase plate to get a single, high-quality image. But for a small protein and a bad sample, laser-on is best,” Müller said. “This could fill an enormous gap in our knowledge of protein structures that can’t be crystallized or are too small for today’s cryo-EM. And it will be revolutionary for cryo-ET.”

Protein mass is typically expressed in daltons, a unit named after English chemist John Dalton. One dalton is defined as one-twelfth the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Conventional cryo-EM struggles to image proteins smaller than approximately 70 kilodaltons. That limitation is particularly significant because proteins below this threshold account for about 90% of the human proteome, the complete collection of proteins produced by the human body.

With the laser phase plate, researchers can now image proteins as small as 50 kilodaltons, although doing so remains challenging. Such proteins are even smaller than hemoglobin.

Müller believes the technology could eventually allow scientists to study proteins as small as 17 kilodaltons (the size of the protein myoglobin).

Reaching that goal may require another improvement: using a tightly focused electron beam rather than the defocused beam currently needed to produce sufficient contrast without the laser phase plate.

According to Müller, the laser system should make it possible to obtain contrast directly from phase changes in a focused electron beam.

If successful, this adjustment could double the contrast and signal-to-noise ratio again, adding to the improvement already achieved with the laser phase plate.

Such progress could make it possible to study a far larger proportion of the body's proteins and provide unprecedented insight into how they interact within the complex environment of cells.

“This technology is a step function change for biology,” said Stephani Otte, Biohub’s Vice President of Imaging Science. “We are going to be able to see how molecular machines operate inside the living cell, in context, for the first time. What was once invisible will become visible — and that changes everything about how we understand disease.”

The Berkeley research team also includes Glaeser; postdoctoral fellows and co-first authors Petar Petrov and Jessie Zhang; staff scientist Jonathan Remis; postdoctoral fellow Hang Cheng; and current and former physics graduate students Jeremy Axelrod, Eric Cooper, Ian Hicklin, Shahar Sandhaus, and Cooper Schnurr.

At Biohub, Carragher and David Agard, the organization's founding scientific director of imaging, jointly lead the Dynamic Structural Cell Biology group. Both are co-corresponding authors of the Biohub preprint, alongside Biohub engineer Pavel Olshin.

#2807. The helicopter-powered quest to save salmon in B.C.

Jessica Hutchinson, executive director of conservation group Redd Fish Restoration Society, remembers the long, restless night in December 2025 listening to the thrum of a rainstorm in Tofino, B.C. Lying in her bed, she hoped the culmination of years of planning, weeks of construction and eye-watering helicopter bills would hold.

As Hutchinson lay awake, rain poured down from the steep hillsides surrounding Tranquil Creek like it normally did, sending a surge of water into the river below. But now the deluge had an obstruction: a tangled mass of around 50 trees, tufted like a giant bird’s nest and lodged into the riverbed, causing the flow to ricochet and swoop under and above. Pools and eddies had begun to form below the wooden mass of the river’s new intruder, also known as an engineered logjam, reflecting a years-long effort that is relatively uncommon in B.C.

Engineered logjams are large-scale interventions for places with large-scale problems. In Tranquil’s case, the watershed needed life support.

Redd Fish watershed restoration director Tom Balfour describes the approach as “open-heart surgery.”

“You don’t do this to rivers that need a little help,” he says. “You do it when things are really desperate.”

Logged extensively between the 1960s and the 1990s, the valley soon became a funnel for sediment flowing down from the steep hills. The river weaving through it had become a highway of fast-moving water, and sand and gravel, threatening to bury or drag salmon eggs out to sea. By 2017, Chinook were on the verge of local extinction, while chum, coho and steelhead were in severe decline.

The Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation, whose territory encompasses Hiłsyaqƛis (the Nuu-chuh-nulth name for Tranquil Creek), identified the region as an urgent priority, and Redd Fish subsequently launched their multi-year quest to figure out how to help it heal.

“Tranquil’s got a special place in my heart,” Tatt Charlie, lead guardian for Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation, says. Tla-o-qui-aht guardians are working with Redd Fish to restore the watershed. “It’s one of those amazing ecosystems where we could get almost all of our salmon spawning in that again and rejuvenate it for our people.”

The Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation worked in partnership with Redd Fish to develop a long-term plan for Tranquil. It required learning about what went wrong, and then gradually bringing the missing pieces back. It wasn’t cheap or easy, but ten years into their journey, Hutchinson says it’s been worth it.

“The dock is very slippery,” calls out Frank Witter as he steers the silver skiff up to the small wooden landing just outside the mouth of the Tranquil Creek inlet. Witter, Charlie and fellow Tla-o-qui-aht guardian Nathaniel Currie get ready to disembark.

The dock is remnant infrastructure from the watershed’s heavy logging days. So is the dusty sorting yard to our left, replete with metal loading skids angling into the ocean, as if the valley’s forests were still full of big trees to take away.

Hutchinson has come back from working on the riverbed to meet us, and we pile into one of Redd Fish’s company trucks. From the window, other signs of change soon follow: we pass a pile of pipes and metal planks near a recently decommissioned forest service road.

Witter, Redd Fish’s project manager, stops the truck as we reach a high point on the road, and we climb out to take a look. From here, the watershed’s history opens like a storybook.

What appear to be white, fuzzy scars carved into the mountainside are actually landslides, Hutchinson tells me. These are the places where disturbance-loving alder trees have lodged themselves onto the scree slopes, their foliage still bare from the winter.

After they lost the big conifer trees whose roots were anchors, the fine-grained sediment, gravel and jagged rocks that comprise the region’s hillsides started to fall. Logging roads made things worse, cutting holes in the hillside like a dispenser below a gumball machine. When Clayoquot Sound’s famous rainstorms bear down on the valley, they tend to pull the already-loose sand and rocks down with them.

The valley bottom, too, had lost its defences. Once fortified by big, steadying trees like cedars and spruce, the flat-lying ground is now peppered with daintily rooted species like alder. Over time, the river got wider and flatter.

For salmon, this layered onslaught of sediment spells trouble.

All five species of North American Pacific salmon have historically spawned in the Tranquil watershed, and for salmon, spawning is a miraculous event. It requires adult salmon to survive in the open ocean, replete with fishing nets, predators and pathogens, sometimes for up to around decade. If the salmon is fortunate enough to reach its elder years, it tracks the earth’s magnetic field and then draws on its keen sense of smell to return, sometimes over thousands of kilometres, to the same stream channel where it was born.

Under the right conditions, the salmon digs a nest beneath the gravel just deep enough for its eggs to stay covered during the river’s most turbulent months. If things go wrong, baby salmon can asphyxiate beneath a buildup of sand, or if the water moves too fast, they get washed out to sea.

It wasn’t always like this, Charlie says, as he gestures to the mouth of the estuary below us. It was once the bustling village of Hiłsyaqƛis, one of many village sites in the territory, where members of the Tla-o-qui-aht came to fish and camp each year.

Back then, surrounded by an intact forest, the now-rushing Tranquil Creek consisted of meandering paths of water braiding together. For millennia, the Tla-o-qui-aht people helped keep it that way, using various techniques to keep the populations in balance: cultivating strong-rooted crabapple trees that helped mitigate erosion in the estuary, for example, and building rock weirs to count and manage how much salmon swam up to spawn.

“You would allow the strongest of the river to go up, enough fish to make sure every square inch of gravel is spawned,” Saya Masso, Tla-o-qui-aht First Nation’s natural resources manager, says. Once enough salmon had travelled up the river to support the forest and its animals, Masso says the community would then begin their own harvest.

That stewardship was suppressed as Canada dispossessed the nation of its territory.

Over a century later, the Tla-o-qui-aht partnered with Redd Fish to conduct a years-long investigation to determine the damage caused in the interim, piecing together Tranquil’s history of disturbance through historic photos and terrain assessments. Their research determined logging strongly contributed to the watershed’s downhill slide. They also discovered that the watershed was unlikely to heal on its own in time for salmon to benefit.

“It’s not on a healing trajectory,” Hutchinson says. “It’s actually unravelling and becoming worse through the years.”

Witter’s team worked to stabilize the landslides, creating terraces and staking willow trees, known for their hearty roots, to prevent further movement. Throughout the lower watershed, Redd Fish planted 120,000 trees and restored 100 hectares of young, replanted forests by thinning out trees to create light to help big ones grow.

They also turned their attention to the river.

“Rivers take annual presence to become productive,” Masso says. “Our plan is to get to a system of abundance.”

Western science was slow to realize that wood and salmon need each other.

Since at least the last Ice Age, salmon’s decomposing bodies have fed and plumped the big trees of the Pacific Northwest. Those trees, in turn, feed the river, depositing big logs that create the smorgasbord of habitat types and pools salmon require to survive. Logs and logjams act like sorting hats for gravel, distributing patches of coarse and fine gravel that suit different species’ needs.

“It quickly became apparent to me in studying the rivers of this region that wood was as important as sediment and water,” geomorphologist David Montgomery says. He was one of the early contributors to a growing scientific recognition of the relationship between wood and salmon in the 1990s.

By then, much of the damage to these ancient Pacific rivers was done. Logging companies had clear-cut many watersheds, as they did in the Tranquil. Some fish biologists even encouraged wood removal from streams and rivers, thinking it would help fish travel upstream.

This antipathy to river debris had deep roots. Large-scale “log drives” in the late 1800s and early 1900s culled wood from the vast rivers, including in the Cowichan Valley, where people used explosives to clear off wood and other impediments.

In B.C. and throughout the Pacific Northwest, government-funded restoration crews gradually began bringing wood back in the 1990s. Early interventions were often targeted to smaller streams where a single log or two could make a meaningful impact. But big rivers like Tranquil Creek’s mainstem require something sturdier.

A short walk downriver, one of Redd Fish’s many engineered logjams comes into view, knotted and gnarled with trees piled atop one another, holding firm like a fortress as water passes dutifully around its edges.

“Frank, have you seen it?” Hutchinson says, getting Witter’s attention. River debris is accumulating in the logjam, a natural addition to the engineered effort. “That’s not even us, that’s just wood piled up.”

This is the promise of a well-built logjam: it takes up a life of its own, collecting wood the river throws at it. Ideally, it becomes self-sustaining, forming forested islands in the river that contribute their own source of wood.

To match the ferocity of the Tranquil’s rainy season flows, Redd Fish had to craft logjams with around 50 trees piled atop and between each other, lasagna-style, staked in place with tall logs piled deep into the riverbed.

“It basically is trying to mimic what an old growth tree would do if it rolled into the river with its giant root plate,” Hutchinson says.

“It would maintain the force of the river.”

These human-made jams are not cheap, requiring heavy equipment to temporarily isolate a spot along the riverbed with pumps and sandbags, and helicopters to haul wood and logs up to 15 metres tall. They also require teams of engineers working closely with traditional knowledge holders, geomorphologists and other scientists to ensure the investment and design will hold up.

“All those gears have to sing. But when they do, I’ve seen really good results.”

A shock of cold hits my legs as I venture forward into Tranquil Creek, in a channel that’s surprisingly deep and aquamarine blue.

Last year’s work is already visible, marked by the first crop of young willow trees clawing their roots into the gravel, one of many impediments Redd Fish and Tla-o-qui-aht Tribal Park Guardians have installed to slow the water down.

Redd Fish staff are here, hammering the new generation of stakes into the dry riverbed. For a moment, conversation lulls, and there’s just the sound of running water and syncopated tapping, then the conversation starts up again.

So far the outcomes of their efforts appear hopeful, though Hutchinson avoids drawing early conclusions. #2816. Honey bees are bullying other bees, scientists discover

Honey bees are proving to be formidable competitors for food in the UK, and scientists have discovered that they frequently use aggression to drive other pollinators away from flowers. New research has documented how honey bees chase, displace, and intimidate rival insects, providing the first detailed evidence of how extensively they interfere with other species' access to food.

The study, conducted by researchers at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) and published in Ecological Entomology, followed pollinator activity over two years at five locations in and around Cambridge, England. Each site was situated within 300 meters of an established honey bee colony.

Researchers observed insects visiting bramble (Rubus fruticosus) and ivy (Hedera helix), two flowering plants that provide important supplies of nectar and pollen. Both attract large numbers of insects during their flowering periods, making them useful places to investigate competition for food.

Honey Bees Dominate the Competition for Flowers

To examine how different pollinators interact, the team divided flower visitors into five groups: honey bees, bumble bees, solitary bees, hoverflies, and an 'other' category containing butterflies, beetles, and wasps.

Over 93 hours of observation, the scientists documented encounters in which one insect interrupted another's feeding or prevented it from reaching a flower. Each interaction was classified according to its outcome. A 'win' occurred when one insect gained access to a flower at the expense of another, which experienced a 'loss' of feeding opportunities. A 'share' meant both insects were able to access the flower without conflict.

The results revealed a clear pattern. Locations with greater concentrations of honey bees generally had fewer other pollinators and experienced more competition for flowers. Honey bees also emerged victorious in a larger proportion of their encounters with other insects.

On bramble flowers, doubling the density of honey bees was associated with a 14% decrease in bumblebee abundance. On ivy, higher honey bee numbers were linked to reduced numbers of solitary bees and hoverflies.

The honey bees did not simply reach flowers before their competitors. They frequently initiated aggressive encounters, driving other insects away to secure feeding opportunities.

Other pollinators actively avoided honey bees in 28% of observed interactions on bramble and 36% of those on ivy. In at least 20% of the encounters honey bees won on either plant, they physically pushed aside or chased away competing insects.

Aggressive behavior initiated by species other than honey bees was uncommon. However, one insect stood out as an exception.

The ivy bee, a solitary species not native to Britain that was first documented in the country in 2001, was the only pollinator observed actively challenging honey bees. Unlike most other insects, ivy bees sometimes defended their feeding spots by chasing or displacing honey bees, successfully competing for access to flowers.

Climate Change Could Make Competition Worse

The findings are part of a broader research effort to understand competition among pollinators and identify ways to reduce its ecological effects.

The results also come as beekeeping continues to expand across the UK. The number of registered beekeepers increased from 19,000 in 2010 to 47,000 last year, reflecting growing numbers of managed honey bee colonies.

Researchers warn that rising temperatures could add to the pressure on wild pollinators. Their observations showed that competitive encounters became more frequent during warmer conditions.

One possible explanation involves changes in flower nectar production. When plants experience 'heat stress', they can produce less nectar, potentially forcing more insects to compete over a smaller supply of food.

Temperature also affects different pollinators in different ways. Bumblebees and hoverflies are sensitive to high temperatures and may forage more effectively in cooler weather. Honey bees, by contrast, generally become more active as temperatures rise. Previous research found that increasing the temperature by 12°C caused a tenfold increase in the number of honey bees leaving their nests.

Together, these differences suggest that continued warming could give honey bees an even greater competitive advantage over some wild pollinators.

Protecting Wild Pollinators as Honey Bee Numbers Grow

Lead author Victoria Buckle, a PhD student at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) in Cambridge, England, emphasized that the research is intended to improve conservation efforts rather than discourage people from keeping honey bees.

"The aim of our study was not to discourage beekeeping, but to better understand how competition happens between wild and managed pollinator species with the aim of helping to conserve our wild pollinators, which play such an important ecological role.

"We found that most interactions when competing for food were instigated by honey bees and they had a greater number of 'wins', resulting in a loss of forage for other pollinators. Bumblebees, solitary bees and hoverflies would avoid, be displaced by and on some occasions be chased off by honey bees.

"An interesting exception was the ivy bee, which would win contests more often against honey bees. It is the first time, to our knowledge, such behaviors have been observed and shows ivy bees are able to 'hold their own' in such encounters.

"However, honey bees outcompeted all other wild pollinators and based on our findings, future predicted warming in the UK is only likely to further exacerbate interference competition from dominant honey bees. Therefore, it's important to find ways to ensure honey bees and wild pollinators can continue to coexist, particularly in locations with a high density of hives, such as urban areas."

The BC Conservatives are promising to cut income taxes for everyone, slash regulations in a bid to spur economic development and boost funding for police.

Both parties are promising billions in spending on health care and infrastructure without a lot of detail on where that money will come from. The province currently faces a deficit of nearly $14 billion.

Meanwhile, the BC Green Party’s 2026 campaign is also focusing heavily on kitchen-table issues. Lowan has proposed a wealth tax the party estimates would bring in billions of dollars that could be used to improve housing affordability and health care access.

What politicians are saying — or not saying — is likely tied to what voters have been telling pollsters lately.

Most of them are already government policy or continuations of the NDP’s previous commitments, including protecting 30 per cent of the province’s land base by 2030. “The thing about a polycrisis is that it implies that pressing problems go well beyond your top three,” he said.

“People know how much they’re paying for eggs, but nobody knows if their house is going to be the one that will burn down,” Harrison said. “There’s this uncertainty about who will bear those risks.”

“People are worried about their jobs and about prices, so we need to deal with these immediate concerns,” Ivanova said of the way top politicians have been framing the current moment. “Once we sort that out, only then can we look at climate change.”

“It almost seems like they’re looking for an economic initiative that would generate money as soon as possible, rather than taking a longer-term view on what kind of an economy are we building?”

“I think the way the NDP and the Conservatives [in B.C.] have really pressed for this accelerated extraction reflects the way in which the Liberal Party federally has gone flat out for maximizing and diversifying that kind of extractivist economy,” he said.

“Historically, climate and environment have been an issue that the NDP has had an advantage over the Conservatives on,” she said. “I think it’s reasonable for climate-concerned voters to say, ‘Tell us what you’re going to do on this issue that we care a lot about and that reflects a crisis that is only getting worse.’ ”

On the other hand, after years of not living up to some of its promises — climate-focused and otherwise — the NDP may have trouble convincing some voters the party will do as it promised if it forms government again. During the 2024 election campaign, the NDP promised to bring in a cap on oil and gas emissions, finish implementing recommendations from the 2020 old-growth forest strategic review and deliver a $1,000 grocery rebate — none of which happened in the past two years.

“Will some of the most climate-concerned voters vote again for the NDP because they fear that the Conservatives will be worse?” Harrison wondered. “Will they vote Green or will they stay home?”

Plenty of British Columbians may be fed up with the NDP but Lawson noted the Conservatives have gone through a lot of upheaval in the past couple of years, including the recent resignation of former leader Kerry-Lynne Findlay just 113 days after taking the reins of the party and only two days before Eby made the snap election call.

“The B.C. party system is in flux and we’ve never, in recent years, had platforms that gave reliable insight into what governments do afterwards.”

#2825. Coffee calories have jumped nearly 40%. Sugar isn't the biggest culprit

Americans are getting more calories from their daily coffee, even though the amount they drink has barely changed. Researchers at the Gerald J. and Dorothy R. Friedman School of Nutrition Science and Policy at Tufts University have identified a surprising reason for the increase: non-dairy creamer, which has overtaken sugar and milk as the largest contributor to added calories in coffee.

The study, published online in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, examined dietary information from 30,000 American adults aged 20 and older. Between 2003 and 2018, average coffee consumption stayed relatively constant at approximately 13 ounces per day. However, the average number of calories consumed from coffee increased from 43 to 60 per day, a rise of nearly 40%. By 2018, the leading sources of added coffee calories were non-dairy creamer, sugar or syrup, and dairy milk.

"Coffee consumption has been associated with several health benefits. However, coffee is often accompanied by the addition of sugar and cream, which can contribute to an increased consumption of calories from added sugar and saturated fat," said Fang Fang Zhang, senior author of the study and Neely Family Professor at the Friedman School. "The more we know about what people are consuming, the better we can understand the link between nutrition and overall health."

How Researchers Tracked Changes in Coffee Habits

To investigate how Americans' coffee habits evolved, the research team examined eight consecutive cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), covering 2003 through 2018. The nationally representative dataset included 30,000 adults aged 20 and older, each of whom completed two 24-hour dietary recalls.

Researchers separated coffee beverages into two broad groups: non-combination beverages and those containing additional ingredients. They then classified the additions into several categories, including dairy milk, half-and-half, dairy cream, non-dairy milk, non-dairy creamer, sugar/syrup, sugar substitutes, and other ingredients such as alcohol and cocoa powder.

Some participants reported consuming pre-mixed coffee beverages/powder, making it necessary to estimate the amounts of individual ingredients they contained. To do this, the researchers used the USDA Food and Nutrition Database for Dietary Studies corresponding to each survey cycle, along with linked Standard Reference codes.

Non-Dairy Creamer Consumption More Than Doubled

The analysis showed that Americans' overall coffee intake, including coffee consumed without additions, remained largely unchanged between 2003 and 2018. What changed was the amount and type of ingredients being mixed into those beverages.

In 2018, dairy milk was the most commonly added ingredient by weight, averaging 21.4 grams per day. Non-dairy creamer followed at 8.1 grams per day, while sugar accounted for 4.4 grams per day. Consumption of dairy milk, sugar, and sugar substitutes showed no statistically significant changes over the study period.

Non-dairy additions, however, followed a very different pattern. Their average consumption rose from 3.65 grams per day in 2003 to 10.5 grams per day in 2018. Much of this growth came from non-dairy creamer, which increased from 3.37 to 8 grams per day. Non-dairy milk consumption also climbed sharply, rising from 0.27 to 2.45 grams per day.

Other coffee additions increased slightly as well, with an average gain of 0.5 grams per day.

Why Non-Dairy Creamer Now Adds More Calories Than Sugar

Although dairy milk remained the most common coffee addition by weight, non-dairy creamer had become the leading source of added calories by 2018.

On average, non-dairy creamer contributed 18.5 calories per day, compared with 16 calories from sugar and 11 calories from dairy milk. In 2003, non-dairy creamer had contributed just 7.71 calories per day, illustrating how dramatically its role in Americans' coffee consumption had changed.

"We found that non-dairy creamer being added to coffee has now passed both sugar and dairy for its contribution to the total energy consumed from coffee. Non-dairy creamers can be high in saturated fat, which is calorie dense. This could explain the increased calories that non-dairy creamer is contributing," said Yongyi Pan, the study's lead author and a Ph.D. candidate at the Friedman School. "Even though it has been on the market since the 1950s, non-dairy creamer has increased in popularity in recent years as consumers seek plant-based or dairy alternatives."

Sleep, Work Hours, and Coffee Consumption

The researchers also investigated whether coffee consumption differed across groups of adults based on their daily routines and overall diet quality.

They found that people who slept fewer hours generally drank more coffee than those who slept longer. A similar pattern emerged among adults who worked longer hours, who tended to consume more coffee than those with shorter work schedules.

However, coffee consumption was comparable among people with higher and lower overall diet quality, suggesting that drinking more or less coffee was not strongly associated with how healthy their broader diets were.

The researchers acknowledged that the study relied on participants recalling what they had eaten and drunk, which can introduce measurement errors. Because food and beverage consumption can vary considerably from day to day, self-reported dietary information does not always perfectly reflect people's usual habits. The research team took steps to improve the reliability of its estimates.

It is easy to get lost in the numbers when talking about fracking in Alberta: to be temporarily dazzled by the growth of production, the insatiable thirst for water and the profits that follow.

The big picture is abstract; hard to fathom. But here at Blindman Creek, the impacts are tangible.

“It’s really obvious that it’s an issue, but it’s really hard to make it concrete for people,” Iseli-Otto says. “This is an issue for people in Calgary, this is an issue for people in Edmonton, but they don’t know. The amount of water that’s getting sucked out is mind blowing.”

#2828. Your eyes dilate depending on your thoughts

Published Oct 16, 2026 11:45 AM EDT

Your eyes really can betray your thoughts, but don’t get too self-conscious about it. Researchers in Japan believe that the subtle signs our eyeballs make are more useful to neuroscientists than the interrogation room. Based on their findings recently published in the journal iScience, examining the difference physiological responses from “inner” versus “overt” speech may help us better understand everything from linguistic development in children to the efficacy of silent pep talks.

To learn more, a team at the University of Tsukuba in Honshu enlisted dozens of volunteers for a series of simple counting exercises. After observing the participants at rest while thinking about nothing in particular for a control, researchers then asked them to count sequentially (1, 2, 3…), in number-subject combinations (one dog, two dogs…), and in specific intervals (1, 3, 5…). They also completed each task both silently and aloud. In each phase, the team carefully monitored their pupil dilation.

The results were subtle, but striking. Participants’ pupils dilated during each overt speech task, but this same muscle movement depended across each inner speech directive. Pupils constricted while volunteers silently counted sequentially, dilated for interval counting, and didn’t change at all for number-subject counting. But regardless of the inner speech task, pupil size changes were always smaller than they were during overt speech.

“The results of the present study do not support the initial assumption that inner and overt speech produce equivalent pupillary responses,” the team summarized. “Across the three experiments, overt speech consistently elicited pupil dilation, whereas inner speech did not.”

This collective data indicates that our inner speech isn’t simply the nonverbal counterpart to talking aloud. Instead, it’s a form of communication that appears to correspond to lower levels of brain arousal. If true, measuring pupil dilation may become a valuable tool when attempting to assess internal language and thinking. This could be particularly useful for studying language development in young children and infants who can’t properly describe their experiences to adults yet.

#2831. The Solar Boom in Developing Nations: Fast, Chaotic, and Unequal

Rooftop solar arrays in Beirut, Lebanon. Wael Hamzeh / EPA

Soaring energy costs are driving a solar rush in developing countries, but as households unplug from the grid, those who remain face higher electricity bills. If governments fail to manage the buildout, the growth of solar could destabilize power systems and deepen economic inequality.

Look at satellite images of Pakistan and you will be struck by the number of solar panels dotting rooftops and balconies across its major cities. Solar is the single largest source of electricity in Pakistan, generating around a quarter of the country’s power during peak months. A decade ago, it was contributing less than 1 percent. The solar boom was not driven by a major subsidy program. Rather, households and businesses largely drove it themselves in response to unreliable and expensive electricity.

Pakistan is probably the most dramatic and well-known case of a bottom-up solar revolution, but it is not unique, and versions of the same story are unfolding across the world right now: Countries with hot climates, fast-growing economies, expensive energy, and fragile grids are increasingly turning to solar. Not because of policy, but because individuals are voting with their wallets. In six developing nations — Pakistan, Nigeria, Lebanon, Sri Lanka, Zambia, and Yemen — recorded solar generation has grown more than sixfold since 2019. Solar’s share of the electricity mix has risen sharply in each of them, in some cases by an order of magnitude.

But while Pakistan’s rapid, bottom-up solar shift offers a hopeful model for ramping up clean energy, it also offers a warning. The proliferation of household solar in Pakistan has led to an exodus from the national grid, rattling the network while saddling the poorest ratepayers — who cannot afford solar panels — with higher bills. Similar dynamics are now at play in Nigeria and Zambia. These countries are facing a boom and a crisis at the same time.

That matters far beyond these countries, because most of the world’s future growth in electricity demand will come from emerging and developing economies, and much of it will be supplied by solar. The challenge is clear: Solar power can be a liberatory force in developing countries, offering a new source of cheap electricity in places long starved of energy, but if governments fail to manage the transition well, the coming buildout could weaken already creaky power systems and worsen economic inequality.

The scale of the solar boom now underway in parts of the developing world is unprecedented. Sri Lanka reports that rooftop solar capacity grew more than sixfold between 2020 and 2025, while in Lebanon, recorded solar capacity rose more than tenfold over that period. In Yemen, more than half of all people now rely on solar as their main source of electricity for lighting.

What is remarkable is that much of this data does not appear in government statistics because the new installations are overwhelmingly rooftop arrays and home systems that are behind the meter and not officially registered. Analysts have increasingly turned to customs records and even satellite imagery to reconstruct what is really happening on the ground. In Nigeria, they found an estimated 96 percent of solar capacity is off-grid. In Pakistan, as much as three-quarters of the country’s actual solar deployment may never be officially recorded.

Satellite images of Sector G-13, a neighborhood in Islamabad, Pakistan, in 2022 and 2024. The area has seen an explosion of rooftop solar. Google Maps

What we see happening in these places is called “leapfrogging.” People in developing countries are adopting new energy technology without first going through the same stages of fossil-fuel development that wealthier countries went through. Leapfrogging happens when three conditions are met: The new technology is already proven in mature markets. It offers better or comparable functionality to the incumbent technology. And it provides a way around expensive or dysfunctional infrastructure.

How China became the world’s leader on renewable energy. Read more.

Solar is now a mature technology, widely adopted in wealthy countries. It has established supply chains around the world, and as manufacturing has ramped up, primarily in China, the price of solar has collapsed. Chinese manufacturers have built far more factory capacity than the world currently needs, and with trade tariffs now keeping many of their panels out of markets such as the United States and India, much of the surplus has ended up in places like Pakistan and Nigeria instead.

In countries with rickety power grids, solar can offer more reliable electricity, especially when coupled with batteries. The grid in Pakistan is frequently unstable, and in the worst-served districts, households can face 16 to 20 hours of outages in a single day. In Nigeria, around 40 percent of the population has no grid connection or electricity at all, while in Yemen, damage to the power system from the country’s brutal civil war has left close to 90 percent of the population without access to the grid. In these places, solar offers far greater functionality.

Solar also offers a workaround for expensive electricity. In Pakistan, the average electric rate almost tripled over the last decade as subsidies were withdrawn, imported fuel costs surged following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, and consumers were left paying to maintain underused power plants. In Nigeria, electricity prices for some consumers rose by over 240 percent following rate reform in 2024. Yemen offers what is perhaps the most extreme case of rising grid electricity prices: In July, its government approved increasing rates thirty-six-fold. With every rate hike, solar has become a more attractive alternative.

Climate campaigners have celebrated the solar boom underway in emerging economies, but the rapid growth of solar can create problems if it is poorly managed. Power grids have large fixed costs: Transmission systems must be maintained, and power plants must remain available even when customers buy less electricity. When households and businesses that can afford solar panels stop consuming power from the grid, utilities lose revenue. The result can be a vicious circle: Fixed costs are spread across a smaller number of customers. Rates rise, which in turn makes solar more attractive. That spurs more customers to defect from the grid, and the cycle starts again. This is the famous “utility death spiral.”

Solar panel import figures are based on Chinese customs data through the end of 2025. On-grid solar capacity is based on official statistics for 2025. Sources: Ember, IRENA. Yale Environment 360 / Made with Flourish

Pakistan is already showing signs of spiraling. Grid electricity sales fell 10 percent in 2023, and the country’s energy minister has warned that falling demand could make grid electricity increasingly unaffordable. The distributional consequences are potentially stark. Wealthier households and businesses that can afford the upfront cost of panels and batteries can insulate themselves from rising prices, but poorer households cannot. If rates are raised to recover the grid’s costs, those unable to invest in solar can end up paying more for electricity. And thus, what is a profoundly democratizing technology can ultimately worsen economic inequality when it is adopted into an already dysfunctional electricity system.

In some cases, solar users may only partly exit the system, which can make the arithmetic for the grid even worse. In Pakistan, many solar owners have sharply reduced their use of the grid during daylight hours but still rely on it after sunset. The grid, therefore, has to remain capable of meeting the evening peak even as it sells far less electricity during the day. Those still on the grid full-time are paying to keep power plants on standby so they are available to generate electricity after the sun goes down.

None of this is an argument against solar. Quite the opposite. Installing solar in countries with hot climates, growing economies, and unreliable grids generates huge benefits for the economy, education, and health. Solar pumps let farmers irrigate their fields without paying for diesel-operated equipment. Solar lets schools without grid connections run lights, fans, and computers. Swapping diesel generators for solar panels and batteries also means cleaner air. In countries like Nigeria, where millions of homes and businesses rely on diesel for their electricity, the adoption of solar can have a meaningful impact on public health.

An important task for governments and international agencies is to bring distributed solar into official energy statistics, using import data, registrations, and, where necessary, satellite monitoring. Think tanks such as Ember in the U.K. and Renewables First in Pakistan are already piecing this picture together from customs data. Governments need to use that data for planning. With a clearer picture of what is happening on the ground, officials can begin to integrate fast-growing solar capacity into the power system and do it in a way that strengthens the grid rather than undermines it. That means installing batteries that can store cheap solar during the day for use in the evening. It means building more flexible transmission networks that can move surplus rooftop power to where it is needed instead of wasting it. And it means offering targeted support for low-income households to make solar affordable for more people.

Why isn’t this happening already? One reason is that regulators in developing countries are struggling to keep up with the solar boom. The rules on electric rates and grid connections were written for a system built around large power plants rather than millions of rooftop arrays. Many utilities are also in deep financial trouble — Pakistan’s power sector alone has accumulated billions of dollars of debt. This leaves few financial resources for batteries, smart meters, or network upgrades.

Governments need to update regulations to make it easier for households and businesses with solar arrays to sell power back to the grid. Wealthy nations and development banks can help countries integrate solar by providing the financing they need to modernize their power systems. However, that support should be tied to regulatory reform and not used to cover utility losses.

Workers install solar panels on a home in Lagos, Nigeria. Sunday Alamba / AP Photo

Lebanon, whose extraordinary solar boom may have begun to plateau, offers a glimpse of the path forward. With a new government in place and a $250 million World Bank loan to build solar farms, modernize the state utility’s electric billing, and set up a national control center to manage the power network, the country has an opportunity to integrate thousands of decentralized systems into a functioning grid. That will mean registering rooftop systems, paying their owners a fair price for surplus power, and using that solar to cut the utility’s costly fuel imports.

Nigeria, on the other hand, risks following the same path as Pakistan, but with an even weaker distribution sector to manage the consequences. Its 2024 rate increase fell on its top tier of customers, who are promised at least 20 hours of power a day. These are the businesses and better-off households that have the resources to switch to solar. They are also the customers whose monthly payments the struggling distribution companies rely on most. Rising electric rates will likely spur many consumers to take up solar and ditch the grid.

A first among major nations, India is industrializing with solar. Read more.

The problem with solar is not the technology itself. The key challenge in many developing countries is that the existing power infrastructure and the institutions running it are struggling to keep pace with the energy transition. The choice for governments is between letting distributed solar spur an unmanaged exit from the grid or intentionally making solar part of a more flexible and reliable network. A well-managed transition can ensure that the solar boom strengthens the electricity system and protects those who cannot afford to leave it behind.

#2832. Will a macaque eat a grape sitting on a pile of poo? A study on germophobia

One of the macaques at a Texas sanctuary, where researchers ran tests to see if the monkeys became germophobes after experiencing a viral outbreak. Stephan Kaufhold hide caption

In the very early days of COVID-19, millions who'd rarely thought twice about germ avoidance became germophobes.

Some folks wiped down groceries before stuffing them into the refrigerator. Others let mail quarantine outside for days lest it harbor stray viruses. With the hindsight that SARS-CoV-2 spread primarily through the air, these sorts of precautions seem extreme. But in the moment, they were perhaps reasonable ways of avoiding an unknown pathogen.

Around this same time, a different virus hit a macaque sanctuary in Texas. New research suggests the monkeys responded in similar ways as humans did to COVID-19 — by being extra germophobic.

Humans are far from the only animals to avoid possible sources of disease. Scientists have found that critters as diverse as monkeys and nematodes (aka roundworms) can sense and steer clear of feces, for instance, which can harbor harmful bacteria.

"Something that has been less studied is the question of how flexible this avoidance is," says Stephan Kaufhold, a cognitive scientist at UC San Diego. He wanted to see if experiencing an outbreak could make individual monkeys even more wary of picking up pathogens.

The outbreak in the Texas sanctuary, home to over 100 macaques, offered Kaufhold and his colleagues an opportunity to put that question to the test.

In 2020, macaques in an enclosure at the sanctuary started getting sick with an HIV-like virus called SRV.

Once a monkey gets infected through bodily fluids, the disease "can take a rapid progression, where about three to four weeks after you initially get infected, you might die," says Kaufhold. Many monkeys died, while others developed chronic immunodeficiency.

Sanctuary staff quickly started separating monkeys quickly, ultimately creating two groups — those who'd experienced the virus, either by getting sick themselves or witnessing others get sick, and those who hadn't experienced the outbreak.

To see if those different histories made the monkeys more germophobic, the researchers presented individuals with two tasty treats — grapes.

"They get a fantastic diet at the sanctuary, but not grapes every day, so grapes were definitely a treat," says Kaufhold.

One red grape was presented on a non-descript, clean-looking piece of wood. The other was on top of macaque poop, encased in epoxy. "Basically not to make the animals eat off actual feces," says Kaufhold. "It did look quite gross, feces with a glossy wet look. Definitely worked also in triggering disgust in humans."

From 2022 to 2024, the researchers presented monkeys with this choice in nearly 200 trials. Among monkeys who didn't experience the outbreak, 79% ate the poop-plated grape. But only 47% of the outbreak veterans ate it, researchers report in the journal Royal Society Open Science.

Essentially, those who'd been oblivious to the outbreak were more likely to throw caution to the wind and eat the poop-plated grape than those who'd lived through it.

"I was surprised," says Kaufhold. "I think it's pretty clear evidence that something is going on." Kaufhold thinks that something might be a heightened sensitivity to disgust.

"If you think about disgust, the evolutionary origins most likely are to try to protect the body from contamination because there are pathogens and parasites that we ideally would want to avoid," he says. Becoming more easily disgusted after a devastating outbreak could pay off if it means avoiding infection, of whatever cause.

Of course, scientists can't know whether macaques are actually experiencing disgust. "But the possibility that things like disgust are influencing the behavior of other species, and especially species like primates, it's definitely a possibility worth considering," says Andrew MacIntosh, a biologist at the Wilder Institute who wasn't involved in the study but has done similar experiments with macaques.

"It seems plausible that past exposure to outbreaks could make individuals more cautious or risk averse," he says, but he thinks more evidence is needed to show that. For example, the experiment was carried out several years after the outbreak, enough time for other factors to influence these behaviors, he says.

MacIntosh also points out that the fecal avoidance experiment bears little connection to how the initial outbreak spread, through bodily fluids transmitted through scratches or bites. "The mode of transmission of that virus has really nothing to do with fecal contamination," he says.

"We're not claiming that macaques had any sort of understanding of the mechanism of viruses, it just seems to be an increased caution," he says. People didn't initially know how SARS-CoV-2 spread either, he says, but many went to great lengths to avoid infection. Those impulses may have been shaped not by paranoia, but by evolution, as a way of staving off sickness in an uncertain world.

#2835. A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea and unleashed a 330-foot tsunami

A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea roughly 43 to 46 million years ago, triggering a tsunami more than 100 meters (330 feet) high. Now, after more than two decades of scientific disagreement, researchers have uncovered compelling evidence confirming the ancient collision.

The impact created what is now known as the Silverpit Crater, a massive structure buried deep beneath the seafloor. Although scientists first discovered it in 2002, its origins remained fiercely debated, with some researchers arguing that geological processes, rather than an object from space, were responsible.

A research team led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson of Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh has now provided the strongest evidence yet that Silverpit was formed by an asteroid or comet. By combining advanced seismic imaging, microscopic examination of rock samples, and computer simulations, the scientists have reconstructed the violent event and identified minerals that could only have formed under extraordinary pressure.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, establish Silverpit as one of the relatively few confirmed impact craters on Earth.

A Mysterious Crater Beneath the North Sea

The Silverpit Crater lies approximately 700 meters beneath the seabed in the southern North Sea, about 80 miles off the coast of Yorkshire, England. The crater itself measures roughly three kilometers across and is surrounded by a much larger, 20-kilometer-wide region of circular faults, which are fractures in the rock where movement has occurred.

When scientists first identified the formation in 2002, several features suggested that it had been created by a high-speed collision with an asteroid or comet.

Its nearly circular outline, elevated central peak, and surrounding rings of faults resembled structures found at other impact sites. Central peaks can develop when rocks compressed by a powerful collision rebound upward, somewhat like the way water rises in the center after an object splashes into a pool.

Some geologists proposed that the unusual formation resulted from the movement of underground salt deposits, which can deform the rock layers above them. Others suggested that volcanic activity had caused part of the seabed to collapse, producing a structure that only appeared to be an impact crater.

The disagreement persisted for years. In 2009, geologists even voted on the competing explanations, as documented in Geoscientist magazine. Most participants rejected the asteroid impact hypothesis.

The latest findings show that the impact explanation was correct.

Scientists Find the Smoking Gun for an Ancient Asteroid Impact

The breakthrough came from combining newly available seismic images with microscopic evidence recovered from beneath the seabed.

Seismic imaging allows geologists to investigate structures hidden underground by analyzing how sound waves travel through different layers of rock. This technique can reveal buried faults, craters, and other formations that cannot be observed directly.

Using the improved data, Nicholson and his colleagues were able to examine Silverpit's internal structure in unprecedented detail.

But the most decisive evidence came from tiny mineral crystals discovered in rock cuttings collected from an oil well near the crater.

Dr. Uisdean Nicholson, a sedimentologist in Heriot-Watt University's School of Energy, Geoscience, Infrastructure and Society, said: "New seismic imaging has given us an unprecedented look at the crater.

"Samples from an oil well in the area also revealed rare 'shocked' quartz and feldspar crystals at the same depth as the crater floor.

"We were exceptionally lucky to find these -- a real 'needle-in-a-haystack' effort. These prove the impact crater hypothesis beyond doubt, because they have a fabric that can only be created by extreme shock pressures."

Shocked minerals are particularly important because their internal crystal structures have been altered by immense pressure. Such changes can occur when an asteroid strikes at tremendous speed, generating powerful shock waves that pass through the surrounding rock.

Ordinary geological processes generally cannot produce these distinctive microscopic features. Their discovery at the same depth as the crater floor provides strong physical evidence that an extremely violent impact occurred.

A 160-Meter Asteroid Unleashed a Monster Tsunami

With the crater's origins established, researchers used numerical models, or computer simulations based on physical laws, to reconstruct what happened during the collision.

Their results suggest that an asteroid approximately 160 meters across approached from the west and struck the seabed at a shallow angle.

Although much smaller than the asteroid associated with the extinction of the dinosaurs, an object of this size could still release an enormous amount of energy when traveling at cosmic speeds.

According to the simulations, the impact violently displaced vast quantities of rock and seawater, producing a towering curtain of debris before the material collapsed back into the ocean.

Dr. Nicholson said: "Our evidence shows that a 160-meter-wide asteroid hit the seabed at a low angle from the west.

"Within minutes, it created a 1.5-kilometer-high curtain of rock and water that then collapsed into the sea, creating a tsunami over 100 meters high."

That means the collision sent material nearly a mile into the air before it fell back into the sea, generating a tsunami taller than a 30-story building.

Unlike ordinary wind-driven waves, tsunamis involve the movement of enormous volumes of water. They are typically generated when a sudden disturbance, such as an earthquake, underwater landslide, or asteroid impact, rapidly displaces the water above it.

The estimated height of Silverpit's tsunami comes from computer modeling of the ancient impact rather than direct observation. Nevertheless, the reconstruction illustrates just how destructive even a relatively modest asteroid can be when it strikes the ocean.

A Scientific Debate Finally Settled After Decades

For Professor Gareth Collins of Imperial College London, the discovery brings a satisfying conclusion to a controversy he witnessed firsthand.

Collins participated in the 2009 debate over Silverpit's origins and contributed the numerical simulations used in the new research.

Professor Collins said: "I always thought that the impact hypothesis was the simplest explanation and most consistent with the observations.

"It is very rewarding to have finally found the silver bullet. We can now get on with the exciting job of using the amazing new data to learn more about how impacts shape planets below the surface, which is really hard to do on other planets. "

The findings do more than settle an old argument. They also give scientists a valuable opportunity to study the underground damage caused by high-speed asteroid impacts.

Because much of Silverpit's structure remains buried and well preserved, researchers can investigate how the collision fractured, displaced, and reshaped the surrounding geological layers.

These observations could help scientists interpret impact structures on other worlds, where the subsurface effects of collisions are often much more difficult to examine.

Earth has experienced countless asteroid and comet impacts throughout its history, but relatively few recognizable craters remain.

Weathering, erosion, sediment deposition, and plate tectonics continually reshape the planet's surface. Over millions of years, these processes can wear down, bury, or completely destroy evidence of ancient collisions.

As a result, exceptionally preserved impact sites like Silverpit are scientifically valuable.

Dr. Nicholson said, "Silverpit is a rare and exceptionally preserved hypervelocity impact crater.

"These are rare because the Earth is such a dynamic planet -- plate tectonics and erosion destroy almost all traces of most of these events.

"Around 200 confirmed impact craters exist on land, and only about 33 have been identified beneath the ocean.

"We can use these findings to understand how asteroid impacts shaped our planet throughout history, as well as predict what could happen should we have an asteroid collision in future."

Hypervelocity impacts occur when objects strike at extremely high speeds, typically several kilometers per second. At those velocities, even an asteroid measuring only a few hundred meters across can deliver a devastating amount of energy.

With its origins now confirmed, Silverpit joins a small group of important underwater and buried impact structures, including the Chicxulub Crater in Mexico, associated with the mass extinction that wiped out the nonavian dinosaurs, and the Nadir Crater off the coast of West Africa, another recently confirmed asteroid impact site.

Together, these craters provide evidence of the powerful role cosmic collisions have played in shaping Earth's geological and biological history.

By studying Silverpit's remarkably preserved features, scientists hope to better understand what happens when an asteroid strikes the ocean, how the surrounding rock responds, and what such an event might mean for Earth in the future.

The thing with these drugs is that they work incredibly well. Whether it’s semaglutide (Ozempic), tirzepatide (Mounjaro), or the yet-to-be-FDA-approved retatrutide, these compounds are very good at getting you to eat less. They have other effects, too, like slowed gastric emptying, “slower” digestion, and others—some positive, some more negative.

But the one thing almost everyone reports is that you eat far less food on these things.

That can be good if you need it. Most people eat too much. Even if it’s healthy food. I remember being at PrimalCon back in the day and being shocked by the amount of food people could put away. Everything was high quality, cooked in good fats, well-sourced, but people would routinely report that they gained weight at PrimalCon. Food is food. Volume is volume. And that’s why these drugs are having such powerful effects in the overweight and obese. They’re losing weight because they aren’t eating very much and, most importantly, they aren’t hungry doing it.

But what about when you start losing too much? When you start losing muscle, losing energy?

Everyone says the obvious: “make your calories count.” Get your micronutrients. Make it high quality, whole foods. With less volume coming in, the quality of what little volume you do get becomes increasingly important.

And they talk about protein, too, rightfully. A big part of countering the negative effects of GLP drugs, like muscle loss, requires that you stay active. Lift weights, and support that activity by eating protein.

These are all good, but you’ve heard them before.

The one piece of advice you might not have heard, that sounds a bit unconventional, but that becomes useful especially as you start approaching your ideal weight: eat more.

Try to eat a bit more than you feel like.

Many issues we see with these drugs are from undernourishment. You’re running a deficit and, depending on the drug in question, burning more energy. You need to eat a little more than you think.

#2845. Dandelion, Water and Surface Tension Investigation

Submerging a dandelion in water always looks impressive, as the delicate puffball (seed head) appears wet in the water but is completely dry when removed. It’s a bit like a super high-tech rain coat.

Several factors contribute to the waterproof properties of a dandelion seed head.

This investigation uses dish soap ( washing up liquid ) to disrupt the surface tension of water to discover how it affects the waterproof properties of a dandelion puffball.

Fill the glass or jar about 3/4 full of water.

Gently submerge one of the dandelion seed heads into the glass of water and pull it back out. You should find the seed head looks dry!

Add a little dish soap to the water and stir.

Submerge the dandelion again. You should find it gets wet and falls apart.

The layer of air doesn’t just keep the dandelion seeds dry. It allows them to float on top of water, so if they fall into a puddle, they don’t just sink.

You probably noticed that when the dandelion was dunked in water, it looked like it had a silver coating. This is caused by a thin layer of trapped air. The bristles of the parachutes (pappus) repel water, and because they are so closely packed, they hold onto a bubble of air, preventing water from getting inside. When the dandelion is pulled out of the water, it’s completely dry as the water only came into contact with the air bubble around the bristles.

A second factor is that the high surface tension of water creates a ‘skin’ that doesn’t allow it to bend around the tightly packed dandelion bristles. When dish soap (washing-up liquid) is added, the water’s surface tension breaks, and it slides past the trapped air to reach the dandelion bristles. The shield of air collapses, and the bristles clump together.

#2846. Millions take fish oil for brain health. A two-year study found no benefit

Millions of Americans take fish oil supplements in hopes of protecting their memory and keeping their brains healthy as they age. These products have become a major business, with Americans spending more than $1 billion each year on supplements containing omega-3 fatty acids. These essential nutrients help build and maintain the connections between brain cells that support memory, learning, and other cognitive functions.

But a new study suggests that getting more omega-3s into the brain through supplements may not provide the protection many people expect.

Researchers at Keck Medicine of USC found that even when high doses of omega-3s successfully reached the brain, they did not produce measurable improvements in memory, thinking abilities, or protection against brain shrinkage.

The findings, published in eBioMedicine, come from a two-year clinical trial involving older adults considered to be at increased risk of developing Alzheimer's disease. Despite taking omega-3 supplements daily, participants showed no significant cognitive advantage over those who received a placebo.

"We all wish there was a silver bullet for preventing Alzheimer's, but our findings showed that fish oil supplements do not appear to protect brain health," said Hussein Naji Yassine, MD, director of the USC Center for Personalized Brain Health and lead investigator of the study. "While omega-3s play an important role in forming brain cell connections needed for cognition, our results do not support fish oil supplements as a preventive measure against Alzheimer's."

A Two-Year Study Tests Fish Oil's Brain Benefits

To investigate whether omega-3 supplements could protect the aging brain, researchers enrolled 365 adults between the ages of 55 and 80. All participants rarely ate fish, a major dietary source of omega-3 fatty acids, and were considered at elevated risk for Alzheimer's disease.

Nearly half (47%) carried APOE4, a gene variant associated with a substantially higher risk of developing Alzheimer's later in life. Although having this variant does not mean someone will necessarily develop the disease, it is the strongest known genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer's.

Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a daily fish oil supplement or a placebo, an inactive treatment used for comparison. The supplements provided 2,000 mg of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), an omega-3 fatty acid that is especially important for the brain.

DHA is a major component of brain cell membranes, helping maintain the structure and function of neurons. It also supports communication between nerve cells, making it an important nutrient for normal brain activity.

The study was double-blind, meaning neither participants nor researchers knew who was receiving the active supplement during the trial. This approach helps reduce bias and makes it easier to determine whether any observed benefits are actually caused by the treatment.

Omega-3 Levels Rose, but Memory Did Not Improve

Before evaluating whether the supplements improved brain health, researchers needed to answer a fundamental question: Were the omega-3 fatty acids actually reaching the brain?

To find out, they measured DHA concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid that surrounds and cushions the brain and spinal cord. Examining this fluid can provide information about substances entering and circulating within the central nervous system.

After six months, DHA levels in the cerebrospinal fluid had increased by an average of 17% among participants taking the supplements. This provided evidence that the omega-3 fatty acids were reaching their intended destination.

However, getting DHA into the central nervous system was only part of the goal. The more important question was whether those higher levels would translate into better brain health.

Researchers assessed participants' memory and other cognitive abilities at the beginning of the trial and again after two years. Despite the increase in DHA, those taking fish oil performed no better on the cognitive tests than participants receiving the placebo.

Brain imaging revealed similarly disappointing results.

The supplements did not prevent shrinkage of the hippocampus, a region deep within the brain that plays a central role in forming and organizing memories. The hippocampus is particularly vulnerable to changes associated with aging and Alzheimer's disease, making its size an important indicator researchers use when studying brain health.

Taken together, the results suggest that simply increasing DHA availability in the brain is not enough to produce measurable cognitive benefits in this group of older adults.

Why Fish Oil Supplements May Work Differently Than Food

The unexpected findings have raised a new question for researchers: If omega-3s can reach the brain, why don't higher levels necessarily improve its function?

Yassine and his colleagues are now investigating how the brain absorbs, processes, and uses these nutrients. Their earlier research suggests that omega-3s may be more beneficial when consumed as part of a broader dietary pattern rather than taken in isolation as a supplement.

One possibility involves the Mediterranean-style diet, which emphasizes foods such as fish, vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and olive oil. This eating pattern naturally supplies omega-3 fatty acids alongside other nutrients and has been associated with a lower risk of Alzheimer's disease.

The researchers suspect that the combination of nutrients in a balanced diet, along with an individual's overall health, may influence how effectively omega-3s support the brain.

"We're focused on better understanding how the brain processes omega-3s and whether factors, such as poor health, dietary pattern, genetic risk and age, may change the brain's ability to effectively absorb and use omega-3s," said Yassine. "We are working to develop medications that may help the brain better utilize these nutrients to preserve cognitive function."

Understanding these differences could eventually help scientists identify why certain people might respond better to omega-3s than others and whether new treatments could improve the brain's ability to benefit from these essential fats.

Importantly, the findings do not mean omega-3 fatty acids are unnecessary or that eating fish offers no health benefits. Instead, they indicate that taking high-dose DHA supplements alone did not provide measurable protection against cognitive decline or hippocampal shrinkage in the population studied.

Healthy Habits Remain Key to Alzheimer's Prevention

Although the trial focused specifically on fish oil supplements, the researchers emphasize that protecting brain health requires a much broader approach.

Rather than relying on a single supplement, they recommend maintaining healthy habits throughout life, including regular physical activity, sufficient high-quality sleep, and a nutritious, balanced diet.

These behaviors support overall health and may help reduce factors associated with cognitive decline. Physical exercise, for example, promotes cardiovascular health and blood flow, while sleep plays an important role in memory formation and normal brain maintenance.

"Staying healthy throughout life remains the most powerful tool we have for reducing Alzheimer's risk, including regular exercise, quality sleep and a balanced diet," said Yassine. "Living a healthy lifestyle is the brain's equivalent of getting regular car maintenance and high-quality oil changes. The brain is more likely to lose greater function if health issues in other parts of the body go unaddressed, in the same way that car engines stop working if regular maintenance is skipped."

While these lifestyle factors were not directly tested in the trial, the researchers believe they offer a more promising foundation for long-term brain health than depending on fish oil supplements alone.

The results also highlight an important distinction: A nutrient can be essential for healthy brain function without providing additional protection when consumed in large doses as a supplement. Understanding why that happens may open new avenues for research into Alzheimer's prevention and the nutritional needs of the aging brain.

#2848. Colostrum vs. Collagen: Which One Should You Take?

A surprisingly common query I get is colostrum vs. collagen—which is better? Maybe it’s the fact that they both start with the same three letters. Maybe it’s that they’re both alternative health darlings currently enjoying a huge uptick in interest.

Whatever the reason for their connection in people’s minds, I thought it would be a good idea to do a quick write-up on how colostrum and collagen might interact in the body, what the relative benefits are, and whether you can take them both together.

Contrary to what the influencers might tell you, there isn’t a ton of research showing that bovine colostrum has a wide range of benefits for adults. It’s definitely helpful for newborns, since their guts are incredibly permeable, and colostrum is literally the first food they’re supposed to eat. Even cow colostrum has been shown to improve outcomes for newborns and premature infants. No arguments there.

But I’d caution against expecting too much from colostrum supplements. The best case for adding it to your diet as an adult is improving gut health, reducing intestinal permeability, and improving immune function—particularly for athletes.

Colostrum has been heavily researched for its ability to reduce gut permeability in various populations, including athletes, medical patients, and everyday folks.1-4 People who take bovine colostrum supplements often report better gut health and a more intact gut lining. Zonulin, a gut compound that increases permeability, frequently drops with colostrum intake.

Researchers aren’t entirely sure how bovine colostrum benefits the gut. Some people claim it regulates specific growth factors in the gut lining that help repair and support its structure. That’s colostrum’s primary role in infancy: improving gut viability so the immune system can develop, enabling digestion of food instead of relying on the womb for sustenance. Thanks in large part to colostrum benefits, this new member of the human race is able to handle what the world throws at it and develop a healthy immune response and gut lining

The challenge is that the adult gut isn’t as permeable as a newborn’s by design. For instance, the IGF-1 in bovine colostrum isn’t absorbed or utilized by adults.5

Colostrum can also regulate inflammation and help maintain iron balance in athletes, largely due to its lactoferrin content.6 Lactoferrin is an immune protein that improves iron absorption while reducing free iron—an inflammatory agent—circulating in the body. Athletes taking colostrum for instance see higher spikes in inflammation during workouts but lower resting inflammation, showing it promotes “smart” inflammation.

Meanwhile, collagen benefits for gut health are much more direct. Collagen provides the actual building blocks—amino acids like glycine—for repairing and growing the gut lining. Whether it turns out that colostrum does indeed trigger growth factors that help the gut repair itself (by no means confirmed), or if it just lowers inflammation via lactoferrin which allows the repair to proceed, you’ll need extra collagen to make those repairs.

Colostrum assists with gut repair while collagen provides a substrate required for the repair to occur.

And so the two compounds are not competing with each other, but potentially complementary. Both colostrum and collagen improve gut health, enhance skin health and appearance, and boost immune function by different but complementary routes.

Yes, you can absolutely take collagen and colostrum together. In fact, I’d argue that if colostrum works, they’re almost made for each other. You don’t need to mix them in the same shake or take them simultaneously; just include both in your routine for maximum benefit.

Adding both colostrum and collagen supplements can create a synergistic effect, particularly for gut and immune health. Collagen serves as the substrate, while colostrum provides potential activation via growth factors.

It depends on your dietary needs. If you only take one, take collagen. Collagen is like the fourth macronutrient, providing glycine—an amino acid we no longer get enough of in modern diets. This makes collagen essential for maintaining skin, bones, blood vessels, and more. It’s also widely beneficial, whether you’re looking for joint health, anti-aging, or gut support.

If you regularly consume foods like bone broth, gelatinous meats, or stews, you may already be getting plenty of collagen. In that case, colostrum supplements could add more value, especially for individuals with immune or gut issues that dietary changes haven’t resolved.

Colostrum, derived from the mature milk of cows after giving birth, is essentially absent from the diet unless you specifically seek it out. It’s best suited for people dealing with gut inflammation or immune dysregulation. Athletes might benefit from colostrum’s ability to regulate iron and inflammation, while others may see improved resistance to respiratory infections.

Adults prone to upper respiratory tract infections.

Individuals with leaky gut or gut inflammation.

If you’re on a budget, you can likely replicate most of the colostrum supplement benefits with a high-quality whey isolate (which contains some lactoferrin, but not as much as colostrum) and a dedicated lactoferrin supplement. My guess is that lactoferrin is doing much of the heavy lifting for colostrum’s benefits.

Both colostrum and collagen offer unique benefits, but collagen stands out as the more universally essential supplement. Its role in collagen production, gut lining repair, and overall health makes it indispensable for most people. Bovine colostrum can complement collagen, especially in specific scenarios, but it isn’t as necessary or widely applicable.

For most, collagen is the clear winner. Still, if you have unique needs or simply want to experiment with bovine colostrum benefits, adding both to your regimen can offer the best of both worlds.
