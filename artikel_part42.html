#2060. Why quitting your job might be your best decision yet

Below, Lindsay Crouse shares five key insights from her new book, The Case for Quitting: The Surprising Benefits of Opting Out.

Lindsay is a marathon runner and journalist who worked at The New York Times for 15 years. Her reporting helped bring maternity rights to pregnant Nike athletes.

What’s the Big Idea?

Quitting isn’t failure. Knowing when to walk away from a goal that no longer serves us can free up the time, energy, and opportunity we need to build something better.

Listen to the audio version of this Book Bite—read by Lindsay herself—in the Next Big Idea App, or buy the book.

1. Perseverance can work against us.

Perseverance is essential. That’s why our culture holds it in such high regard. But misplaced perseverance backfires. It can lead us to push too hard against dead ends, pouring ourselves into futile goals we don’t actually have the power to achieve.

When we fail to quit, we waste precious resources—energy, money, time—on pursuits that make us increasingly miserable, even as we fall short of what we want. Focusing on the work keeps us from registering the mounting costs, or even recognizing that we’re failing at all.

I spoke to a woman who poured a decade of her life and thousands of dollars from her savings into fertility treatments, only to finally accept that they weren’t working. She quit, and began rebuilding the life she’d put on hold. A year later, she became pregnant naturally. Despite the promises of those interventions, her fertility goal had never been fully in her control. I also spoke with a woman who pushed toward her dream of becoming an Olympian, even as her diligent training led to overtraining and injury, turning the sport she loved into an ordeal.

Both women had spent their lives learning to do hard things. But they hadn’t learned what to do when perseverance failed them. For both, quitting was the only realistic way out—no matter how much it felt like failure. Continuing would have only brought more failure. Quitting freed them to redirect their energy toward pursuits where they could succeed.

2. Fear of quitting is often misplaced.

When we contemplate quitting, we’re easily paralyzed by fear. Usually that fear centers on judgment: the worry that we made the wrong choice, that others will notice and think less of us.

But this fear is often proven to be unfounded. There’s a cognitive bias called the spotlight effect in which we tend to think that others are paying more attention to our choices than they actually are. I spoke with an actress who quit her career after noticing that everyone around her was putting their lives on hold for a dream they no longer truly believed in—largely because they’d already told everyone back home that they were going to be a star. She felt that pressure too. If she quit, what would everyone think? It turned out they thought the same thing as when she’d set out for Hollywood: Sounds great, we support you. People rarely care about what we do as much as we care about it ourselves.

“We tend to think that others are paying more attention to our choices than they actually are.”

Then there’s fear of our own judgment. What if we regret our decision to leave? But research shows that over the long arc of our lives, we tend to regret inaction more than action. Quitting is an action. It may mean taking a loss, but it also means opening yourself to new opportunities.

We should fear failing to decide more than the act of quitting itself. Kicking these decisions down the road to our five- or 10-year-older selves only leaves them with the same hard questions and even higher stakes. Quitting doesn’t get easier. We just become older.

3. Testing the waters usually beats a cannonball.

We often think of quitting as one blunt, seismic act—the day we’ve finally had enough and storm out in a rage. But that’s rarely the healthiest way to cut the cord. Instead of diving headfirst into a new life, we can wade in slowly.

In one research study, would-be entrepreneurs who kept their steady jobs while building a runway to their next step were more likely to succeed than those who quit outright. Knowing this helps counter the mental pushback of “I can’t quit,” because wading in mitigates the risk.

R&B legend Bill Withers is a great example of this approach. He was born into poverty in the segregated coal country of West Virginia and worked a series of jobs to support himself, eventually landing work in Los Angeles manufacturing toilet seats for airplanes. At the same time, he started playing guitar in clubs, exploring new interests. He discovered he was enormously talented, but he kept his factory job. Even after his breakthrough song, “Ain’t No Sunshine,” became a hit, he hedged his bets and kept making toilet seats. It was only after he was laid off that he realized he was ready to embrace his new career. By then, he had the gift, the confidence, and the financial foundation to succeed on his own terms.

4. Women’s endurance can become a weakness.

Women have specific physical, mental, and social qualities that help them endure more and quit less than men, especially in scenarios where conditions are harsh and the stakes are high. I wrote about this for The New York Times in 2018, after watching a Boston Marathon staged in freezing rain, where women finished at higher rates than men.

“We live longer than men, but we don’t live better.”

But is all this endurance good for women’s life outcomes? We live longer than men, but we don’t live better. Women are more likely to battle chronic pain, lie awake with insomnia, and suffer migraines. Women are catching up to men in rates of excessive drinking, and are more likely to take medication for anxiety and depression. Women graduate from school at higher rates than men, but our incomes are lower and we occupy fewer leadership positions. Motherhood complicates this dynamic further.

Is women’s ability to keep going at all costs something to celebrate? In a world that will demand as much as it can get from us, how can women learn to moderate our ability to persevere, before this strength begins to hold us back?

5. Quitting can be the path to better.

Remember the aspiring Olympian from the first insight—the one whose relentless perseverance led to injury and burnout? After she quit track, she didn’t run at all for years. She started a family. Got a regular job. Found other interests. Moved on. With time, her injuries healed. And then she started running again, just for fun.

Everything was different this time. Instead of running for medals, she was running for herself. Suddenly, she got faster and faster. Eventually, she set the national record in the marathon, reaching heights she’d never approached during her years of grinding pursuit.

The point isn’t that quitting is a comeback tool or a success hack. It’s that quitting deserves to be treated as a genuine option—far more than the never-give-up, keep-showing-up messages we’re so used to seeing blasted around us. We’re mostly conditioned to see quitting as a way to close a door, but that’s only half the story: It often opens doors in ways we can’t predict.

#2066. Trump's executive order targets mortgage insurance, refinance costs. What it means for borrowers

President Donald Trump has signed an executive order that aims to reduce mortgage costs as rising interest rates and inflation make homeownership less affordable.

The order, titled "Decreasing Housing Costs for the American People," directs federal agencies to proactively cancel mortgage insurance on eligible future loans and expand a program to make refinancing cheaper, among other actions.

In an X post on Thursday, Federal Housing Finance Agency director Bill Pulte said "millions of Americans will save thousands and thousands of dollars."

But it's difficult to know the future consumer impact without more details, according to Keith Gumbinger, vice president of mortgage website HSH.

Trump's executive order comes less than two weeks before the Nov. 3 midterm elections, with rising costs and housing affordability a major concern among voters. It builds on previous orders to restrict Wall Street firms from buying single-family homes, among other actions.

Homeownership has become less affordable in recent years. In 2025, the monthly payment for a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage on a median-priced home was roughly 33% of the median family's income, up from 26% in 2022, according to the Federal Reserve's latest Survey of Consumer Finances published in early October.

Whether you're a current homeowner or future buyer, here are some of the key things to know about the new executive order.

Conventional loans — mortgages not insured or backed by U.S. government programs — typically require private mortgage insurance, or PMI, when a down payment is less than 20% of the home's purchase price. By contrast, Federal Housing Administration mortgages require mortgage insurance on all loans.

Trump's executive order directs the FHFA to create a pilot program that cancels eligible borrowers' mortgage insurance "proactively" once their home equity reaches a certain threshold, according to the White House. This would apply to mortgages originated after the pilot program launches.

Currently, conventional loan borrowers can generally remove PMI once their loan balance is below 80% of the purchase price or once they have reached 20% equity, according to Fannie Mae. Once your balance reaches 80% of the original value, you can ask your loan servicer if you're eligible to remove PMI, or they may remove it automatically when your balance reaches 78% of the original value.

In a statement Thursday, Seth Appleton, president of U.S. mortgage insurers, an industry trade organization, said: "We commend the President's focus on lowering costs for future homeowners, and the private mortgage insurance industry is proud to already be delivering on that goal."

Typically, the cost of PMI can be 0.2% to 2% of the loan annually, but varies based on your down payment size, credit score, amount borrowed and other factors, according to Fannie Mae.

For example, if you purchased a $600,000 home with a 10% down payment and a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage at 7.3%, your monthly mortgage insurance could be $351, according to a private mortgage calculator from Freddie Mac.

"Most mortgages ... are carrying PMI at this point in time, especially with the way home prices have been and down payments being as small as they've been in recent years," Gumbinger said.

During the second quarter of 2026, the median home down payment was 13.7% of the home's purchase price, or $27,100, according to a September report from Realtor.com.

Trump's executive order also aims to reduce mortgage refinancing costs by making permanent the FHFA's pilot program that waives the lender's title insurance policy and allows "cost-friendly alternatives," according to the White House.

"I don't know that it's necessarily a game-changer in terms of the cost of housing, which is dictated by home price and mortgage rates at the moment," Gumbinger said. "But you know, every little bit certainly helps."

As of Oct. 23, mortgage interest rates for 30-year fixed-rate mortgages were 7.32%, according to Freddie Mac, and refinance demand was down 59% in mid-October, compared with the same week the previous year.

Originally planned through November 2027, the FHFA pilot was "limited in scope," for refinances with a loan-to-value ratio below 80%, in a handful of states, former FHFA director Sandra Thompson said during a congressional hearing in April 2024.

Thompson estimated that consumers could save $500 to $1,500 in closing costs for loans sold under the pilot program.

Making the program permanent could save borrowers because the lender's title insurance policy, which is usually required for new loans and refinances, "has always been fairly pricey," Gumbinger said.

The lender's fees related to title and title insurance generally make up the largest share of mortgage closing costs, according to a 2025 Urban Institute report.

#2068. How Dropbox plans to prevent AI brain rot in its workers

Concerned about its workers losing brainpower as they outsource work to artificial intelligence, Dropbox is considering using cognitive training tools, like memory exercises and attention-span training, to keep them mentally sharp. 

The cloud storage pioneer has also experimented with “analog” offsites, where executives practice breathwork and put aside their electronic devices. 

“We’re incredibly optimistic about [AI’s] impact, but we also believe we have a responsibility to understand how it’s affecting the people who use it every day,” Melanie Rosenwasser, the cloud storage pioneer’s chief people officer, told Fast Company.  

“Brain rot” has emerged as a term to describe the cognitive decline caused by AI and social media, as studies have shown. “That’s why we’re beginning to explore training around brain health and cognitive well-being, while also experimenting with ways to foster critical thinking and human connection,” said Rosenwasser.

While evidence is limited that brain-training exercises improve general intelligence, some research suggests that targeted exercises can produce lasting improvements in specific mental skills. 

A National Institutes of Health-funded study found that training in reasoning and processing speed, for example, produced benefits that lasted up to a decade in older adults.

#2077. Your customer service problem isn’t a customer service problem

A few years ago, one of our team members came down with a big health problem—a splitting headache that just wouldn’t let up. She visited several doctors, who recommended various treatments and medications to alleviate the pain, none of which worked. The pain was so intense that it made it difficult to carry out basic tasks, affecting both her personal and professional lives.

After a few days of nonstop pain, she decided to try a different route and visit a chiropractor. The chiropractor listened, asked probing questions about her headache, then examined her from head to toe—leading to a surprising discovery. As it turned out, our team member’s jaw was out of alignment. The chiropractor proceeded to treat the misalignment, and almost immediately, the pain started to evaporate. The headache, though disruptive, was not the core problem to be solved. Instead, it was a symptom of a problem somewhere else in her body, specifically her misaligned jaw.

Over the years, our agency has been asked by many a customer to help them solve a major brand-related headache—poor customer experience. In much the same way our team member’s headache turned out to be a symptom rather than a root cause, we found in each and every one of these client engagements that the poor customer experience was a symptom of a much bigger problem—poor employee engagement. It wasn’t that the employees of these companies didn’t know what great customer experience looked, smelled, and tasted like. The problem was that they didn’t care enough to deliver that level of service.

The link between employee engagement and customer experience

When it comes to customer experience delivery, engaged employees will always outperform disengaged ones. As Timothy R. Clark, an author and organizational consultant, rightly notes, “Highly engaged employees make the customer experience. Disengaged employees break it.”

There is significant data to back up this claim. Research by Gallup showed that teams scoring in the top quartile on employee engagement enjoyed 10% higher customer loyalty/engagement. And research conducted by the Institute of Customer Service found that every 1% improvement in employee engagement leads to a 0.41 point increase in Customer Satisfaction Index scores. Based on these insights, it is clear that poor customer experience is the headache, while poor employee engagement is the misaligned jaw that needs to be treated.

Treat the cause, not just the symptom

I’ve spent nearly 20 years helping clients build brands that are loved by their employees and customers (in that order). Over that time, I’ve observed frustrated senior leaders with the best intentions trying to improve their poor customer experience delivery by commissioning customer service training, without doing anything about the actual cause of their subpar customer experience. If you want to get rid of the headache of poor customer experience, you have to treat the cause—not just the symptom. This means developing a sturdy employee engagement program that addresses the issue of poor engagement head-on.

Here are the steps I recommend my senior leadership clients take to elevate employee engagement to the same level of importance as other internal programs designed to strengthen organizational performance and ultimately deliver better business outcomes.

1. Make employee engagement a strategic priority

The first step in elevating the importance of employee engagement in your workplace is to make it a strategic priority rather than a “nice to have.” If customer experience is important enough to be discussed at senior leadership meetings or in the boardroom, so is employee engagement. If you don’t track employee engagement scores across the workplace, start doing so—today!

Make improving employee engagement scores a permanent agenda item for your senior leadership or board meetings. Begin treating it as a nonnegotiable priority, giving it the same discipline, attention, and urgency as any other core business metric.

2. Form your engagement unit and assign accountability

Your next step should be to form a team of experts with the skill sets needed to improve your employee engagement, and hold them accountable for doing so.

In modern workplaces, the responsibility of developing a culture where employees are highly engaged lies with Human Resources. However, some companies choose to form an employee engagement team that sits outside of HR on the organizational chart, while others may hire a specialized third-party employee engagement agency to do the heavy lifting. No matter which option you run with, hold your engagement team accountable by setting and documenting clear employee engagement goals, metrics, and key performance indicators for that team to aim for.

3. Specify desired outcomes

The most effective employee engagement programs are specific and measurable. Be sure to clearly articulate and document your employee engagement program’s goals, objectives, and success metrics so that your employee engagement team is aware of what they should be aiming for and how you define success.

4. Let employees cocreate your employee engagement programs

One of the biggest mistakes organizations make is designing employee engagement programs for employees rather than with employees. In doing so, they risk investing time, energy, money, and other resources in programs that don’t address the workplace issues employees actually care about. If employee engagement is the cumulative result of the experiences employees have within the workplace, then employees themselves are uniquely qualified to help identify what is working, what is not, and what needs to change in the workplace. 

Savvy business leaders involve their employees in the design, implementation, and evaluation of engagement initiatives. Empowering employees to cocreate your engagement programs increases the likelihood that employees will embrace those programs and that they’ll make a positive impact on the workplace environment.

5. Measure, monitor & refine

Chances are that as your engagement unit builds out your employee engagement programs, they’ll make some mistakes along the way. Be patient with your engagement team, and accept that some elements of your engagement programs may initially fall short of their desired impact.

With this in mind, be prepared to measure and monitor your employee engagement programs so you can refine and fine-tune them and, in the long run, develop programs that best fit your unique employee engagement needs.

Excellent customer experiences start with excellent employee experiences

If you have a customer experience headache, remember that poor customer experience is a symptom. If you want to eliminate the headache, you need to treat the cause—not just the symptom.

Invest resources in developing programs that engage employees with the same vigor as you engage customers. Then, follow up with customer experience training that your newly engaged employees will appreciate and benefit from. Only then will you see an improvement in your organization’s level of customer experience.

And, in a way, web developers have been made obsolete! These days, if a local bakery or a dentist or an artist needs a website, they're probably not hiring a developer and paying them tens of thousands of dollars to build something from scratch. I'm far from an expert when it comes to LLMs?Large Language Models, the machine learning models that power tools like GPT-4, but I do understand how they work at a high level.

Fundamentally, LLMs are super-powerful text predictors. Given a prompt, they use machine learning to try and come up with the most likely set of characters that follow the prompt.

Companies like OpenAI spend a ton of time and energy tweaking the models to improve the output. An army of human labelers “grade” the model's output, and the model learns and evolves.

If you've experimented with tools like Chat GPT or Bing's AI-powered search, you've probably noticed that the responses are maybe 80% correct, but they're said with absolute and unshakeable confidence.

LLMs aren't able to validate their assumptions, or test their hypotheses. They can't confirm whether what they're saying is true or not. They're playing a probability game, and estimating that this string of characters seems to be compatible with the string of characters from the prompt.

Sometimes, parts of that response are nonsensical. The OpenAI team refers to these as “hallucinations”.

As the technology improves, we can expect some of the rough edges to be sanded down, but fundamentally, there will always be some level of inaccuracy. These tools just don't have any mechanism to objectively validate their responses.

And so, the accuracy will improve, but it'll never be perfect. And that's a problem, if we're talking about making web developers obsolete. If you're not a programmer, you won't be able to tell which parts are accurate or not. You won't be able to spot the hallucinations.

But wait, in the GPT-4 demo, we saw how the AI can fix itself! Copy/paste the error message, and it'll find and fix the problem.

But hm, not all hallucinations will lead to explicit errors. For example, I recently used GPT-4 to generate a component using React, and while the output was surprisingly good, it still made a few accessibility mistakes. The person creating the app might not notice these problems, but the end users sure will!

Accessibility is just one example. What about security vulnerabilities in the code? Who is liable when things go horribly wrong?

AI isn't magic. It's only as good as its training data. Code snippets are all over the internet, and are often generic. By contrast, every codebase is unique. There are very few large open-source codebases. How's the AI supposed to learn how to build big real-world projects?

We're very quickly reaching the point where non-developers can sit down with a chatbot and crank out a small self-contained project, the sort of thing that folks currently use tools like Webflow to build. And that's awesome!

This is a really interesting use case to me. Essentially, ChatGPT is like a pair programmer, someone who can help you make sense of things you don't understand. You can ask it specific questions and get specific answers.

But I do think you need to be careful. There's a right way and a wrong way to use tools like this to help you learn.

The wrong way would be to treat it like GPS navigation. When I have to drive somewhere, I pop the address into my GPS, and follow its instructions indiscriminately. I do generally wind up where I need to go, but it requires 0 mental effort on my part. As a result, my sense of direction has totally atrophied. I can't go anywhere now without a synthesized voice telling me where to turn. 😬

Instead of treating it like a GPS, I'd suggest treating it like you're a member of a jury, and the LLM is a witness called by the prosecutor, taking the stand.

You'll listen to what they have to say, but you won't accept it as fact. You'll be skeptical, and think critically about every word.

Instead of blindly copy/pasting the code that ChatGPT generates, go through it line-by-line, and make sure you understand. Ask it for clarification. And double-check things that seem suspicious with an authoritative source (eg. the official documentation). Keep in mind that LLMs are 100% confident, but not 100% accurate.

If you follow this strategy, I think LLMs can offer a lot of value. 😄

#2095. From zeitgeist to zeitghost: How Netflix kept a hold on viewers—but lost its spot in pop culture

Netflix has been pumping out original content for so long and at such an immense scale that it recently released its second original movie entitled War Machine.

Although the Brad Pitt-starring first iteration sank like a stone in 2017, the unrelated new War Machine, led by Reacher’s Alan Ritchson, was reportedly a ratings smash, hitting 10th on Netflix’s all-time most-watched movies list, with 140 million views. So far, it’s the platform’s biggest movie this year.

If the dearth of online chatter around it is any indication, though, it’s gotten all too easy to have zero interest in or opinions about Netflix’s biggest projects.

Presumably, very few people got yelled at on social media for sharing War Machine spoilers, or felt slighted by a significant other for watching it without them. By all accounts, it’s a pretty good movie, but far from what could be considered an “event,” despite a sheen of event-ness being a reasonable expectation for any studio’s biggest film of the year.

Instead, it feels more like a movie designed with the goal of capturing a Super Bowl number of eyeballs. That’s the main problem with 2020s Netflix: It gets eyeballs watching far more than it gets tongues wagging. 

If a Super Bowl-size event takes place and nobody talks about it, does it even matter?

To be clear, Netflix is still an apex predator in the streaming wars (although it may be dwarfed by the world’s true streaming leader, YouTube). At the end of last year, Netflix topped 325 million subscribers, and in the first half of 2026, it logged 97.7 billion hours watched, landing 11 titles on Nielsen’s top 20 original series.

But just because a lot of people still watch Netflix doesn’t mean they like what they’re seeing.

While revenue was up 12% during its third quarter, engagement is estimated to be down. According to a Jefferies analysis cited by Reuters, average monthly viewing time per member declined from 66 hours in the first half of 2023 to 49 hours in the first half of 2026.

It’s as clear an indication as any that Netflix has drifted away from the center of pop culture.

Between an overabundance of streaming options—and the fact that they’re all competing against the likes of TikTok and other attention-seekers—it’s tempting to posit there’s no longer any such thing as a zeitgeist hit. But only sometimes.

Eventually, something like HBO’s heart-palpitating The Pitt or Apple TV’s horror-comedy Widow’s Bay emerges as a word-of-mouth sensation that sweeps the Emmys, proving it’s still entirely possible to make movies and shows that put pop culture in a choke hold. Netflix is just no longer as efficient as it once was at developing them.

While the zeitgeist remains intact, Netflix is now in danger of becoming a zeitghost.

Quantity over quality

One of the major issues affecting the platform is too much signal and not enough noise. 

Just last year, Netflix released the anime film K-Pop Demon Hunters, a bona fide phenomenon, along with the timely chiller Adolescence, a must-see limited series, but those were only 2 of the 597 original movies and series that Netflix reportedly released in 2025—and it’s hard to think of something comparatively popular that the streamer has put out since.

The quirky melodrama Remarkably Bright Creatures won some Emmys, and a just-released, Florence Pugh-led adaptation of East of Eden made a splash.

But they’re sharing crowded Netflix menu space with Sacha Baron Cohen’s dud romcom Ladies First, two adaptations of Harlan Coben novels, and star vehicles for each of Adam Sandler’s daughters, all of which will be joined later this year by reality game show Squid Game: The VIP Challenge, featuring Zac Efron’s younger brother, Dylan.

It’s as if Netflix has taken a little too literally the idea of making something for everybody.

It wasn’t always this way. When Netflix first emerged as a player and innovator in original content in 2013, it came out swinging.

Not only were House of Cards and Orange Is the New Black both powerhouses—the kinds of shows people ask whether you’ve seen yet—but they also comprised two of only three new series Netflix released that year. (Eli Roth’s long-forgotten Hemlock Grove was the third.)

It may not be fair to expect Netflix to retain the same 66% hit rate when it puts out 597 new projects in a year; however, Netflix was under no legal obligation to churn out that many new projects.

At what was arguably its cultural peak in 2019, Netflix released a relatively slight 371 new originals, among them The Witcher, Sex Education, and The Umbrella Academy, not to mention Ava DuVernay’s When They See Us, Martin Scorsese’s The Irishman, and Noah Baumbach’s Marriage Story.

Back then, that level of quantity was a dramatic escalation.

While Netflix has slowed down on original output in the years since its peak, its closest competitors dabble in the dozens instead of hundreds. Their market share may be nowhere near Netflix’s, but their hit-to-miss ratios are comparatively off the charts.

Breadth over depth

Following reports of a subscriber drop in April 2022, Netflix posted its largest one-day plunge in stocks in the company’s entire history, shedding $54 billion in market cap.

To right the ship, Netflix cracked down on password-sharing, introduced ad-supported tiers, and stopped pushing the limits of how much original content one streamer can possibly produce in a calendar year.  

As it began stabilizing subscriber growth and accelerating profits, though, Netflix also embarked on a different form of expansion—instead of more original content, more kinds of content.

Starting in 2023, Netflix branched out into live events with Chris Rock’s special Selective Outrage.

Later that year, it got into the live sports business, with The Netflix Cup, followed by a series of similar events and, eventually, a $5 billion deal to become the home of WWE Raw through 2035.

Finally, earlier this year the streamer began airing a wide range of well-established video podcasts, or “talk shows” as they were once known.

With all this expansion, Netflix increased the breadth of reasons to subscribe—while weakening its earlier promise as a hub for buzzy movies and TV shows.

It feels almost like a slap in the face to scroll through a vast menu of seemingly interchangeable new Netflix movies and shows, only for those options to be surrounded by a blizzard of podcasts, sports events, and The Roast of Theo Von. (That last one is not a real thing. Yet.) 

Throwing a bunch of stuff at the wall to see what sticks mostly just leaves the thrower with a ridiculously messy wall, and that’s what Netflix looks like now. Instead of reinventing the maximalism of linear cable, perhaps the streamer should try to reinvent the hit-rate of 2013 Netflix.

It certainly seems to have worked for Apple TV, whose tightly curated slate keeps producing prestigious smashes like Pluribus—and exponentially less filler.

Not only did Apple outperform Netflix at the last two Emmys, Apple led all network and streaming services for the first time this year. It scored 29 total wins, including 14 for Widow’s Bay, breaking the record for most-winning freshman comedy, which Apple previously broke in 2025 with 13 wins for The Studio.

Whether Apple’s success is a direct result of its less-is-more strategy or not, it reflects poorly on Netflix-style excess.

Burn and churn

It’s unfortunate enough that Netflix now encompasses everything, but it also feels permanently on the edge of becoming anything.

Of all the explanations for the recent phenomenon of viewers not returning for a second seasons of hit Netflix shows, one of the more plausible ones is that Netflix’s itchy trigger finger for cancellations has trained viewers to not get too attached to new series.

Barring vanishingly rare exceptions like the juggernaut of Bridgerton, most shows on Netflix feel ephemeral and thus skippable. That transient nature is starting to bleed over to the talent behind its programming, along with Netflix’s staff and its C-suite.

Over the last few years, Netflix has reportedly lost out on renewals for its deals with Dahmer creator Ryan Murphy, Stranger Things’s Duffer Brothers, and, most recently, director David Fincher—who made three movies with Netflix over the past six years.

It must also sting that after The Haunting of Hill House creator Mike Flanagan left in 2022, his first post-Netflix series, the just-released and much-discussed Carrie, already appears to be a sizable hit for Prime Video.

As for in-house turnover, Netflix laid off 1,000 employees last week, its largest workforce reduction yet, and this week the company fired film chief Dan Lin, director of original series Carolina Garcia, and content chief Bela Bajaria.

Perhaps these exits could be read as a sign that Netflix is aware of having missed the mark with its movies and TV shows recently. On the surface, though, it just scans as instability, suggesting these executives’ replacements have an impossible job ahead.

It’s anyone’s guess what the next iteration of Netflix will look like, but the 2026 version is overstuffed, strategically unmoored, and completely illegible from a branding perspective.

What has long been considered the most essential streamer is starting to feel increasingly optional—a membership to dip in and out of when one of its hundreds of annual original projects merits it.

If Netflix wants to be more than just a profitable quagmire again, it will have to go back to winning viewers’ hearts instead of capturing their eyeballs.

#2097. I paid off $11,000 of credit card debt in 18 months while still traveling, shopping and living my life

Yes, I am a personal finance reporter with over seven years of experience telling readers how to wisely manage their money. Yes, I've also been in credit card debt for basically all of that time.

I have carried a balance on one or more credit cards for nearly all of the 10 years I have had credit cards. Sometimes, especially in the early years when I was still a college student, I did not have the cash on hand to pay it all off. Most of the time, however, I just didn't have the discipline.

I was making more than the minimum payment every month, but I also felt like credit card debt was normal so I didn't make a concerted effort to fully clear my balance. I assumed when my peers were putting things on credit, they too, weren't paying it off right away, though many of them actually were.

I did get my cards paid off for a brief period in 2020 when I put my Covid-19 stimulus checks toward my debt. But then I got a dog, moved into my own apartment and continued traveling and spending at levels I felt were manageable. Soon I was carrying a balance again and that balance gradually grew to a peak of $11,310 in March 2025.

But as of Sept. 18, I am credit card debt-free. Here's how I racked up five figures of debt and paid it all off in under two years.

My debt came from a mix of genuine necessities and trivial pursuits. I charged vet bills and supplies for my dog but also flights and hotels for trips whenever I felt like I could afford them, and treated myself to dinner or a new outfit admittedly too often. Instead of intentionally making payments to bring down the balance, I'd often use so-called "girl math" to justify my continued card usage. I'd make a mental note of whatever I spent when I swiped my card, then pay back what I thought was more than the total I'd spent — without actually keeping track or paying much attention to the interest adding up.

For example, in October 2024, I spent $764 on one of my cards on dog-sitting services, a hotel and dining out, and I made payments totaling $838, which seemed like a net positive. But when you factor in the $60 I was charged in interest during that period, it was as if I'd hardly made a payment at all.

Despite the months I paid the credit card company more than I spent, an interest rate of at least 21% made my balance continue to grow. My strategy was purely vibes-based, just spending when I wanted to then throwing a chunk of money from my paycheck at my balance every other week. I didn't really sit down and look at the numbers to see how much I was moving my balance each month — or in which direction it was headed.

The end of 2024 and beginning of 2025 got particularly spend-heavy as I went on several trips and attended my best friend's wedding. I got a bit carried away, and my balance surpassed $10,000. Though I checked my account regularly, it didn't really occur to me how dangerous it was to carry that much debt. If I'd lost my job for a period or had some expensive emergency at that time, I could have struggled to keep up with even a minimum payment or meet my other financial obligations.

Thankfully, that didn't happen. I could still manage all my regular monthly bills including my rent and student loan payment. I was still contributing to my 401(k) and some emergency savings. Chipping away at my credit card debt felt like just another one of those obligations, not something that was actively holding me back.

I started dating my now-fiancé in early 2025, and he kindly helped me realize how significant this level of debt was, even if it's not all that uncommon. Around 45% of credit cardholders have carried a balance at least one month in the last year, according to LendingTree's analysis of Federal Reserve data. Americans with any kind of debt have an average credit card balance of $7,756 as of the first quarter of 2026, per LendingTree's analysis of over 400,000 of the company's users.

I sat down with my budgeting spreadsheet and crunched the numbers. Pulling up my monthly credit card statements, I looked at the net payments I was making on the debt each month and was fairly surprised to see how many months I was actually adding to the balance instead of bringing it down, even slowly.

There are plenty of tactics people use to address credit card debt, from putting their cards in the freezer to committing to "no-spend" periods. I am fortunate enough that I didn't have to take any kind of drastic measure when I got serious about paying off my debt.

I did cut back on some spending areas, mainly dining out and shopping for clothes. Living in New York, it's easy to drop $100 on dinner or drinks with friends. I said yes to fewer nights out, and when I went out to dinner, I would skip the $15 cocktail, for example. I stopped Ubering home as frequently as I used to, saving another $40 or so when I did go out.

I can be a very impulsive shopper when it comes to clothes, so I got more intentional about pre-selecting items and waiting before buying to make sure I really needed or wanted them. Instead of going to Target and mindlessly adding things to my cart, I'd make online orders so I only got what I needed. I made a great effort to save for trips ahead of time and paid those charges off right away instead of over several months.

And instead of just throwing big chunks of money at my debt hoping it would exceed whatever I spent in a month, I actually started putting my monthly statement balance in a spreadsheet. This way, I couldn't deny the months I added to my balance, despite still making payments. I moved in May 2025, so that month, for example, my balance went up slightly as I paid movers instead of putting extra cash toward my debt.

For the most part, I would use my debit card for purchases, but I didn't cut myself off from my credit card altogether.

I took the classic budgeting advice of "paying myself first" to work down my credit card balance while also saving some for emergencies. On my biweekly pay days, I made a payment on my credit card balance and had an automatic contribution to my emergency savings fund. Then I'd use whatever was left in my checking account for groceries and other necessities. I'd often make an extra payment the weeks I didn't get paid, too, if I had extra cash after those essentials. From October 2025 through September 2026, I've managed to throw nearly $600 a month, on average, at my debt.

That emergency fund proved especially handy in May 2026 when my dog had a very expensive vet visit.

It's something I write about often — how it's smart to keep emergency savings even while you're paying down debt — but actually going through it was a good learning experience. It kind of hurt to have to tap into the cash savings I'd been really proud of building. But in the end, I was glad I didn't just have to add the $6,000 vet bill to my credit card debt. It slowed down my progress a bit that month, but ultimately didn't derail me. And now that I'm not putting $500 toward credit card debt every month, I can focus on rebuilding my savings and other financial goals.

Personal finance columnist Michelle Singletary recently told me "Debt limits your possibilities." I didn't really feel that way when I was on a vacation that I charged to my card. But as I recently turned 30, started planning my wedding and thinking about my future, I started feeling like my debt payment is a bill I don't need or want to have to pay every month. And I feel lighter without it.
