#901. France: Young voters blase about Glucksmann's Socialists

Raphael Glucksmann wants to lead France's Socialists to power without working with the left-populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, who is particularly popular with young voters. How can the center-left candidate win them over?

The historically working-class Parisian neighborhood of Belleville has gentrified in recent years. It was here that Raphael Glucksmann, a member of the European Parliament for France's center-left Socialists, celebrated his victory in the party's presidential primary on October 11.

Two rounds of voting had been anticipated, but the 46-year-old son of philosopher and essayist Andre Glucksmann triumphed in the first round with a convincing 66% of the vote and will be the Socialist candidate in France's 2027 presidential election.

As he left the Cantine de Belleville restaurant, Glucksmann was flour-bombed by an unidentified assailant. The following morning, he called on his rival, the left-wing French populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, to condemn attacks carried out by his supporters, though it is not clear that the attacker was indeed a supporter of Melenchon's La France Insoumise (LFI).

The incident has highlighted the issue set to dog Glucksmann's presidential candidacy over the coming weeks: How can he win over Melenchon's supporters if he refuses to work with their candidate?

"Where do you stand on Melenchon?" That was the question that dominated much of the Socialists' primary campaign. Party leader Olivier Faure spoke in favor of leaving the door open for cooperation with the LFI, but Glucksmann called for a clear break with the far left. His decisive victory is therefore also seen as a vote in favor of a clear demarcation.

The other primary candidates questioned Glucksmann's political experience. Segolene Royal, a previous presidential candidate, compared his bid for the Elysee with that of an "intern" wanting to take charge of a company. She said Glucksmann had no experience in government or even running a local authority.

Despite Glucksmann's victory in the primary, his starting position is a difficult one. A poll by Toluna Harris Interactive, published the day after his win, projects that he would win 10–11% of the vote. The same poll put Melenchon on 18%, meaning the far-left man would be much more likely to reach the runoff. But the survey was conducted before Glucksmann won the primary, and so does not take into account any potential boost the nomination may give him. Still, 10% would be an improvement on the Socialists' performance in the last presidential election in 2022, when their candidate Anne Hidalgo won just 1.75% of the national vote.

There is also the complication that the Socialists are banking on a man who is not even a party member to lead them from irrelevance. Raphael Glucksmann actually belongs to a small center-left party called Place Publique.

Glucksmann has his work cut out for him — especially with young people. In an Ipsos survey in September, which posited Glucksmann as the social-democratic candidate and Edouard Philippe as the only candidate from the political center, Melenchon scored 41% among the under-25s. Meanwhile, 20% of this age group supported the far-right nationalist Marine Le Pen. France's young electorate is politically divided, but Melenchon still dominates on the left.

The 75-year-old is currently rallying alongside the students whose protests have drawn international attention. At a rally near Clermont-Ferrand in central France, he expressed solidarity with their movement and reiterated his calls for lowering the voting age to 16 and abolishing Parcoursup, France's national admissions procedure for higher education.

In an analysis for the Fondation Jean-Jaures, a Socialist-aligned think tank, the political scientist Antoine Bristielle distinguishes between a core group of committed LFI voters and the broader left. Melenchon's most loyal supporters, he says, are often young, educated city-dwellers, and many are under financial pressure. Four in 10 households in this group live on a net monthly income of less than €2,000 ($2,200).

Bristielle estimates that just under a third of the left-wing electorate in his study care more about a left-wing victory than their allegiance to any particular party. Almost half of the people in this group are younger than 35 and would consider voting for the Socialists, the Greens or LFI. Redistribution and an active government are important to them, but most would prefer politicians who are willing to compromise.

The pattern recently observed in Germany, where young women are more likely to vote for the left and young men for the right, does not apply in France. Political scientists Anja Durovic, Nonna Mayer and Noemie Piolat found that in the 2024 European and parliamentary elections, young French women were not significantly more likely to vote for the left, or less likely to vote for the far right, than men of the same age. It remains to be seen whether this pattern will be replicated in the 2027 presidential election.

Glucksmann says he would ease the burden on earned income by reducing France's CSG social security tax while increasing taxes on inheritances worth more than €2 million. He also presents himself as a committed supporter of European integration, while Melenchon has called the EU a neoliberal institution. Glucksmann's political profile combines social policy demands with foreign policy positions that could also appeal to voters from the camp of the current president, Emmanuel Macron.

France's Ukraine policy is one such issue. Glucksmann favors decisive support for Kyiv. Manon Aubry, a member of the European Parliament for Melenchon's LFI, says her party supports military assistance only under certain conditions, namely: The provision of aid to Ukraine must first be subject to democratic debate; it must not enable attacks deep inside Russian territory; and it must not weaken France's own defense capabilities. In 2024, LFI MPs voted against the French–Ukrainian security agreement.

The tension between the parties of the left was once again apparent in the aftermath of the flour attack in Belleville. The LFI MP Sebastien Delogu posted a declaration of support on X for the taxi driver who was going to have to clean his car.

Glucksmann must win over a lot of voters from Melenchon's camp if he is to have a chance of winning the presidency. This is likely to prove a much more difficult task than simply distancing himself from Melenchon.

#905. Serbia’s Aleksandar Vucic concedes defeat in historic election win for student protest movement

Serbian Progressive Party prime ministerial candidate Aleksandar Vucic reacts after conceding defeat during a press conference on Oct 25 at the party headquarters in Belgrade, Serbia.

BELGRADE – Serbia’s Aleksandar Vucic conceded defeat to a student-led protest movement in a historic election on Oct 25 that ends his long grip on power and heralds a new political era in the Balkan country.

Vucic, a populist and nationalist, has dominated Serbian politics as prime minister or president since 2014.

But he called snap elections in September after almost two years of student-led demonstrations against perceived corruption and creeping authoritarianism triggered by the deaths of 16 people when a train station canopy collapsed in the city of Novi Sad in 2024.

“It is clear that we have lost these elections, the Student List has won,” Vucic said in a televised speech.

Within moments of his announcement, the streets of Belgrade erupted in celebration. Tens of thousands of people thronged along the city’s main boulevards, with cars honking, people dancing and students shouting “victory” and “he’s done”.

The students’ win marks a profound political shift in the country of some six million people and is reminiscent of the defeat of Hungary’s Viktor Orban in April.

It may also offer a blueprint for other student movements across Europe, which have taken to the streets in recent weeks to protest, in some cases violently, against poor education standards and a lack of prospects.

Vucic said the students would have between 135 and 140 seats in the 250-seat Parliament and that his Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) would have 105, based on preliminary results.

“Dear Serbia, tonight the voice of the people is being heard,” said Radomir Sajcic, a professor at Belgrade University, speaking on behalf of the students at their headquarters.

“Tonight we celebrate victory, tomorrow we build the future.”

European leaders and officials congratulated the Student List on its victory, among them President of the European Commission Ursula von der Leyen and European Commissioner for Enlargement Marta Kos.

“We stand ready to... work together to advance Serbia’s future in the European Union,” von der Leyen wrote on X.

Hungarian Prime Minister Peter Magyar assured the students of his country’s support.

Lines snaked from polling stations in cities and towns across the country earlier in the day, and even Vucic waited for nearly two hours to vote in Belgrade. Pollsters and the election commission put turnout close to 70%, the highest since the break-up of Yugoslavia in 1992.

“(The turnout) did not surprise me because there was a large and strong mass that has formed throughout the past two years,” said voter Katarina Petrovic.

Vucic, who resigned from his second term as president to run again for prime minister, was a strong incumbent and few in the student protest movement dared believe he would concede so fast.

Supporters credit him with modernising Serbia’s economy and building new highways, railways, schools and hospitals.

“Some businessmen, some people will leave us but our structure is firm, we are very united, very strong, we are used to being in the opposition,” Vucic said.

Much remains unclear about how the student-backed candidates will fare.

Their list of 250 largely unknown candidates includes university professors, actors and farmers, and is led by cardiologist Ilija Srdanovic, who was in tears after Vucic’s announcement.

However, it is unclear who will be prime minister and enact the students’ domestic agenda, which aims to root out graft and improve schooling.

Political analysts predict some kind of purge of Vucic allies in the police, security services and ministries. But the students have been quieter on their international ambitions.

As Vucic did for years, they will have to juggle relations with China, a major investor, Russia, Serbia’s traditional ally and principal supplier of natural gas, and the European Union, Serbia’s largest trading partner.

“The biggest uncertainty is... who is going to lead the government, who will be the key ministers, and how they will operate based on the promises they made to fight corruption, to reintroduce the rule of law, but also to re-establish Serbia’s position in foreign policy,” said Radivoje Grujic, a Warsaw-based election consultant.

The student-led street protests sparked by the station disaster started small but quickly became the biggest in a generation, calling for Vucic to step down. Rights groups and activists say repression worsened in the period since.

Vucic and the SNS repeatedly denied accusations of repression, corruption and spying on their opponents. He has said the students are funded by foreign agents in Croatia and Montenegro, a charge the students dismiss.

Meanwhile, on the streets of Belgrade, a traditional brass band struck up, and a group including a man in a “students are champions” jumper floated balloons in the shape of the number three, representing the number of the students’ list of candidates.

“We will drink a beer to celebrate, we will get drunk, we are so happy, I have no words to describe how much,” said Andjela, a student who was with a group of friends.

#911. Is Germany's eight-hour workday under threat?

Would you work 12 hours straight? The CDU/CSU and SPD are locked in a dispute over reforms to the Working Hours Act. Workplace flexibility, collective bargaining agreements and worker protection rules are at stake.

The town of Leuna, in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt, is one of the country's largest chemical industry hub, where more than 100 companies have established operations. Among them is the Finnish biotechnology group UPM, which has recently built a biorefinery for wood-based chemicals in Leuna. It's now hiring.

One of the job listings is for a chemical technician to oversee the rollout of new facilities. But to qualify, candidates must be willing to work 12-hour shifts. Is that legal? According to German law, standard working hours are limited to eight hours per day. It is permissible to extend this to ten hours, provided that workers receive compensatory time off for the overtime, and there must be at least 11 hours of rest between two shifts.

Business associations and the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU) parties have long called for these rules to be made more flexible.

Rainer Dulger, president of the Confederation of German Employers' Associations (BDA), points to the European Working Time Directive, which allows for a maximum weekly working time of 48 hours. "The allowance is being taken advantage of almost everywhere else — except here," Dulger told the dpa news agency in August.

Companies say they often find the existing rules too rigid, particularly in industries with seasonal fluctuations — such as tourism — or in project-based work, or during irregular peaks in workload, such as in the restaurant industry. They could deploy their staff more efficiently, says Dulger: "The Working Hours Act dates back to the era of fax machines and rotary-dial phones. It knows nothing of laptops or daycare hours."

In their 2025 coalition agreement, the CDU/CSU and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) agreed to reform the Working Hours Act. "In keeping with the European Working Time Directive, we want to allow for a maximum weekly rather than daily working time — also and especially to promote a better work-life balance," the agreement said.

The SPD would have preferred to leave the law unchanged, but had to accept the conservative parties' plans. The Social Democrats pointed out that longer working hours are already permitted in exceptional cases, such as in emergency services, the health care sector and security services. Workers in power plants, refineries and the chemical industry are also allowed to work up to 12 hours at a time.

Even Chancellor Friedrich Merz himself seemed unaware of this exception until quite recently. After visiting the UPM refinery in Leuna in September, he recounted to business leaders at the German Employers' Day in Berlin that he was "completely surprised" to learn that the facilities operate on a continuous 12-hour shift schedule, thanks to the approval of the works council.

Merz recounted the shift supervisor describing the work schedule as "really great" — with three-day work weeks. "He said we have plenty of time for our families — it's a great way to organize things." Merz went on to conclude that this demonstrates the benefits of relaxing the Working Hours Act.

"Working more than eight hours is not exploitation. Rather, it can be a good opportunity to organize working hours more flexibly — both for businesses and for the people working there," the chancellor said. "We should do it."

But there is resistance from within the SPD and from trade unions opposed to amending the Working Hours Act. The trade union Verdi argues that significantly longer workdays would have negative consequences for employees' health and would disadvantage people with family and caregiving responsibilities. In certain industries, employees could also be put under pressure to accept longer shifts.

An initial reform draft was drawn up in June 2026 by the Labor and Social Affairs Ministry, headed at the time by Bärbel Bas, an SPD politician who has since resigned for personal reasons.

The draft includes some conditions, including a collective bargaining agreement between employers and unions if any schedule changes were made, as well as health and safety regulations, and working hours to be tracked electronically.

Annika Klose, spokesperson for labor and social policy for the SPD parliamentary group, explained the thinking behind the idea. Flexibility, she said, is "not a one-way street." The coalition agreement explicitly aims to promote a better balance between family and work. "So that it's sometimes possible to take your child to football practice in the afternoon and then continue working afterward — but not that my boss tells me against my will: 'You're working the shift from 9 a.m. to 10 p.m. tomorrow.'"

The CDU/CSU immediately rejected the draft. Businesses were also up in arms. "This draft must be withdrawn and completely revised," demanded Dulger in a statement, saying it was "steeped in mistrust." The German Confederation of Skilled Crafts (ZDH) spoke of a "breach of the commitments agreed to in the coalition agreement." The reform has been on hold ever since.

At the Employers' Day event, Merz held out the prospect of progress. "This is indeed long overdue. I believe we can accomplish this relatively quickly and create flexibility in the labor market."

It remains to be seen, however, whether the new SPD Labor Minister, Alexander Schweitzer, shares this view. In early October, his party released a paper on reforming working hours that read like a declaration of war against the CDU. "Self-determination, Not Deregulation" is the title of the seven-page document that was signed by the SPD working group on labor, the youth organization Jusos, the "60plus" working group and the women's wing of the SPD.

In the paper, the Social Democrats not only reiterate the points that already appeared in Bas' draft back in June. They also demand that, in the future, employers and employees should decide on working hours "jointly and by mutual agreement." They argued that it is not "in the spirit of a modern reform of working time law" if only employers can determine when employees finish work.

From a political perspective, the dispute over reforming work hours has long become more than just a debate over legal provisions. It is a fundamental dispute over how much flexibility workers should be expected to tolerate in the future and to what extent the state should protect them. And it is yet another stumbling block for the already beleaguered government in Berlin.

#914. This must be West Adams

With Victorian mansions and Craftsman bungalows tucked off its boulevard, driving down West Adams can feel like you’re traveling back in time. Many of the core structures were built between the 1880s and 1910s, making the South L.A. neighborhood one of the oldest in the city. But pops of vibrant street art bring you back to the present, like David Flores’ pensive portrait of Bob Marley set against crimson poppies or the mural of a little girl in a backward cap who holds her hand up in a “W” sign, a nod to the West Coast.

Get to know Los Angeles through the places that bring it to life. From restaurants to shops to outdoor spaces, here’s what to discover now.

“It’s both a historic neighborhood and one that is in transition,” said Ryan Wilson, co-founder of West Adams’ private co-working club the Gathering Spot. “There are a lot of things that are emerging.”

Founded by wealthy Pasadena industrialists Henry E. Huntington and Hulett C. Merritt, West Adams’ first denizens were of similar economic stature. But as Beverly Hills and other Westside neighborhoods were developed, well-to-do white residents were persuaded to resettle in those areas.

Affluent Black Angelenos took their place, including architect Paul R. Williams, who designed the nearby headquarters of the Golden State Mutual Life Insurance Co. — once the largest Black-owned insurance company in the West — as well as more than 2,000 private homes, with celebrity clients like Frank Sinatra and Lucille Ball. Oscar-winning actor Hattie McDaniels, blues musician Ray Charles and attorney Johnnie Cochran also had homes in the neighborhood that was then known as Sugar Hill.

In the 1950s, the expansion of the 10 freeway fractured a core section of West Adams, leading to the demolition of numerous homes through eminent domain. A second exodus left the area somewhat neglected.

More recently, the neighborhood’s relative affordability has made it a target for developers. In response, longtime residents are stepping up to protect the legacy of the majority Black and Latino neighborhood.

In 2020, siblings Jasmine and Giovanni Maldonado, who grew up in West Adams, began hosting the monthly pop-up Midcity Mercado as a way to remain in the neighborhood and combat ongoing gentrification and displacement. Taking place in the parking lot that Jasmine’s retail store Persona shares with her mother’s long-running restaurant Mariscos Marias, the family-friendly market features more than 30 rotating BIPOC vendors.

“Midcity Mercado is a celebration of survival,” Maldonado said. “It’s a really beautiful way to share our perspectives and our stories. It unites us in a way where we can appreciate the change and at the same time celebrate what once was.”

And even though the neighborhood is changing, there’s still plenty of history to explore. You can visit one of 70 sites recognized as cultural landmarks, such as UCLA’s William Andrews Clark Memorial Library, or meditate in the verdant gardens of an Italian Renaissance mansion. The neighborhood also has long-standing favorites for Oaxacan food and Creole cuisine; a community-oriented bookstore; a cannabis-friendly ceramics studio; special-occasion-worthy Japanese dining and more.

If you think you know West Adams, it’s worth taking another look.

Anyone who’s lived in a major metropolis can tell you that neighborhoods are a tricky thing. They’re eternally malleable and evoke sociological questions around how we place our homes, our neighbors and our communities within a wider tapestry. In the name of neighborly generosity, we may include gems that linger outside of technical parameters. #920. How reliant is Ukraine on the US-controlled Starlink internet service?

Kyiv keeps issuing warnings that the satellite internet system Starlink is being restricted in Ukraine. How dependent is the country on the system, and what would happen if it went down?

Few technological systems have played such an important role in the war in Ukraine as Starlink, central to the communications, reconnaissance and drone operations of the Ukrainian armed forces. There are currently widespread concerns that Elon Musk's satellite internet service in Ukraine may be restricted or even switched off. In recent days, Kyiv has repeatedly reported disruptions and restrictions affecting its use.

Since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, Ukraine has made extensive use of the satellite internet service, which is operated by the US company SpaceX. With it, military personell can continue to communicate and exchange data, even when infrastructure has been destroyed or cellphone networks are down.

According to the Center for Security Studies (CSS) at ETH Zurich, around 50,000 Starlink terminals were in use in Ukraine in 2025. These are the system's specialised receiving and transmitting devices, which enable communications on the battlefield. They are also used to relay orders between command levels and receive drone footage.

The importance of the Starlink network can in part be explained by events at the beginning of the war. According to a CSS analysis, the KA-SAT satellite system previously favored by Ukraine was targeted in a cyberattack just hours before Russia's invasion. Starlink quickly became a vital alternative.

When discussing current affairs, the terms "Starlink" and "Starshield" are often used interchangeably.

#950. China's influence in the gaming industry is growing

Home to more than 700 million gamers, China is the world's largest gaming market — but it's also one of the most tightly regulated. The effects reach far beyond China, impacting players around the world.

In a dark underground hideout, dim light spills from a handful of oil lamps. A comrade fiddles with a rifle. A red flag hangs on one of the roughly hewn stone walls, bearing a hammer and sickle on a white star — the symbol of the ChineseWorkers' and Peasants' Red Army. Weapons are scattered on the ground. One is picked up, and soon the action moves into the snowy forest, where gunfire erupts among the trees.

Such a scene could fit in a new installment of "Call of Duty," the popular first-person shooter game that transports players to battlefields past, present, and future. But it is actually from "The Defiant." The game is currently being developed by the Chinese studio Hoothanes Technology in Hangzhou. It tells the story of Chinese forces fending off the Japanese army in the 1930s and 1940s.

Developer Connor Xu tells DW the weapons featured in the game are unique. They were used only in Asia and are therefore unfamiliar to many Western players, he says. The same goes for the story, which Xu says has never before been told in a video game.

"The Defiant" is also designed to appeal to an international audience. In just a few years, China has emerged as a major player in the global gaming industry despite strict regulations, censorship and a former ban on video game consoles.

More than 700 million people in China regularly play video games. According to market research company Newzoo, most consider gaming an important part of their lives. Many use games to stay in touch with friends.

"Games make life more interesting," developer Xu says. "I can try different roles, different stories, different characters, because we only have one life to live, but we can play a role in another story. That's really attractive, I think, to Chinese users."

People in China primarily play games on PCs and smartphones, while consoles are much less common. There are historical reasons for that: While gamers in Europe and North America debated whether PlayStation or Xbox was the better console in the early 2000s, the devices were not officially sold in China between 2000 and 2014. Chinese authorities said the restrictions were intended to protect children and teenagers from becoming addicted to gaming.

Minors in China are currently allowed to play online games for no more than three hours a week. There are no such limits for adults, but gaming is subject to strict controls. Anyone who wants to play must register using their real name and a personal identification number. That effectively allows the state to track who plays what and when, as well as whom they communicate with.

There is also a gray area that the Chinese government appears to tolerate. Some players log in using VPNs, giving them access to games that are not officially available in China. Some younger players use their parents' accounts to get around the limits on gaming time.

Despite its strict controls on underage gamers, China has become increasingly receptive to video games in general, having recognized the industry's enormous economic potential. Early on, games released for Chinese players often appeared to be copies of Western titles. At the same time, Chinese tech companies such as Tencent and NetEase invested in numerous Western gaming companies.

One distinctive feature of the Chinese market is its licensing system. "Unlike a lot of the other big video games markets in the world, China has a tightly regulated and licensed official video games market," George E. Osborn tells DW. The journalist advises NGOs and governments on video games and spoke at the Gamescom congress. In his book "Power Play," he describes how authoritarian states use video games to pursue political objectives.

The rule for all games in China is simple: No license, no sales. "That system essentially forces developers who want to release their video games to go through multiple layers of approval from China's Ministry of Propaganda, Ministry of Culture," Osborn says.

Games are prohibited from containing material that threatens China's unity, sovereignty or territorial integrity, among other restrictions. Pornographic content and gambling are also banned. Games may not incite crimes that threaten national security or spread information authorities believe could disrupt public order.

"The whole point around this criteria is that it's deliberately designed to be pretty vague," Osborn says. Whether a game violates the rules is a matter of interpretation. Sometimes games are altered even after release. In the 2025 action role-playing game "Wuchang: Fallen Feathers," for example, enemies based on famous Chinese figures from the Ming Dynasty suddenly could no longer be killed. Instead, they would collapse from exhaustion.

The Chinese action role-playing game "Black Myth: Wukong" became an unexpected international hit in 2024. By then, it was clear Chinese developers could do more than cater to the domestic market — they could create successful games for a global audience as well. The game was inspired by the 16th-century Chinese literary classic "Journey to the West."

Osborn sees nothing wrong with bringing Chinese culture to audiences around the world. The question, he says, is how much the state actually intervenes in the games themselves. "I think in general, video game players are actually quite deeply skeptical of these kinds of things. They are generally very good at spotting when someone might be selling to them or someone might be propagandizing. And so I think if it's too obvious, it won't work," he says.

Because of the licensing system, Western developers also have to comply with government requirements if they want to sell their games in China. And they do want to, Osborn says, because the market is too big to ignore. "But I think when you start thinking about the potential impact this has on creative standards around the world, I think it's really important that developers ask themselves the question of what will we or won't we compromise on here?"

He fears the real danger lies in self-censorship. That could lead developers, for example, to leave queer characters out of their games. That would represent a major step backward, he says, because games help many people explore their identities.

China is not the only country where games are modified to comply with national regulations. In Germany, for example, symbols that violate the country's constitution, such as the swastika, may not be shown in games unless the game in question explicitly contributes to the country's culture of remembrance. Depictions of extreme violence also have to be removed from German versions.

Glorifying violence is prohibited in China, too, yet Xu's team is still allowed to develop a war game. How does this add up? Xu explains it this way: "I think in this game, the most important thing is not violence. The most important thing is to tell history."

Xu says the team is committed to delivering a historically accurate product. Whether it succeeds will be for historians to judge.
