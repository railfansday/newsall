Proposition 3 would make an expiring state income tax on high earners permanent, which supporters say is critical to funding schools and healthcare.

Proposition 3 stems from a decision voters made in 2012, amid a budget crisis, to temporarily increase the income tax rate on the top 2% of California earners. In 2016, voters approved another extension through 2030.

Proposition 3 would make the additional levy permanent.

If passed, tax rates for the top 2% of earners would remain as they are. Income brackets rise with inflation, but last year the top 2% of taxpayers earned at least $371,000 for single filers and $743,000 for joint filers.

If rejected, tax rates for the top 2% would fall in 2031 from at least 10.3% to 9.3%.

Fundraising data will be updated regularly until the election.

Money generated by Proposition 3, like the current tax, would flow to education, with 89% going to K-12 schools and 11% to community colleges.

Then, because the state constitution requires minimum levels of spending on education, more dollars are freed up to pay for services including healthcare and the University of California and California State University systems, said Kayla Kitson, a senior analyst with the California Budget and Policy Center.

Proposition 3, like the current high-earners tax, has the added effect of raising the minimum levels of funding required to flow to K-12 schools and community colleges, because of the complicated formulas California uses to set its budget, Kitson said.

If Proposition 3 fails, the state could expect to lose between $5 billion and $15 billion annually in tax revenue starting in 2031, according to estimates from the Legislative Analyst’s Office.

Supporters of the measure include the California Teachers Assn., the California School Nurses Organization, the California State PTA and Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California.

The California Teachers Assn., whose members gathered signatures to put Proposition 3 on the ballot, says if voters reject the measure, public schools could face a 15% budget cut, with 1 in 6 educators at risk of losing their jobs.

“Prop. 3 keeps the wealthiest Californians paying their share so our public schools get the funding our students deserve,” the union says on its website.

The California Taxpayers Assn., the Family Business Assn. of California and the California Hispanic Chambers of Commerce have come out against the measure.

Opponents say the state already has the nation’s highest income, sales and gas taxes and Sacramento needs to stop wasteful spending, rather than make taxes that were supposed to be temporary permanent.

“Californians shouldn’t have to bail out politicians and special interests’ mismanagement,” an opposition website sponsored by the California Taxpayers Assn. says. “Waste, fraud and overspending by the state government is not an excuse to raise our taxes even higher.”

#403. How Macron Destabilized France

Last weekend, mass riots erupted across France after weeks of protests by lycée students over underfunding and poor conditions in their schools. As the demonstrations grew, they expanded to include other groups, including those upset with the cost of living and the Presidency of Emmanuel Macron, and rioters looting property and setting fires around Paris and other French cities. Macron’s second term ends next year, and even before the violence of last weekend, Marine Le Pen, the leader of the far-right party known as the National Rally, was, according to polls, his most likely successor. That appears to be even more true now, with Le Pen’s final-round opponent now likely to be Jean-Luc Mélenchon, a left-wing politician who has associated himself with the protest movement but who is also radically unpopular in the country at large.

I recently spoke by phone with Julian Jackson, an emeritus professor of modern French history at Queen Mary University of London, and the author of a number of books on French history, including the definitive biography of Charles de Gaulle, about the situation in France. During our conversation, which has been edited for length and clarity, we discussed what the riots mean for the political future of the country, why Macron’s Presidency is ending disastrously, and whether the riots will be understood as a hinge point of French history.

How much are the youth protests important in and of themselves, and how important are they because of what they symbolize about the state of the country?

That is a good way to think about it, because some events become important for what they represent, and these events are important because of what they reveal about a whole mass of anxieties and discontent in France today. But I think their importance has now become qualitatively metamorphosed, or changed, by what happened on Saturday with the rioting. So I think that’s created a qualitative change. We have moved from a protest that is revealing a lot of anxieties of the younger generation—and not just them, but also older generations—into a whole questioning about the functioning of the state or the government. I remember reading something saying that there will be a “before October 17th” and an “after October 17th.” And I think that may be slightly overdoing it, but it certainly transformed the political landscape.

You are referring specifically to the amount of violence that night?

Yes. But not just the violence. There was also the sense that the state was completely taken unawares and just had no control. It was like there was almost a void. There was a similar mood in 1968, actually, when de Gaulle’s No. 2, Édouard Balladur, described how he just had a sense that the state wasn’t able to do anything. And so I think there was that sense of a sort of void on that night, because people do need reassurance that they can be protected—their property and their lives. I was at a dinner party that evening just by chance, in Paris, and they were all, I think, fairly centrist, maybe slightly right of center, but totally not extremist people. And it was striking how one of them in particular was in a state of almost apoplectic fury. It was a bit like the way that some people wrote, after the Paris Commune of 1871, that this was a kind of Saturnalian, orgiastic, obscene uprising. And he talked in the same sort of language, that these people should just be shot. He meant the rioters, not the students, but what it showed me was a sort of class panic, a social panic, that that night created. And everywhere you go in France, people are terribly worried now about what’s going to happen next.

You are suggesting that this could be seen as a hinge moment, and my first thought was that you meant it will be the moment when everyone looks back and says that this was when they knew Le Pen was going to come to power.

And then, when you were just describing that dinner party, I thought to myself, this is also going to be a reason, perhaps in their own minds, for people like that—centrist to right-of-center—to justify taking this giant step and voting for Le Pen.

Whether or not it’s pulled down the last obstacle to a lot of people accepting the idea that they could vote for this party or not, I think you’re right. I still won’t say that Marine Le Pen is definitely going to be elected. They’ve got six months to go, and a lot of things can happen. But obviously if you’re putting money on it, she will be elected, and the chances have obviously increased hugely as a result of what happened, not only because it will frighten people into voting for her but also because the only other person who’s been audible, really, in this moment is Mélenchon, on the extreme left. He had lost a bit of traction over the last two years, I think because of a number of things, partly because of antisemitism and partly just because he did seem to be slightly losing it and slightly a figure of the past. But this has definitely reinvigorated him.

And he obviously can’t defend the pillaging and looting on Saturday night, but he’s very clever. He says when he’s asked, Well, I’m not the government. It’s not for me to make a comment on that. I’m going to say that I think the students are at least legitimate in their demands, and I’m completely with them, and I will demonstrate with them. And it’s not for me to comment on the violence because that’s something the state should deal with. And it’s obviously a completely devious answer.

But what’s very interesting is that what you might call the centrist politicians have almost been completely inaudible over the last few days. They just don’t know what to say. So, yes, I think it is a radicalizing moment, but in both directions. I think it will win Mélenchon new support and win Marine Le Pen new support. So I agree with your skepticism. Who knows whether it’s a turning point, but it sort of feels like that at the moment.

And the way the French election works is that there’s a first round for the Presidential contenders and then the two front-runners go forward. And Le Pen is polling well against the centrist candidates in a possible final round, with small to medium leads. But she beats Mélenchon by over thirty points.

Absolutely. And all I would say about that is that if, and it is an if, a centrist candidate does get to the second round, at that point there’s a chance they could be in a position to actually win six months down the line, but we don’t know how long the impact of what happened on Saturday is going to go on because there are more demonstrations coming up, obviously. I had the sense, talking to people, that they’re living on a volcano and they’re sort of tiptoeing around, knowing that the government’s paralyzed, and it can’t do anything, so they just wait till the Presidential election. And there’s this sort of strange, ghostly expectancy, which is quite weird, a mixture of apprehension and fear.

You said the centrist candidates, from the socialist center-left to the center-right, seem somewhat paralyzed. Why?

Well, Mélenchon has been with the lycée a hundred per cent from Day One, they love him, and Le Pen basically says, We need order. Meanwhile, the centrists are not sure where to situate themselves. If they go too close to the school protesters, they seem to be legitimatizing disorder, but then if they go too close to supporting everything the police does, that is a problem, too. It is the classic problem of centrism when the two extremes are so voluble and actually convincing to their own constituencies, and also some of the centrists were in Macron’s government, so that is also a problem for them, and it is difficult for them to find their voice.

And the reason for that is that the one thing you cannot underestimate at this moment is the cold contempt everyone has for Macron. I have not met a person who has anything good to say about Macron. Actually, I had lunch with one of his ministers the other day who obviously did say good stuff about him. But beyond that, it’s almost impossible. And, of course, the picture people have of Macron at one level is a rather authoritarian figure telling people what to do, a sort of vertical President. But then another, a vertical President who simply has no power. He’s the worst of both worlds.

I was about to say he seems helpless, but people also think he’s a top-down–

They’re right; he is. But unfortunately, without being able to do anything, because there are no levers to pull any longer, which is a very sort of strange situation.

Saying the only person you met who likes him is a minister of his reminds me of the Onion headline, “Dukakis Wins 80 Percent of Dukakis Family Vote.”

Yes, absolutely. I remember that actually.

I remember something people were saying four or five years ago, maybe around the second time that Macron fought off Le Pen, in 2022, that basically Macron has had incredible political success, and he will have been President for ten years, but at the same time, he’s killed off the center right and the center left and not provided any alternative. And so you’ve had this chasm that, when he leaves, is going to exist. I remember political analysts saying that five years ago, and it seems like sometimes political analysts say things that turn out to be true and sometimes it turns out to be not true. But it turned out to be exactly right.

I completely agree. I have written something along those lines myself in various pieces about Macron. And I think I must have written five years ago myself that the tragedy of Macron, really, who did seem to offer something, was that he either seemed indifferent to or incapable of thinking about the succession. If you want to take a contrast with a President who he resembles, Valéry Giscard d’Estaing, who was also a centrist liberal, but who really did make efforts to build a center party between the Gaullists and the left. And Macron simply has either not bothered to do it or failed to do it. I don’t think he’s particularly bothered to do it. And what’s so striking is that whenever he’s had a prime minister, he immediately sets out to undermine that person who should be a credible successor.

Macronism was always going to be strange because it does move from the center left to the center right. Some of the people under the Macron umbrella are really very far right, and the people on the left are not very far left, but they’re certainly not far from the Socialists. So he had succeeded, but he only succeeded in bringing over individuals. He was never interested enough in a structure. You know what people jokingly said about how his movement was originally called En Marche? And people said, typically, that the initials of the movement were his own initials.

I think Macron both profited from the weakness of the political parties, first the Socialists and then the Gaullists on the right, and also hastened it, as it were. And he created no alternative future. He also really did show too much contempt in the early days for what the French call intermediary organizations. He sort of thought it was just him and the people. So, trade unions, local mayors, local associations—he was terribly dismissive of those sort of civil-society organisms that can actually make it possible to get things done. Admittedly, I started off as an admirer, and I had great hope, like so many people. I think he’s politically been brilliant at exploiting the possibilities of coming to power and then disastrous at knowing what to do with it.

We have talked a lot about Macron, and almost all of the things you’ve said about his failures are essentially character flaws or political errors based upon his character flaws. How would you define what his agenda was, and to what degree his policies were failures or not failures, too? Because France is obviously facing what a lot of people think is a pretty calamitous budget situation going forward. And you do hear some people blaming him for it. You say other people say, No, he tried his best; he raised the retirement age. But I’m curious how much you think his failures were, in fact, mostly personal and how much they were also policy-based.

Well, I think it seems to me incontrovertibly the case that the French need to deal with their issue of the pensions and that he was absolutely right in what he wanted to do. I think he was a liberal, he was a left liberal, a liberal in the European rather than the American sense, and he wanted a degree of deregulation, but with slightly leftist social rhetoric. It was a kind of Blairism, you might almost say. So there was something attractive and plausible in the agenda. But the Yellow Vest movement started after a gasoline-tax increase. And then it was done for good.

He had framed the gasoline-tax increase as an environmental policy. But of course, he wasn’t prepared for the reaction in any kind of way. And what he never thought of was that the people who were going to be most hit were not the sort of urbanites who believe in environmental protection in Paris but people living in deprived areas who desperately needed their cars to get to work. And so it’s almost as if he has this sense of detachment from what France really was. I also think he made a very big error at the very, very beginning. He said, Well, it’s going to be a regime. I’m going to encourage entrepreneurialism and a “startup nation,” all that kind of thing. But one of his first measures was to abolish a redistributive tax, and that was a really bad symbol that announced, I’m the President of the rich.

And it was so badly judged because it wasn’t going to liberate the economy. It was a handout, really, to the richer segment of the population. And so I think he had some perfectly good ideas. People of the socialist left would not agree with that, but I, as a centrist Social Democrat, think that he had perfectly good ideas. But the problem isn’t the ideas. The problem is his disconnectedness. Remember, he had never been elected to anything before becoming President. He didn’t have that feel for France. I don’t know whether you saw, but he made a very revealing comment to the French writer Emmanuel Carrère a bit of time ago. He said, “I was never a teenager,” which is an extraordinarily revealing comment, actually.

Also slightly disturbing given his romantic history, but we can leave that alone.

I want to ask you about de Gaulle because you wrote a really astonishing biography of him. There are obviously a lot of differences, but there are a lot of similarities: rising above the established parties; coming to power as this figure above politics, in some way; staying for ten years in Macron’s case, eleven in de Gaulle’s case; student uprisings at the ten-year mark. So anyone can come up with the similarities. But what struck me especially from your book is that de Gaulle was also just a very strange figure who would try to make these, in the Macron phrase, Jupiterian decisions. De Gaulle could be almost dictatorial. He could be authoritarian. He would sometimes remove himself from politics in strange ways and make decisions that no one could understand. And it almost felt to me, after reading your book and watching Macron, that Macron was sort of self-consciously trying to do this, but it just didn’t work in his case.

I think you’re right. I think that is how he pitched himself. Each President has an official photograph of themselves, and Macron’s has him with de Gaulle’s war memoirs in the back. Macron came after the failed Presidency of François Hollande, who said he wanted to be a normal President. And in a way, the problem is the Fifth Republic doesn’t really quite allow normal Presidents, and you’ve got to somehow inhabit the role. He did want to reëstablish the idea that the President of the Fifth Republic needs to have a certain weight and so on. He talked about verticality and about being Jupiterian. That’s all Gaullist. And what I said about ignoring intermediary institutions, very Gaullist. But of course what he didn’t have is what nobody could have really, which is that de Gaulle could do this with all the history that went with being de Gaulle in 1958, when he came to power. Macron arrived with a history of being a palace special adviser and a Rothschild banker. De Gaulle could do this because he had all the weight of legitimacy that came from being de Gaulle. I don’t say that Macron’s a lightweight, but he simply couldn’t do that. He couldn’t be that.

I’m quite lucky you haven’t asked, but usually people ask me the question, What would de Gaulle do now? And I always say to them, it’s such a stupid question because he was a man of his own time, and what de Gaulle would think about #MeToo or A.I. or the environment, who knows? But the great thing about de Gaulle was his extraordinary pragmatism. He was very adaptable despite his strangeness. But with Macron, I think he was preparing himself to reinvigorate the institutions that de Gaulle created, but failed. ♦

41-year-old set on ‘dream to be able to race in Africa’

Lewis Hamilton has said he hopes to remain racing in Formula One until he can take part in the African Grand Prix which is set to take place in Rwanda in the 2030s. The seven-time world champion believes it is right that F1 is to hold a race there despite criticism of the country’s human rights record.

Last week F1 confirmed Rwanda was to host the African Grand Prix having concluded a 10-year agreement for the meeting on a purpose-built circuit, with the first race set for the early 2030s. It is lined up to be held on a circuit in Bugesera, 25 miles south of the capital, Kigali, which has yet to be constructed and marks a return to the African continent for the first time since it last held a race at Kyalami, South Africa in 1993.

Hamilton has been championing a race in Africa for some time. The 41-year-old is understood to be currently contracted with Ferrari until the end of the 2027 season, and speaking before this weekend’s US Grand Prix he said he would like to continue racing for at least another four years in order to make it to the meeting in Rwanda.

“I’m now forced to have to stay a lot longer because we have a race in Africa and that’s been my dream to be able to race in Africa,” he said. “So, I have to keep fighting to try and stay fit if I can. If I can still punch out good results over the next year, we’ll keep it going.”

Hamilton would be at least in his mid-40s if he was to continue until 2030. F1’s current oldest driver is 45-year-old Fernando Alonso.

Serbia has entered a new political era. The Student List has won the country's parliamentary election, marking a dramatic breakthrough for a movement that emerged from months of student protests and rapidly transformed itself into the country's most important political force.

In a brief statement to the nation, outgoing Serbian President Aleksandar Vučić conceded defeat and declared that the Student List has won the majority in parliament.

Vučić said that “based on the stratified sample, it is clear that we have lost the election,” adding that “the Student List has won and will have between 135 and 140 seats, while we will have around 105.

“No one else has entered parliament. The Alliance of Vojvodina Hungarians (SVM) has won three seats, the Albanians one, and I think the SDA has also secured a seat,” he added.

The Serbian Progressive Party received around 1.72 million votes, admitted Vučić. The Student List won 2.2 million, giving it a majority of 52% to 32.3 %.

It is still too early to assess the final results and determine whether any other smaller parties have crossed the 3% electoral threshold, but it is clear that this would not affect the final outcome of the election or the identity of the winner.

The result represents far more than a change in the distribution of parliamentary seats. It is a profound rejection of the political model that has dominated Serbia for more than a decade.

The Student List's victory is particularly significant because it was not created as a conventional political party. Its origins lie in the student protests that became a powerful expression of dissatisfaction with the country's political institutions, the government, corruption allegations, media conditions and what many protesters described as a lack of accountability.

What began as a protest movement has therefore achieved something considerably more difficult - it has translated street mobilisation into electoral legitimacy.

The result suggests that the movement managed to attract voters far beyond the university campuses where the protests began. Young people were its most visible supporters, but the electoral coalition appears to have extended to parts of the urban middle class, opposition-minded voters and citizens who had previously been reluctant to support established opposition parties.

For the ruling establishment, the result is a major political setback.

For years, President Aleksandar Vučić and the Serbian Progressive Party dominated Serbian politics through a combination of strong electoral organisation, extensive political influence, control over a large part of the media environment and a highly personalised political strategy centred on Vučić himself.

The election result indicates that this model has reached a critical limit.

The transition from protest movement to government could therefore prove much harder than winning the election for the Student List.

Running a country requires decisions on the economy, foreign policy, relations with the European Union, Kosovo, security, energy, public finances and Serbia's relations with Russia and China. The students will now have to demonstrate that their political legitimacy can be transformed into effective state governance.

First, it represents a generational change in Serbian politics. A movement dominated by young people has succeeded in challenging a political establishment that has shaped the country for years.

Second, it could lead to a major institutional reset. The new government is likely to face strong expectations concerning the independence of institutions, the judiciary, corruption, public media and electoral conditions.

Third, Serbia's foreign policy could enter a new phase. The new political leadership will face immediate questions about the country's EU accession process, relations with Brussels and Washington, and its traditionally close relationship with Moscow and Beijing. Whether the Student List seeks a clear pro-European orientation or attempts to maintain a more balanced foreign policy will be closely watched.

Fourth, Aleksandar Vučić's political future becomes uncertain. Even if he remains president, an electoral defeat would fundamentally change the political balance in Serbia. His ability to dominate the political agenda would be significantly reduced, while his party would have to rebuild itself in opposition.

The Student List has won the election. But its real political test begins now.

Winning an election requires mobilisation, a powerful message and the ability to unite voters around the desire for change. Governing requires something different: institutions, experienced personnel, economic policy, compromise and the ability to take responsibility for difficult decisions.

For Serbia, the election therefore represents both a political rupture and a moment of uncertainty.

The central question is no longer whether the students can challenge the established political order.

The question now is whether they can replace it - and what kind of Serbia they intend to build in its place.

The study was to be funded with $1.6m from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), along with another $1.6m from private partners like the Bill Ackman-backed Pershing Square Foundation and the Bluebell Foundation.

The government of Guinea-Bissau now has control over the researchers’ vaccines, which they apprehended when they searched the offices of the Bandim Health Project on Monday.

It was vital to support Guinea-Bissau in rolling the immunizations out to all newborns as soon as possible, Offit said. Guinea-Bissau currently plans to offer the universal birth dose in 2028.

The Danish researchers, led by Schaltz-Buchholzer, Christine Stabell Benn and Peter Aaby, hypothesized giving live vaccines, like the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), may have an overall mortality benefit beyond simply preventing illnesses like tuberculosis – but giving another vaccine like hepatitis B alongside it could dampen that effect.

“There’s no evidence for that, and it doesn’t make biological sense,” he continued. #432. The Fragile Unions of “Possible Love”

The South Korean writer and director Lee Chang-dong makes beautiful films about terrible things. The question that haunts his stories, giving them a pulsing undertow of dread, is whether those things have already happened or not. In “Secret Sunshine” (2010), a young widow moves to a new town with her son; tragedy has scarred them both, but the film, relentless in its ability to surprise, has even graver ills in store. “Peppermint Candy,” which had its première in 1999, unspools a nightmare in reverse: it opens with a man’s suicide, then rewinds gradually, through two decades of accumulated agonies, to reveal how he got there. In Lee’s movies, time tends to flow inexorably in a single direction, sans flashbacks (or flash-forwards). We see the characters’ histories in their anxious posture, their stricken faces, their frustrated yearning for a glimmer of transcendence.

Time works a bit differently in “Possible Love,” Lee’s first feature since the masterly psychological drama “Burning” (2018). The pain of this story lies squarely in the past, and Lee, who wrote the script with Oh Jung-mi, proves more willing than usual to bridge the gap between then and now. The outset is linear enough. We are in an unnamed town where, two years earlier, a manufacturer named SP Heavy Industries laid off forty per cent of its workforce. The workers organized a strike, but their efforts were brutally suppressed. Among the terminated is a welder, Choi Ho-seok (Sul Kyung-gu), who wears a frown so implacable that it might have been etched in steel. He has since found another manufacturing job, and his sharp-witted, warm-spirited wife, Mi-ok (Jeon Do-yeon), works at a protective-face-mask factory. They have a young son, Cheol, an ordinary kid with a telltale core of solemnity. The Chois can’t shake their memories of more prosperous times, or of the profit-motivated culling that brought them to an end.

They are not alone in their trauma. We meet the Chois on their way to a memorial gathering for a former colleague of Ho-seok’s who took his own life. The grief of those in attendance goes beyond one death; they seem zombified by their anguish. One woman, though, stands calmly apart. Her name is Lee Ye-ji (Cho Yeo-jeong), and she’s shooting a documentary about laid-off workers. Ye-ji is an amateur but she has a muckraker’s instinct for drama. When Mi-ok whisks a cup of soju away from Ho-seok—he’s an alcoholic, and has promised never to drink in public—Yeji, anticipating an outburst, thrusts her lens into the fray. “Do we amuse you?” Ho-seok growls at her. She apologetically lowers her camera, though not for long. She knows there’s a story here, and Lee does, too.

Despite this inauspicious start, Mi-ok and Ye-ji become fast friends. So enlivened are they in each other’s company—and so gracefully does Jeon’s performance harmonize with Cho’s—that you’re tempted to forget Ye-ji’s ulterior motives. But Mi-ok is never blind to them, or to another complicating fact: her new friend is very wealthy. At one point, she calmly calls her out for wearing a pricey-looking shirt while interviewing people who’ve been laid off. Ye-ji has a hunky hedge-fund-type husband, Sang-woo (Zo In-sung), who condescendingly tolerates, but doesn’t finance, his wife’s cinematic caprices. Seeing her attempt to empathize with her subjects, Sang-woo extends the most backhanded of compliments. “You’re different from those people. But you yourself believe that you’re the same,” he says. “That’s great.”

Ye-ji blanches at that jab but only tightens her grip on her project: the Chois are to be the battered working-class foundation of her vérité masterpiece. Ho-seok wants no part of it. Inarticulate with self-loathing, he can scarcely drag himself to his ex-colleagues’ regular group-therapy sessions, let alone unburden himself on camera. But Mi-ok is game to be interviewed, and her words spring to life in wistfully lovely flashbacks. Here she is as a young girl, witnessing, for the first time, the depths of her mother’s working-class shame. And here she is as a teen-ager, catching the eye of a brashly confident Ho-seok, and sensing, with a thrill, that her sheltered world has finally burst open. The first time I saw “Possible Love,” these forays into the past, for all their dewy poignancy, felt like missteps—a rare concession to the backstory gods. Only on a subsequent viewing did I see the craftiness in Lee’s design: Are these Mi-ok’s tearily recalled flashbacks? Or are they being imagined by Ye-ji, eager to transform her subject’s every reverie into a reënactment?

All of which is to say that “Possible Love” is a multitasking marvel: a drama about the fragility of unions, in every sense, wrapped in a seminar on documentary ethics, but one with implications for the fiction realm, too. In an interview, Lee has said that it’s no coincidence he and Ye-ji share a surname, however common. He employs a lightly jostling handheld camera that insidiously mimics the formal syntax of Ye-ji’s documentary—and, in so doing, leaves the mere idea of authorial objectivity by the wayside. (The cinematographer is Kim Ji-yong.) Lee seems to be acknowledging his own potential for dramatic exploitation, if not of his characters, then of the events that inspired the story—an automaker’s restructuring in Korea in 2009 that threatened thousands of layoffs, leading to both a strike and a police crackdown. Lee knows that the liberties of fiction do not absolve him of a responsibility to tell the truth.

No wonder some of the film’s most piercing moments are on the periphery. One woman in a group-counselling session recalls the joyous early days of the strike, with their almost sacred spirit of community: “In all my life, I’ve never felt such happiness.” Another speaks of dreading the elevator in her building, lest she run into neighbors who still work at SP Heavy. Lee’s films have often zeroed in on misfits and outcasts, cut off from society’s embrace. What he evokes here is a different kind of alienation, suffused with bitterness and loss: a mass- casualty event that once brought people together must now be suffered in isolation. The boldest declarations of solidarity, Lee knows, often precede the quietest and cruellest of betrayals.

After the film’s première, at the Venice International Film Festival (where it won the Silver Lion Grand Jury Prize), more than a few observers invoked another class-conscious, double-decker marriage story from Korea: Bong Joon Ho’s “Parasite” (2019). (In both films, Cho Yeo-jeong, evidently cursed with resting rich face, plays the wealthier of two wives.) Lee’s contemplative realism could hardly be further removed from Bong’s blood-slicked flamboyance, but let’s indulge the reductive comparison. Like “Parasite,” “Possible Love” leads us into an enviable piece of real estate—Sang-woo’s family vacation home, a beachside paradise that was built for knotty third acts. But Lee, who has been known to gut his characters in the final stretch, doesn’t raise the body count to Bongian proportions. There is violence here, but it comes at us obliquely, with no inflection or exaggeration—in a sex scene that shivers with menace, or through a grimly mediated screen of documentary footage. Physical force has a hundred ways to shock and linger, but, crucially, it isn’t granted the last word.

The title asks whether love can survive what life throws at it: unemployment, depression, addiction, disappointment, poverty. A superficial glance would conclude that Ye-ji and Sang-woo are a dream couple, yet the more we see of them, the more glaring their lack of emotional capital. Miraculously, though, whenever the camera shifts to Ho-seok and Mi-ok—whether they’re bickering on their way home or scarfing down late-night snacks on the kitchen floor—love feels not just possible but plausible. Sul and Jeon have admittedly had plenty of practice; this is the third movie, after “I Wish I Had a Wife” and “Birthday,” in which they’ve played a couple. Both actors have also worked with Lee before, to tremendous effect. Sul was the suicidal protagonist of “Peppermint Candy,” and Jeon was the grieving heroine of “Secret Sunshine”—two performances that, for sustained emotional force, have few rivals in the acting annals of this century. They, too, are part of the past that haunts “Possible Love.”

Faced with such a high-stakes reunion, the actors, wisely, rise to the challenge with exacting understatement. You keep waiting for Sul to explode, but Ho-seok’s sadness lies beyond histrionics, and we realize that he isn’t angry or distant by nature; his decency cost him almost everything, and he now wields detachment as a shield against further harm. Without his wife to chide and console him, where would he be? Mi-ok feels her family’s losses acutely, but her impishly defiant spirit—watch as she tells her boss she’s starting a union, almost on a whim, like a goofball Norma Rae—has armored her against despair. And Jeon, in the most intricately detailed and emotionally liberated performance I’ve seen this year, gives flesh and definition to the character’s sardonic sense of mischief, her sudden spasms of grief, and her deep understanding that the most romantic gestures can be the most pragmatic. Hence the gorgeous moment when Mi-ok joins Ho-seok for a nightcap under the stars—secret moonshine—and affirms that, in the face of insurmountable problems, enduring them together is their only solution. “Drinking without you, it’s so bitter,” Ho-seok says. Solidarity forever. ♦

#442. Political Skirmishes in “Other Desert Cities” and “A Bright Room Called Day”

On the night that I attended the current Broadway revival of Jon Robin Baitz’s 2010 play, “Other Desert Cities,” there was a mad clapper at large, a mercenary determined to let us know whose side he was on. It was clear which actor he favored: Joe Keery, who shot to fame on the Netflix hit “Stranger Things” as the moody, mulleted Steve Harrington, a small-town doofus drafted into a teen militia. In “Other Desert Cities,” Keery plays a different sort of conscript, the quietly perceptive reality-TV producer Trip Wyeth, a louche charmer negotiating a ceasefire between his neocon parents and his sister, Brooke, who is threatening to spill the family’s secrets in the pages of this very magazine. I have no idea whether the clapper was a Keery superfan or just agreed with Trip’s critique of his family, but he wasn’t wrong on either count, with the actor delivering a career-making performance: loose, bright, and unpredictable. After his best speech—a witty stem-winder that ends with the declaration “I am California! And California is not happy!”—I was tempted to clap, too.

Unfortunately, the starry ensemble surrounding Keery felt unevenly weighted and off-kilter, like billiard balls only occasionally landing in the pocket. Baitz’s story is set in Palm Springs, in December, 2004, at the home of Polly and Lyman Wyeth, an opulent sandstone aerie with meditative views of the desert, a massive Christmas tree, and portraits of eminences like Barry Goldwater. The Wyeths are Republican grandees and L.A. royalty: Polly (Julia Louis-Dreyfus, all sparkle and venom) is a retired screenwriter, Lyman (a craggy, mournful Ed Harris) a former Hollywood cowboy. Polly, a verbal sharpshooter in a family of clay pigeons, is a dab hand at witticisms like “Sarcasm is the purview of teen-agers and homosexuals,” but if she’s a casual bigot she’s also a reinventor, a steerer of fates: a Jewish go-getter from Texas, she co-wrote frothy commercial films with her sister, then married into Wasp aristocracy with Lyman, who became a government ambassador after their pal “Ronnie” got elected President. By the time we meet the couple, however, that’s ancient history: the Iraq War is raging, and they rarely leave Palm Springs, where they’re in a right-wing bubble, barely tolerating Polly’s now estranged sister, Silda, a lefty dipsomaniac fresh out of rehab, who is played with leggy relish by Allison Janney. The plot revolves, as it inevitably does in shows like this, around a collective trauma. Here, it’s the loss of a third sibling, a radical son who, in the chaos of the seventies, blew up his life and warped the family trajectory. That’s the dark story Brooke is ready to tell the world in a manuscript she’s brought from Sag Harbor—the world’s least welcome Christmas present.

This material should feel resonant, given how many families are split right now—over MAGA, over Israel—and how much of a default confessionalism has become online. (These days, Brooke could post an incendiary tweet storm and skip the airfare.) The script is full of pungent, funny debates about power, with observations that still land. “Families get terrorized by their weakest member,” Polly argues. And Trip tells Brooke, “Isn’t it revenge enough that everyone worries about you all the time?” If the exposition creaks, the cast is sprightly, and, for a long time, it’s a blast to watch them spar, particularly Louis-Dreyfus, who scores laughs off the smallest phrases—a disgusted “amongst the Basques,” say. Together, she and Harris capture something formidable about the slice of Old Hollywood the Wyeths represent—sybaritic and hypocritical, brined in self-pity but with a fierce, glamorous quality.

The primary problem is, unfortunately, Lily Rabe, who plays Brooke in a different, blunter register than everyone else onstage. I’ve seen Rabe give beautiful, varied performances, from her dignified Hermione in this past summer’s “The Winter’s Tale” to her demon-possessed nun in “American Horror Story,” gyrating saucily to “You Don’t Own Me.” In “Other Desert Cities,” she’s all sulk and grimace. To be fair, it’s a hard role: Brooke is a solipsistic bummer, a frustrated writer who spends months in a mental hospital being tended to by her mother, then ambushes her with a score-settling tell-all. Instead of seeming righteous but flawed, Rabe’s Brooke validates Polly’s harsh judgment that she’s a reckless brat convinced her truth is the only truth. When, in the final act, a twist reframes the Wyeths’ behavior as, if not fully sympathetic, more understandable, it just made me furrow my brow. By then, it was hard to imagine Brooke’s parents opening up and easier to imagine them manipulating her—defusing, by any means necessary, their daughter’s incendiary literary device.

Even so, I had fun. I loved every costume and considered trying to dress more like a Palm Springs Republican, as Polly would want me to. And there was even, perversely, a touch of escapism to the experience. “Other Desert Cities,” so topical in 2010, now feels as if it were written in the Paleolithic era. If the Wyeths are monsters, they’re also dinosaurs, eclipsed by the nihilists who followed. There’s a wonderful moment when Harris puts on a show, mimicking the death scenes he specialized in onscreen: he buckles at phantom gunshots, then mimes shock, his torso atilt. It was his one true skill, Lyman says, modestly, with a Reaganite twinkle—he always knew how to make an exit.

Zillah’s speeches have since been reworked, most notably in a 2019 production in which a Kushner-y character called Xillah debated her, framing the story with an anxious meta-narrative. By then, the Hitler comparisons had a different feel: if Kushner had once been the boy who cried wolf, the wolf was at the door.

Witty, bitter, flamboyant, and emotionally grounded, the dialogue swings, delightfully, between intimate exchanges and grandiloquent soliloquies, capturing the vulnerability of idealists in the face of evil. When the Devil himself appeared, in a puff of smoke, the town-house alarm went off and the play paused. For a moment, it all seemed, surreally, to be part of the production: yet another alarm to ignore so that the show could go on. ♦
