#3201. Labor and the Coalition are poised to take a reckless gamble on AI. Haven’t they realised community support has collapsed? | Edward Santow and Sarah Sacher

Australian workers expect the government to act to protect them from AI harms. There is a clear reform solution at hand

Get our new political email, free app or daily news podcast

Australia’s major political parties are edging towards rare consensus. Labor and the Coalition increasingly want to take a major bet on artificial intelligence. Yet, just as the parties are coming together, the Australian community is running the other way.

Last year, only 30% of Australians believed the benefits of AI outweighed the risks. In 2026, community trust in AI appears to be falling even further.

It was an unusual step for our parliament to form a joint select committee on AI. It signalled that the decisions being made now on datacentres and how we adopt AI could define our economy, environment and society for generations.

As a fresh round of hearings starts this week, the committee members must confront the gulf between the politics of AI and the stark reality on the ground. Some parts of the business sector are urging us to prioritise innovation and AI adoption, which is often another way of saying: “Do a better job of selling the upside of AI.”

Whether you’re an AI optimist or a sceptic, this is a terrible idea. Sam Altman, Dario Amodei and the other titans of Silicon Valley have a golden megaphone to spruik the benefits of their technology. Australians don’t want our elected representatives to do the same thing – especially when the technology is already bringing both opportunity and harm.

This doesn’t mean being blind to the benefits of AI. On the contrary, Australia is among the highest users per capita of Anthropic’s Claude and other generative AI applications. But we must also be clear-eyed about potential risks. And, crucially, we don’t see a compelling response to our concerns from government or the AI companies themselves.

Take the impact of AI on work. Our community is asking practical questions. How will AI impact our jobs and the jobs of our children? What is the government doing to ensure the AI systems used in Australian workplaces are safe?

Our new report, Drawing the Line, lays out how AI is propelling the rise of harmful tech-enabled worker surveillance. Often workers are unaware of the extent to which they are being monitored – including through their work devices and phones, and via software such as Microsoft Teams.

Unlike some of the hypothetical (and frankly terrifying) risks that the joint select committee on AI has already covered, such as AI-enabled biological weapons and engineered pandemics, this is happening today.

Australian supermarket workers are already being pushed to meet AI-generated productivity metrics that force them to work harder and faster under the threat of punitive repercussions – putting them at risk of injury and workplace accidents.

Remote workers are already being subjected to intrusive surveillance software capable of watching everything they do on their screen – including audio recording conversations they have with their families.

Rideshare drivers are already being penalised for road conditions and customer complaints that are unrelated to their performance, which can result in their account being deactivated and days of work lost.

Workers are being quietly crushed by commonplace AI-powered surveillance tools that have crept into our workplaces and home offices. And the impact is real. Think, for example, of the young woman who reported being sexually harassed by a boss that required her to be on video at all times. Or warehouse workers driven to suicidal ideation due to relentless micromanagement by machine.

Our workplace laws don’t protect workers from unreasonable surveillance. Nor do they give employers clarity about what types of monitoring are lawful.

Unlike existential risks from AI, which are outside the Australian government’s direct control, this problem could be addressed through straightforward law reform.

We urge the government to update the Fair Work Act to require any worker monitoring be “fair and reasonable”. This builds on the government’s commitment to require all personal information covered by the Privacy Act to be handled fairly and reasonably.

Australian workers expect the government to act to protect them from AI harms. There is a clear reform solution at hand. This is not a hypothetical future risk – let’s address it now.

Prof Edward Santow is co-director of the UTS Human Technology Institute and served as human rights commissioner from 2016-2021. Sarah Sacher is HTI’s senior responsible technology policy specialist

#3206. In Germany, Wind and Solar Overtake Fossil Power

In a first, Germany drew more power from wind and solar than from fossil fuels last year.

Together, wind and solar generated 44 percent of Germany’s electricity, while fossil fuels generated roughly 43 percent, according to data from the Energy Institute, a think tank based in London. The figures represent a major milestone for the German “Energiewende,” or “energy turnaround,” a national push to ramp up renewable energy and phase out gas, coal, and nuclear power.

Germany shut down its last remaining nuclear power plants in 2023. It is aiming to wean off of coal power by 2038, though experts say it will likely achieve that goal much sooner. By 2045, its target date for net zero emissions, Germany plans to convert any remaining gas-fired power plants to green hydrogen.

Aiding the buildout of wind and solar is an effort to simplify permitting for renewable energy projects. German officials have digitized permitting, streamlined approvals, and declared clean energy to be of “overriding public interest,” a designation that lends projects protection from legal challenges.

Germany has the third largest economy in the world, after the U.S. and China, and the largest economy in Europe. Its embrace of renewable energy has helped push the E.U. toward cleaner power. Last year, for the first time, the bloc saw wind and solar overtake fossil fuels, according to data from energy think tank Ember. Wind and solar generated 30 percent of E.U. power in 2025, while fossil fuels generated 29 percent, and nuclear and hydropower supplied most of the rest.

#3207. ‘Comfort in a bowl’: porridge hotter than ever as fibremaxxers get their oats

Humble breakfast staple at heart of trend stirred up by health concerns and hearty spoonfuls of nostalgia

The clocks may be going back in the UK this weekend, but the biggest sign winter is coming is a steaming bowl of porridge on a breakfast table.

A staple for centuries, porridge is traditionally made with oats, water and a pinch of salt or sugar. However, the humble dish is now experiencing its own renaissance, with the latest iterations transforming it from drab to decadent. The trend is being driven by a growing fibre-centric movement as well as by nostalgia.

On social media, recipe creators are experimenting with everything from miso versions to sour cherry and pistachio butter toppings. At 26 Grains, a central London restaurant that specialises in porridge, this month’s offering includes a five-grain porridge topped with fig and red grape jam and a dollop of whipped cream. Over at Oatis in west London, toppings range from caramelised banana and tahini to mushrooms and pesto.

Alex Hely-Hutchinson, the founder of 26 Grains, describes porridge as “comfort in a bowl”. Part of its appeal, she says, is that it allows a “moment for pause in a busy world” – plus most people know how to make it.

It is a trend that is affecting home store cupboards. Searches for organic jumbo oats at Ocado are up 98% year on year while sales of White’s jumbo oats are up 56%.

Hedié Rhodes, the founder of the UK’s first dedicated cereals bakery, Husk & Honey in south London, describes the “personalisation aspect” of porridge as an instigator of the trend. Her brand, which began in 2015 specialising in granola, now also sells dedicated porridge blends. Bestsellers include cinnamon bark and cranberry and a spiced apple version.

“Porridge is a great carrier for a lot of nice things,” Rhodes says. “You can make a big pan of it and everyone in the family can still have a different style. Some might want it just with honey, others might want to add really fancy toppings.”

Alex Hayes, a co-founder of the food innovation studio Harris and Hayes, compares porridge to salads and smoothies. “It’s a great canvas for adding specific toppings that suit specific taste and nutritional needs,” she says, pointing to nut butters alongside more functional additions such as psyllium husk.

While protein mania isn’t slowing down, the online nutrition trend of “fibremaxxing” is encouraging more people to up their fibre intake. The NHS recommends adults should eat 30g of fibre a day, but according to the British Dietetic Association the average intake is only 18g. Oats are a source of the soluble fibre that is essential for gut health and can also help reduce cholesterol levels. So rather than eating protein-rich eggs for breakfast, many people are now reverting to a bowl of porridge.

On the menu at Café Cùil on the Isle of Skye you’ll find a “wee porridge” featuring pinhead oats that are soaked in oat milk overnight. Its founder, the chef and writer Clare Coghill, then finishes it with seasonal toppings. At the moment it’s a hazelnut praline brittle and poached pears.

Coghill, who recently tasted 86 bowls of porridge in her role on the judging panel of the world porridge-making championship that takes place in the Scottish Highlands each year, says “porridge purists” prefer to use coarser pinhead oats, also known as steel-cut oats, rather than rolled oats which are softer. “Pinhead were the original oats, so it’s a way of celebrating Scottish heritage,” she says.

Hely-Hutchinson opts for a mixture including jumbo oats and rolled oats for “creaminess”, along with rye, barley and malted wheat for added texture and “a slight fermented note”. Other recipes have ditched the oats altogether for rice, amaranth or quinoa.

To avoid a gloopy porridge, Coghill says stirring is key. “It’s all about slowly warming up the pot and constantly moving the spurtle [stirrer] around, which will create a really nice silky bowl of porridge.”

Hely-Hutchinson prefers hers “just right”, which for her is on the softer side. “Porridge is so nostalgic, I make it how my mum used to for me,” she says. “It all depends on the person. It’s like the same as how much Marmite someone puts on their toast or the amount of milk they put in their tea.”

Next, as porridge pivots from basic to bougie, is a move beyond the breakfast table. “People are increasingly busy and seem to be receptive to a more simple dish,” says Hely-Hutchinson, adding that customers get annoyed if porridge is not available on the menu past 3pm.

Hayes says we can expect to see more savoury options popping up on menus taking influence from Chinese congee, a traditional savoury rice pudding, and Indian kitchari, made with yellow split mung beans.

Rhodes points out that similar to how bakers experiment with wheat, people are now realising how much potential oats have. “It’s really interesting pushing the limits with oats. There are so many avenues to explore,” she says.

There is one downside to this new porridge passion, though: having to wash up the pan.

#3210. I’m a CTO. AI still needs your human expertise | Madhu Shashanka

AI is increasingly getting good at doing some tasks, but it can never replace human workers’ judgment

Autonomous cars, which may be the safest we have built, keep getting easily avoidable parking tickets. While Waymos crash far less often than human drivers over tens of millions of miles, they’re still terrible at performing basic tasks – keeping out of disabled parking spots and tow-away zones, or steering clear of a church garage during Sunday service, according to the Wall Street Journal.

That is the state of AI today: impressively capable at something complex, yet embarrassingly clumsy at something easy. And it has implications for what work will remain human and what will be automated by AI.

People expect AI to lead to fewer jobs in 34 of the 37 countries surveyed by the Pew Research Center. In wealthy countries like the US, Australia and South Korea, about seven in 10 expect job losses over the next 20 years. And across countries, concern among young people is rising. While fears focus on job numbers, the bigger change will be what jobs become.

The nature of human work will move towards judgment. Experts refer to AI as “jagged”, superhuman at certain tasks but weak at others, with no clear boundary between the two. AI rapidly improves where work can be automatically checked and examples are endless. But it’s shaky where no automated test can determine whether the answer is right. Even if AI is correct a majority of the time on a task, it often doesn’t tell you when it’s inaccurate. If you’ve used AI, you’ve probably experienced how it can be confidently wrong.

Some of these errors will solve themselves as models get better. But jaggedness is a symptom of how these models learn. I see this often as a technology leader building with these systems.

A smarter model still needs a right answer to learn from. When one gap is closed, it shows up somewhere else no one thought to check. But many business decisions don’t immediately have a clear right answer. Whether a choice is good depends on a future no one can see and data no one has. That’s where judgment comes in. So AI will keep mastering tasks that have checkable results but struggle where a person has to decide what a good outcome looks like.

As a result, a new type of work is starting to appear. Someone tests whether the output of AI is correct based on real work, not just on benchmarks. Someone sets limits on what AI is allowed to do before it runs, so the worst mistakes are avoided. Someone runs and manages several agents the way a shift manager runs a crew. Picture an airport control tower. Radar and software track flight paths, with humans judging the few that matter to avoid collisions. No one is manually watching every screen. That would never scale.

Setting AI’s limits is harder than it sounds. In a 2026 study, after an agent was told to protect a secret, it destroyed its own email server, deleting every single email. It was given a goal, without any limits on what it could do. The researchers described it as the right values paired with terrible judgment. Deciding what is off limits and what a mistake would cost takes someone who knows the work and will answer for the result.

Here is the good news for people worried about their own jobs. The people best suited for doing this new type of work aren’t computer programmers. They are the ones who know how the real work is done. Catching what a confident AI left out or got wrong takes someone who knows the complete answer. That knowledge is uncheckable, unwritten and takes the form of judgment honed over years of doing the task and watching it break.

Ford recently learned the value of human expertise. In recent years, it leaned hard on automating its quality control, cutting thousands of jobs. But earlier this year, it had to rehire 350 veteran engineers to oversee the process and hunt for failure points before a part reached the factory floor. They brought the judgment of watching parts fail over years. With humans at the helm, Ford’s quality ranking then hit first among mainstream brands for the first time since 2010.

But we are making a serious mistake with the jobs we’re eliminating due to AI: the entry-level, routine ones. That routine work is how judgment gets built over time. Junior employees work under the supervision of senior experts until their experience turns into instinct. AI eliminates the friction related to learning how to do the job. But the demand for judgment did not go away. We are just destroying the path to developing it.

The real question is not whether machines will take over our work. It is whether we will have the people who can tell when the machines are wrong. Right now, we are automating the training ground where judgment is learned. If you have hard-earned expertise about anything, the next few years will only make you more valuable. And if you are just starting out, the old apprenticeship model will not hand it to you. You have to go and earn it, deliberately, on tasks the machines cannot yet judge.

Madhu Shashanka is co-founder, chief scientist and chief technology officer at Concentric AI, a data security software company

#3213. Apple Ramp STEM Challenge

This simple apple ramp STEM challenge is a brilliant, hands-on way to learn about the effect of gradient on speed and friction. We’ve made a friction ramp to test toy cars before, and this apple version is a fun twist for an autumn themed STEM activity.

The ramp is covered with bubble wrap to increase friction between the apple and the ramp surface, and then with kitchen foil to reduce the friction between the two.

Friction is the force that slows objects down when they try to slide past each other. If you run your hand over a smooth table, there’s not much friction; if you do the same on a carpet, there’s more friction. The rougher the surface, the more friction is created.

If you walk along a slippery surface barefoot, it’s much harder to slip than if you wear socks. There’s less friction between socks and the floor than between uneven, and sometimes damp bare feet and the floor.

A friction ramp is a ramp covered with different surfaces to investigate how the surface affects the time it takes for a moving object to travel down the ramp. The apple should not be pushed down the ramp with any force; instead, it should be held at the top and then released as the timer starts. The ramp surface is the variable, everything else must be kept the same.

Create a ramp using the cardboard and blocks.

Record how long the apple takes to roll down the cardboard. Repeat twice more, so you have three recordings.

Cover the ramp with bubble wrap and record how long it takes the apple to roll down the ramp three times.

Remove the bubble wrap and cover the ramp with kitchen foil, then record how long it takes the apple to roll down the ramp three times.

Repeat the investigation with a longer ramp and compare the times.

Use a ball or toy car instead of an apple.

Experiment with different materials covering the ramp.

Learn about friction with a teddy zip line.

Which surface allows the apple to move the fastest?

Which surface slows the apple down the most?

#3217. Weather Whiplash Helped Fuel the Wildfires in France and Spain

A wildfire burning in Burgohondo, Spain, last week. Mateo Lanzuela / Europa Press via Getty Images

In a summer of record heat waves, France and Spain are now facing wildfires that have forced the evacuations of more than 300,000 people. Scientists say that a sharp swing from wet to dry weather, a signature of climate change, helped fuel the blazes.

Although western Europe saw a rainy start to the year, “the summer of 2026 is emerging as one of Europe’s most significant heat and drought events in recent years,” said scientists at World Weather Attribution.

Last month was the hottest June on record in Western Europe, which recorded more than 10,000 excess deaths during an unprecedented heat wave. Fires are now burning near Bordeaux, France, and Madrid, Spain, and have scorched hundreds of thousands of acres in both countries. In some places, towering columns of smoke have even given rise to “fire clouds,” which can generate high winds and lightning strikes that further the spread of fire.

Francesca Di Giuseppe, fire forecast coordinator at the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, said the “rebound” from a wet winter to a dry summer was a key factor. After an exceptionally rainy winter spurred the growth of vegetation, an unusual summer drought turned that new growth to tinder.

So-called weather whiplash has been a driver of other wildfires, including the devastating fires that hit Los Angeles last year. After seeing record rainfall in early 2024, Los Angeles was faced with a lengthy dry spell that desiccated abundant grasses and shrubs.

Scientists say that warmer air holds more moisture, producing both more drought and more extreme rainfall. A paper published last year in Nature Reviews likened the atmosphere to a sponge that is growing larger as the planet warms. A bigger sponge soaks up more moisture from plants, soils, lakes, and rivers, creating drier conditions on the ground. It also takes longer to fill up before emptying out, leading to longer stretches between bouts of rain. But when the sponge does empty, it unleashes more water, producing more extreme rainfall.

Weather whiplash, said lead author Daniel Swain, a climate scientist at UCLA, “may turn out to be one of the more universal global changes on a warming Earth.”

Scientists say that France and Spain are facing an additional complicating factor: the decade-long retreat from rural areas. As farms are abandoned and reclaimed by nature, Europe is seeing the growth of large, unbroken expanses of forest and scrubland that allow fires to spread unchecked. Some experts have called for using controlled burns to clear up dried underbrush, or bringing back wild horses, cattle, and other large grazers to do the same.

#3218. Wikipedia often gets taken for granted – but its history is fascinating, and its future is fragile

Wikipedia’s 68m articles have been consulted trillions of times. Not bad for a website that has barely changed since it was founded 20 years ago

A few years ago, I was struck by the tension between how Wikipedia was used, and how it was talked about. Everyone was on it all the time, and yet its reputation had stalled at “don’t trust it”, because it was the “free encyclopedia that anyone can edit”.

Clearly people were trusting it, so much so it had become part of the fabric of everyday life since it was founded by Jimmy Wales and Larry Sanger in 2001. But despite the fact it now has been consulted trillions of times by billions of people (English Wikipedia alone is 5.2bn words long), it inspires an odd lack of curiosity. Even people donating to Wikipedia might say the money was “for the factcheckers”, though there aren’t any – not paid ones, anyway. Meanwhile, the internet in the social media era had begun to feel like a much uglier place, and some digital nostalgia was setting in: here was this holdover that had somehow survived; a website that functions – and even looks – much the same as it did 20 years ago.

You could say that I fell down a Wiki rabbit hole, especially when it came to the website’s early years. Almost everything important about the project came together quite quickly. Its amateurish, homegrown ethos was off-putting to a lot of writers and journalists, and that meant many questions about why it had worked had no answers. Most of the people seriously interested in Wikipedia were a handful of academics – so I decided to write a book about it.

I’m not a tech journalist. Instead, I have a background in the humanities. Wikipedia actually does, too: philosophy, and even off-piste disciplines like town planning and poetry, played a much bigger role in its inception than pure technology. Ward Cunningham, who invented the wiki in the 1990s – that’s a webpage anyone can edit, and the foundational tool of Wikipedia – thought very carefully about how the power of computers might be democratised. He drew on all sorts of oblique concepts for it, such as constructivism and the Zen concept of wabi-sabi – beauty in imperfection – and it’s remarkable how well the culture he created has persisted. Especially wabi-sabi – Wikipedia is, after all, always in a state of incompleteness.

Philosophy drew Wikipedia’s co-founders together as well. Though Wales worked in tech and finance, he corresponded with philosophers for years. And though Sanger was an academic philosopher, he spent lots of time arguing online – only “amateurs” seemed to share his sense that philosophy had life-and-death stakes. Lots of people in the dotcom boom started online encyclopedias, but Wales was the only one to hire a philosopher. In fact, the only person he hired was a philosopher. It was such an unpredictable choice, and an important clue to what made Wikipedia work: it sprang out of this digital “republic of letters”, where all sorts of people were thinking about facts and why they were important.

Because Wikipedia is so massive, writing an “exhaustive” book on it is impossible, like trying to represent a mountain with a grain of sand. Instead, I concentrated on the human history – the small, sometimes very small, number of people who really influenced what Wikipedia became. Wikipedians can be difficult, and the most ornery get a bad wrap – even the Wikimedia Foundation’s leadership has made comments about their “Dorito-stained fingers”, or tendency to stay home on a Saturday night. But by the end of the process, I was in awe of them, and I still am.

Wales and Sanger are prominent, but many other important early Wikipedians are unknown. The first woman to edit Wikipedia, Gaytha Langlois, hasn’t had much attention. She wrote about two things dear to her: Rhode Island and standard poodles – small states and big dogs. Wikipedia has always had a gender imbalance, which has proved stubborn – Sandi Toksvig is only the latest in a long line of critics trying to address it. She is part of an effort to create a more balanced alternative to Wikipedia called Mappa Mundi, which has the potential to feed back into Wikipedia as well.

I became fascinated too with a little-known contributor who had the first biographical page for a woman on Wikipedia (today, it would be called a user page). Her name was Ruth Ifcher, and she was a very intelligent grammar pedant, with a PhD, who had been forced out of the workforce with severe agoraphobia. Wikipedia came along later in her life and opened a whole new avenue for creative and meaningful work.

Proust said that neurotics alone “composed our masterpieces”, and that’s true here.

Neurodiversity is so central to Wikipedia that one former head of the Wikimedia Foundation told me the encyclopedia is a “city built by Asperger’s”. You can sense that energy in familiar topics – planes, trains and automobiles, comic books, weather, and all the rest. Often, Wikipedians are so prolific that they write too much, a phenomenon called “fancruft”. “Road geeks” (like trainspotters for roads) kept having their work about minor roads deleted, so a whole lot of them left in a huff, and started a separate online wiki encyclopedia for roads. That’s almost a microcosm of the inclusionists v deletionists debate, which is the biggest philosophical rift on Wikipedia – a rolling argument about what should be included, and what shouldn’t.

The co-founders had once believed that 100,000 articles would be “a reasonable size” for an encyclopedia, because that’s how big Britannica was. Today, Wikipedia has 68m, and Pew Research estimates that it served 1.9tn page views between 2015 and 2025. Because it is so monumental, I never expected Wikipedia to feel fragile or vulnerable – but as I was finishing the book, AI had begun to encroach on Wikipedia in an existential way by diminishing the web traffic it relies on, for instance, or by editors contributing huge volumes of poorly written AI-generated text. People ask me “is Wikipedia under threat?”, and it is, but so is everything from Hollywood to pure mathematics. I do think it will survive, but the quaking foundations made it feel urgent to say “we saw this made, and we won’t see anything like it again”.

Salt marshes protect coastlines by attenuating wind waves with their elevated marsh platform and above-ground vegetation1. Confidence in the persistence of this service under a changing climate requires knowledge on how extreme storms damage the platform and vegetation; however, a comprehensive, quantified understanding of salt marsh–wave interactions under extreme events is lacking. A unique experiment was conducted by transferring intact salt marsh to one of the world’s largest wave flumes, and subsequently exposing it to increasingly higher waves that reach up to 2 m significant wave height. We show that marsh substrates can persist with negligible erosion, despite cumulative wave exposure. By contrast, the vegetation was progressively damaged during continued wave exposure, gradually reducing vegetation-related wave attenuation. Results emphasize that vegetation was most effective in attenuating waves at low-energy marsh coasts (up to 1 m), where attenuation exceeded 10% (up to 50%), whereas attenuation at high-energy marsh coasts (wave height > 1 m) does not exceed 10%. Our global assessment supports the differentiation between low- and high-energetic conditions resulting from the flume experiment. Our results suggest that the marsh platform remains stable under extreme wave events and that substantial wave attenuation by marsh vegetation is limited to low-energy marsh coasts.

In coastal regions characterized by high flood risk, people and assets are commonly protected from flooding by dikes, dams and sea walls; so-called grey flood protection. These engineered structures are increasingly challenged by changes in local and regional climate conditions, such as sea level rise, land subsidence and increased storm exposure, and may become unsustainable1. Hard engineering structures are static and do not adapt to a changing environment2. Therefore, so-called green–grey flood protection—which combines traditional grey-protection with green-ecosystems—is increasingly considered a more sustainable strategy for keeping coastal regions safe from storm events with extreme waves3.

Green–grey flood protection uses the natural capacity of ecosystems to buffer extreme wave events and consequently reduce flood risk. Specifically, salt marshes with unique salt-tolerant vegetation4, often located in the temperate climate zone at muddy coasts, serve to attenuate large waves during storm surge events5.

Salt marshes attenuate waves by dissipating wave energy due to (1) the elevated bed6, and (2) the drag and roughness of the vegetation growing in the marsh7,8,9,10,11. Both are expected to change during extreme wave events, thereby decreasing the wave-attenuating capacity. Studies so far have indicated that marsh substrates remain stable under moderate storms (significant wave height under 1 m), whereas stability under extreme wave exposure (wave height exceeding 1 m) has not been tested. At the same time, above-ground vegetation breaks and detaches from the marsh surface during moderate storms5,12. Modelling studies extrapolating field evidence highlight that nearly all vegetation is broken after exposure to waves up to 1 m (ref. 13), inevitably leading to decreasing wave attenuation5,13,14. Overall, there seems to be a relationship between wave forcing and vegetation loss: as a storm lasts longer, above-ground vegetation is progressively damaged and wave attenuation diminishes.

In several regions, future storm surges are expected to exceed the water levels that have been assessed in laboratories so far (maximum of 2 m)5,15 ; for example, the UK16 and the Netherlands15. Such surges are also predicted to occur with a 25-year return period in the Yellow Sea and East China Sea (China); the Gulf of Carpentaria (Australia); the Bay of Bengal (Bangladesh); and the Gulf and East coast of the United States17. Surge levels are expected to increase even further in the coming decades18. There is a lack of quantified knowledge explaining progressive vegetation damage and potential substrate erosion under these conditions, and the efficacy of damaged marsh vegetation in wave attenuation during extreme wave events (>1 m).

Based on flume experiments and projected storm simulations at a global scale, here we aim to quantify the persistence of the marsh substrate and address the progressive vegetation damage and associated reduced wave attenuation under extreme storm conditions, by assessing:

A 306-m2 salt marsh with intact above-ground biomass and marsh substrate was installed in the Delta Flume, a 300-m-long, 5-m-wide and 9.5-m-high wave flume (Deltares, Delft, the Netherlands), enabling a full-scale test. A winter-state salt marsh was transplanted to two cross-shore transects using the first 200 m of the flume (Fig. 1). The marsh, fronting the dike, was characterized by a high density of marsh vegetation species, dominated by Elymus athericus (E. athericus), but with Atriplex prostrata present at lower densities (Methods). Such vegetation is present at a high elevation in the tidal regime, which makes it effective in attenuating waves14,15.

Extreme conditions were simulated over the marsh (Fig. 1). The water depth above the salt marsh surface ranged between 1.5 m and 4 m, and the hydrodynamic conditions reproduced depth-limited waves, with maximum significant wave heights (Hm0) set to half of the local water depth. Offshore significant wave heights ranged between 0.75 m and 2 m. The marsh was exposed for a total duration of approximately 29 h, of which 21 h included above-ground vegetation, whereas the last 8 h occurred without above-ground vegetation, but with largely intact below-ground biomass (see Methods and Supplementary Table 1 for the testing programme). The lower end of this range is equal to field observations and past flume experiments, with wave heights of 0.85 m (ref. 12) and 0.90 m (ref. 5). The upper end of the range represents extreme events, for example, in the Western Scheldt (the Netherlands) and Chesapeake Bay (United States)14,19.

Whether salt marshes effectively contribute to wave attenuation primarily depends on the stability of the marsh substrate. When the substrate erodes, below- and above-ground vegetation biomass might also be (partly) removed. Elevation measurements at 32 fixed locations at the salt marsh show, on average, a decreasing marsh surface over the total experimental time of 21 h with damaged vegetation (7 mm; 0.33 mm h–1), which is in the same order of magnitude as previous studies with smaller wave heights (4.4 mm surface lowering; 0.16 mm h–1)5,20, and smaller-than-typical length scales of the marsh microtopography (Methods). The presence of below-ground biomass and roots in the marsh substrate is known to enhance soil stability. A clear root zone was also present in our experiment, mainly in the top 10 cm of the substrate. The observed stable marsh platform aligns with past observations of hurricanes (Katrina and Rita) that eroded 527 km2 of mainly brackish marshes in the Louisiana coastal plain. Salt marshes remained stable and largely unchanged21. The stability of the marsh substrate under extreme conditions is an essential feature for marsh existence in areas in which regular wave attacks may be expected22. Our experiment shows that this essential feature is also valid for extreme storms, which agrees with past field and flume evidence20,21,23. Thus, with negligible surface erosion and largely intact below-ground vegetation biomass throughout all experimental runs, our experiment suggests that marsh platforms are able to contribute to wave attenuation irrespective of the short-term damage to above-ground biomass, as long as there is no lateral retreat due to seaward cliff initiation and erosion24,25. We therefore focus hereafter on progressive vegetation damage reducing wave attenuation over time.

Salt marsh vegetation breaks under prolonged wave exposure5,13,26. Consequently, the above-ground biomass that contributes to wave attenuation decreases. The experimental runs are clustered into three distinct groups of repeating wave height and water depth combinations. In the first two groups, continuous wave exposure caused progressively increasing damage to the above-ground vegetation, whereas the above-ground vegetation was removed in the last group (Methods and Supplementary Table 1). This allowed us to explicitly quantify wave attenuation by vegetation as a result of progressive damage of the above-ground biomass.

The above-ground biomass present in the flume before the experiment started did not statistically differ from the vegetation found in the field during the same period (Mann–Whitney U test; P > 0.05) (Fig. 2). Moreover, the effect of vegetation damage on the decreasing contribution of vegetation to wave attenuation was more pronounced for smaller than for larger waves. The experimental results emphasize a potentially large contribution to wave attenuation for incoming wave heights at the lower range of the experimental program (below 1 m).

The experimental results are described by a generalized linear model (GLM), which uses exposure duration and incoming wave height as predictors and wave attenuation by vegetation as response variable (pseudo R2 = 0.97; measured versus predicted R2 = 0.93; Methods). Following past studies during high waves, vegetation damage starts at significant wave heights of approximately 0.5 m (refs. 5,27) and continues with the increasing heights observed in our experiment. Vegetation damage also increases with exposure duration.

The experimental results in this study contribute to understanding salt marsh–wave interactions under extreme events: vegetation is progressively damaged and wave attenuation decreases, while the bed remains stable. Projecting results from the flume experiments to a global scale suggests that salt marsh vegetation generally enhances the resistance of low-energy marsh coasts to extreme conditions. Results emphasize that at almost all low-energy marsh coasts with waves up to 1 m, waves are attenuated by more than 10% (and up to 50%) for E. athericus, which can be considered a conservative wave-attenuation estimator for salt marshes generally (Extended Data Fig. 1). Attenuation at high-energy marsh coasts (wave height > 1 m) does not exceed 10%. This wave attenuation functionality is also found in seagrass environments under low-energy conditions34, and may also apply in freshwater settings32. Decreasing salinity, towards brackish and fresh water marshes, induces an increase in species diversity, because species diversity increases with decreasing environmental stress33. It also leads to a shift towards species with longer and less flexible stems compared to E. athericus, such as the common reed Phragmitis australis35,36. Both higher species diversity and the shift to longer and less flexible stems enhance wave attenuation, even under higher vegetation damage. This agrees with the result of our sensitivity analysis (Extended Data Figs. 3–6).

Across multi-year to decadal timescales, salt marshes could drown due to sea level rise and/or limited sediment supply37 and lateral marsh retreat may take place due to cliff erosion38. As a result, ecosystem services, such as carbon storage and wave attenuation, could be diminished. Between the years 2000 and 2019, a substantial salt marsh area has been lost globally (719 km2), with main hotspots including the US Atlantic coast and the Gulf of Mexico39. Drivers of marsh loss are similar to drivers of vegetation damage and a decreasing wave attenuating capacity, that is, hydrodynamic exposure. For the aforementioned regions, our global analysis shows considerable vegetation damage due to long exposure duration (Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 2). An increased vulnerability of coastal regions where marshes are retreating can thus be expected, as wave attenuation may also decline, further accelerating exposure of the coast.

Our results emphasize that restoration and conservation of salt marshes as a Nature-based Solution, supporting biodiversity, protect against extreme storms more effectively at low-energy coasts than at high-energy coasts. Hence, application at low-energy coasts should be prioritized, but even at high-energy coasts potential cost reduction is considerable as preventing 1 km dike heightening by 0.2 m, already saves 1.4 million USD2005 purchasing power parity, because of reduced overtopping rates29,40,41. Furthermore, reduction of the wave height by decimetres, offsets predicted average sea level rise of 0.49 m in 2100 (RCP4.5) already by decades42. This cost reduction can motivate continuous support and maintenance that is required for salt marshes to prevent long-term erosion and guarantee their persistence at high-energy coasts. The potential for wave attenuation emphasized by our results support a plea to protect marshes where they are currently present, and to restore former salt marshes, where fitting, to enhance their contribution to flood protection.

The physical model was built in the Delta Flume (a wave flume located at Deltares) by transplanting salt marsh blocks on top of a sand bed with drainage pipes. An artificial dike with a slope of 1:3.6 was constructed at the back. The Delta Flume has a length of 300 m, a width of 5 m and a depth of 9.5 m. We used 66 salt marsh blocks to construct two 71-m-long and 2.0–2.2 m-wide salt marsh profiles. In between the two rows a path was constructed 5 cm below the surface of the marsh, to drain both marsh profiles and enable access, maintenance and (manual) measurements. The height difference between the path and marsh surface was comparable with the variations in the vegetated marsh, that is, the clay heights and the vegetation height.

Blocks with a depth of approximately 0.7 m were installed at the expected erosion-prone areas: six at the seaward salt marsh edge and two at the dike toe. In between these, 25 blocks with a depth of approximately 0.4 m were installed. To connect the dike slope to the marsh, a block with a depth of 0.4 m was also installed (Fig. 1). The seaward edge of the salt marsh had an elevation difference of 0.6 m with the artificial bare tidal flat to recreate field conditions with a cliff. A brushwood dam was used as edge protection at this seaward edge of the salt marsh—similar to field locations in the Wadden Sea area43,44,45 and other regions46—to prevent lateral erosion, enabling comparison with experimental runs. The bare tidal flat was constructed using concrete to prevent bed level dynamics at the flat, which could potentially affect the incoming wave at the marsh edge. The first 40 m of the bare tidal flat had a decreasing gradient of 1:45, followed by a gradient of 1:9 over 12.5 m to create a smooth transition to the flume bottom and to save material.

The salt marsh blocks were collected in the field at nature reserve Peazemerlannen at the mainland Wadden Sea coast (53° 24′ 19.0908″ N, 6° 5′ 58.3476″ E) in the Netherlands. The blocks were harvested at the end of the growing season in September and October 2023. Two locations, landward and seaward, were selected with different soil compositions. The median grain size, D50, was 0.013 ± 0.010 mm (0.003 ± 0.001 mm) and the clay content was 31.4 ± 6.8% (44.5 ± 4.8%) for the seaward (landward) location (Extended Data Table 1). The particle size distribution was determined by adding a peptisator to prevent flocculation to the dried sample. The solution is left to soak for 16 h and subsequently sieved over a 63 μm sieve. The fraction larger than 63 μm was mechanically sieved over a set of sieves ranging from 63 μm to 63 mm. The particles left on each sieve were weighed and processed to determine the distribution. The smaller fraction was analysed using a sedigraph. The smaller and larger fraction together determine the full distribution.

The above-ground vegetation was similar at both selected locations to prevent vegetation gradients in the physical model. The high marsh zone was selected, as the high marsh generally contributes most to wave attenuation due to the high bed elevation15. Elymus athericus was the vegetation species dominantly present, with a mix of Atriplex spp. and Aster tripolium.

The salt marsh blocks were harvested by pushing a mould of 2 × 2 m2 (0.7 m depth) or 2.2 × 2.2 m2 (0.4 m depth) into the ground. The area around the mould was dug out using a 35-to-50 tonne excavator. A steel-bottom plate was then slid under the mould to minimiuze disturbance of the vegetation and the marsh substrate within the mould by as much as possible. Subsequently, the 3,000 to 6,000 kg marsh blocks were lifted, deployed on a closed truck to prevent wind damage, and then transported to the Delta Flume.

The harvest location was elevated above spring tide to assure safe access over land. To work safely and minimize disturbances to the ecosystem, the harvest was executed after the breeding season and before the storm season, during daylight, in the shortest period possible. Temporary steel road plates were used to distribute the weight of the excavator and trucks, which minimized damage to the marsh.

After arrival near the Delta Flume, drainage holes were drilled into the boxes around the blocks. The blocks were continuously monitored visually and saturation (by measuring weight) and salinity were periodically measured in a sub-selection of the blocks. Blocks were irrigated during dry periods after the weight dropped. From December 2023 onwards, the blocks were installed in the physical model of the Delta Flume. After installation, artificial light from growing lights was applied to the part of the physical model covered by a hall, to balance the blocked supply of sunlight. Ventilation was also applied to create a more natural air flow.

Mean organic matter content in the salt marsh substrate in our experiment was 4.7% ± 1.7% and ranged between 2.3% and 7.6% (Extended Data Table 1). Organic matter content was determined using loss-on-ignition. The organic matter content in the substrate in our experiment is similar to many other European salt marshes, which typically range between 0 and 76.7% (mean of 8.5%)47. More specifically, studies in the Wadden Sea, Northwestern Europe show a range between 1.3% and 8.8% (mean of 4.2%)48 and in the Mediterranean between 4% and 30% (ref. 49). The organic matter content in our study is within the lower range when comparing to, for example, North American marshes, which are more organic-rich (range 0−55%)50.

Prior studies describe a rooting depth of vegetation species in salt marshes of 10 to 40 cm below the surface51. The E. athericus marsh cover has a web-like root structure (that is, dichotomous branching), with long roots compared to other species, but an average to low central root and branch density52. Vegetation roots stabilize the soil, potentially with increasing stability if the vegetation composition is more diverse53. Root mass in our experiment was measured at 5, 10 and 20 cm depth, with n (sample size) = 4. Root samples were collected after the experimental wave runs at locations where the soil was undisturbed by placing bulk density rings (6 cm diameter and 4.05 cm depth), with the midpoint at the chosen depth. The rings with material were carefully dug out, roots were gently rinsed and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 48 h, before weighing to obtain the dry root mass (g) at each depth interval. The root density was then calculated. Below-ground biomass was mainly present in the top layer in our experiment (5 cm; 78% of the total biomass; Extended Data table 2).

Our study generally implies that ecosystem services that depend on the stability of the salt marsh substrate, including wave attenuation and carbon storage54,55, are unlikely to be affected by surface erosion during extreme storms, as the marsh substrate remains stable both at low- and high-energy coasts. This conclusion is based on our flume experiment, which contains two soil types (Extended Data table 1). The organic matter content of the soil is representative for European marshes, which corresponds to the lower range of the organic-rich soils on the US East Coast and the Gulf of Mexico (Methods)50. Our experiment provides evidence for the erosion resistance of soils; however, soil parameters, and in turn erosion resistance56, vary globally, emphasizing the need for controlled erosion experiments under extreme storms with a range of established and restored marsh soils. Organic-rich soils can have a more open structure that might result in instability51, and also brackish and freshwater marshes might be more prone to erosion due to shallower root zones and potentially low inorganic sediment content21. Hence, our projection yields a conservative estimate regarding soil erodibility for salt marshes, but may overestimate the stability of organic-rich soils and brackish marshes.

The experimental runs in the wave flume started in February 2024 to conservatively test the vegetation in its winter state. The state of the marsh vegetation installed in the Delta Flume before starting the experimental runs was equal to vegetation in the field, both measured in February 2024 (Fig. 2).

The experimental programme included three different water levels (1.5 m, 2.5 m and 4.0 m at the marsh, which correspond to 4.4 m, 5.4 m and 6.9 m at the wave paddle, respectively). The water levels were coupled with three significant wave heights (0.75 m, 1.25 m and 2.00 m; JONSWAP wave spectrum), maximizing the ratio of wave height and water depth at the marsh platform57,58. Smaller wave heights, with similar water depths (hence decreasing the wave height to water depth ratio) will result in a decreasing contribution to wave attenuation, but also less vegetation damage5. It may be expected that under these conditions the wave height at the landward side will always be smaller than under the most conservative wave height to water depth ratio we tested, as there is a limit of wave attenuation by vegetation with an increasing wave height to water depth ratio5. So, we aimed at the most conservative wave height to water depth ratio cases, enhancing vegetation damage and potentially decreasing wave attenuation by vegetation. Furthermore, two different values for wave steepness (0.04 and 0.02) were tested. These settings were combined in an experimental program with increasing water depths and significant wave heights over time, potentially resulting in increasing damage. First a short test (approximately 100 waves) was performed with water depths of 1.5 m and 2.5 m, to test the stability of the marsh, and subsequently longer tests (approximately 1,000 waves) with increasing water depths and wave heights were applied (Supplementary Table 1). This sequence was executed three times to study marsh stability and wave attenuation under potentially increasing vegetation damage: (1) light vegetation damage; (2) substantial vegetation damage; and (3) removed above-ground vegetation (Supplementary Table 1). This allowed us to explicitly quantify wave attenuation by vegetation as a result of progressive damage of the above-ground biomass, by subtracting wave attenuation by the marsh elevation in experimental runs without above-ground biomass (percentage decrease of Hm0) from wave attenuation in runs with full and damaged vegetation. Before the last repetition all vegetation was mowed level with the surface, to test wave attenuation under extreme vegetation damage potential. A significant wave height was also applied, approximating the maximum significant wave height tested by Möller et al.5, to facilitate comparison with that study (refer to Supplementary Table 1 for the full experimental program). The full range of tested wave heights allows us to generalize our findings.

Waves were forced with a wave paddle equipped with an active reflection compensation. The water level elevation in front of the wave paddle was continuously measured to observe the wave field including reflected waves. The motion of the wave paddle compensates for reflected waves to avoid reflection back into the physical model. The reflection coefficient in deep water varies between 0.2 (low waves and 4% wave steepness; experimental run SM-01 in Supplementary Table 1) and 0.35 (high waves and 2% wave steepness; experimental run SM-14 in Supplementary Table 1).

The clay surface was measured at 32 fixed locations at the salt marsh (16 per transect) using a total station (Trimble Ri) that has a measurement accuracy of 2 mm. The concrete bare tidal flat, seaward from the salt marsh in the flume, was not subject to change. Measurements were taken daily or bi-daily and bed-level-changes (mean, s.d. and standard error) were calculated for each measurement (Extended Data Fig. 7). The s.d. (43 to 48 mm) of all 32 measurement locations exceeded the average bed level decrease over the full experiment (7 mm), highlighting that the surface decrease after 21 h of extreme wave forcing was smaller than typical elevations in the existing microtopography of the marsh (Extended Data Fig. 7).

Vegetation properties were quantified using destructive measurements (bi)daily after changing the significant wave height and daily non-destructive measurements, by (1) measuring the highest and lowest vegetation canopy height at six locations, corresponding to separate salt marsh blocks (n = 6). The canopy height was measured by carefully lowering a styrofoam plate (30 cm diameter) with a hole over a stick. Height measurements were taken after the plate was lying stably at the canopy; (2) measurements of vegetation biomass (n = 6) by collecting all above-ground biomass from a square surface of 20 × 20 cm2, taking wet weight, drying in the oven for two weeks at 65 °C and taking dry weight; (3) measuring stem characteristics (E. athericus) by randomly selecting 20 standing stems from the marsh (n = 20). The length of each stem and outer diameter 5 cm above the base were measured using a caliper; (4) measuring total biomass removal (that is, flotsam) due to wave exposure by collecting all vegetation material at the dike and in the water with a fishing net made from chicken wire. Total wet weight was measured, and subsequently a wet subsample was taken and weighed. The wet subsample was dried for two weeks at 65 °C in an oven, whereafter the dry weight was taken; (5) measuring stem characteristics from already broken stems in the flotsam by randomly collecting 20 stems (E. athericus) from the flotsam after experimental runs. Subsequently, length and diameter were measured analogously to the standing stems, and flexural strength was measured. The destructive measurements for biomass (vegetation measurement 2) and stem length (vegetation measurement 3) affected a marsh area less than 1% of the total salt marsh in the physical model.

Vegetation stems (E. athericus) were collected in the field at the seaward and landward location (n = 75) where the salt marsh blocks were harvested. Stems were collected in February 2024 at the start of the experiment to allow comparison of the development of vegetation next to and in the flume with that in the field.

Vegetation stem length and dry biomass measurements were post-processed by calculating the mean, median, quartiles, inter-quartile range and standard error (Figs. 2 and 3). An exponential decay function was then fitted (Fig. 3), and then used to quantify vegetation damage over exposure time. Wave attenuation was related to stem length per wave height class, that is, significant wave heights of 0.75 m (r2 = 0.89), 1.25 m (r2 = 0.57) and 2.00 (r2 = 0.33; Fig. 3). Significant differences between above-ground biomass in the field and flume at the start of the experiment were tested using a Mann–Whitney U test. Significant differences in increasingly damaged vegetation height and biomass were tested using a t-test for normal and Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed samples with a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR P-value correction. Significant differences over the total experiment were tested using a Welch t-test with Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate (FDR) P-value correction. The few statistical differences might have been caused by the high variability of stem height and above-ground biomass at the marsh, and for the latter also the limited number of replicates to damage the marsh as little as possible.

Wave characteristics were measured using fixed resistance-type wave gauges present in the Delta Flume at the left wall of the flume (looking from the wave paddle to the end of the flume) with a measurement accuracy smaller than 1 cm (ref. 59). No variations in the width direction were discernible during experimental runs, because the setup was symmetrical in the width direction. We assume no variations in width, as is the case in general for flumes (as width, 5 m « length, 300 m)60. For every experiment, Hm0 is determined in front of and at the landward end of the marsh by using the three wave gauges in front of the marsh (locations X = 40.5, 43.5 and 49.5 m; Fig. 1) and at the end of the marsh (locations X = 151.4, 159.1 and 160.8 m; Fig. 1). Zelt and Skjelbreia's method, using three wave height meters, was used to separate incoming and reflected waves61. Hereafter, the difference in wave height in front of the marsh and at the landward end of the marsh was calculated. Subsequently, wave attenuation was quantified. The explicit contribution of vegetation was quantified by calculating the difference in wave attenuation for the experiments with (Supplementary Table 1; experiment SM-1–29) and without (Supplementary Table 1; experiment SM-30–37) vegetation. Wave attenuation by vegetation was calculated as the relative share of the incoming wave to enable comparison of different experimental runs and global scenarios.

The global contribution of breaking vegetation to wave attenuation is explored by a parameterization where wave attenuation is a response variable of the predictor variables exposure duration and incoming wave height. The assessed E. athericus (also Elytrigia atherica) is widespread in Northwestern European salt marshes, appearing in for example, Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, United Kingdom, France and Portugal (Extended Data Fig. 1). Stem height decrease of E. athericus through exposure duration in this large scale experiment (Fig. 3) is used as a conservative estimator of wave attenuation by above-ground vegetation under the most severe conditions (water depth to wave height ratio of 0.5).

To explore the contribution of salt marsh vegetation to wave attenuation globally, a GLM is applied with a Tweedie distribution and a log link function enabling the use of zeros and positive continuous data and proportional effects (for example, exponential decrease of wave attenuation with increasing exposure duration). The GLM is a strong predictor of the variability of wave attenuation by vegetation (pseudo-R2 = 0.97). Wave attenuation by vegetation decreased with increasing incoming wave height (β = −1.3258, SE = 0.185, z = −7.16, P < 0.001) and longer exposure duration (β = −0.0494, SE = 0.011, z = −4.42, P < 0.001). The GLM including incoming wave height and exposure duration is a globally significant predictor compared with the null intercept-only model (χ² = 59.26, df = 2, P < 0.001).

Key for the functionality of the marsh is the presence of salt-tolerant vegetation (halophytes), such as herbs, grasses and shrubs, which typically depend on salinity and inundation gradients4. The stem height of the vegetation is an important parameter for wave attenuation. Stem height is used as a proxy for wave attenuation as penetration of the stem in the water column is considered a strong predictor of wave attenuation, especially under extreme wave heights and water levels13. As a result, damage to vegetation by a decreasing stem height will have a direct impact on wave attenuation. The stem height and biomass of E. athericus are comparable with global marsh vegetation species that carry stems. At the end of our experiment, both the values of the damaged stem height and biomass were lower than global values for (other) salt marsh vegetation species (Extended Data Fig. 1) throughout the year.

Increasing wave attenuation with stem height can be expected both at low- and high-energy coasts, and is also found as a result of the flume experiment (Fig. 3). Yet for high-energy conditions, the higher risk of damage may cancel this benefit. Comparing Elymus with Spartina spp., locally a species growing at a lower elevation and regionally a dominant species in, for example, North America62,63, wave-induced damage to Spartina spp. is lower due to a higher flexibility26. Hence, attenuation can be expected to be higher as more stems and biomass persists (Fig. 3). Attenuation can also be expected to increase towards a lower salinity environment, since due to decreasing environmental stress, species diversity increases32,33,64.

70% of the salt marshes globally present are found in the temperate climate zone and therefore experience a winter season31. In general, the vegetation state at the end of the winter can be considered to yield a conservative estimate for vegetation stem height, as vegetation outside the winter season, for example, in the growing season, is more resistant26. Nevertheless, vegetation will be damaged during extreme storms, also when being more resistant early in the winter season, and might reduce wave attenuation during consecutive storms. Furthermore, E. athericus in our experiment can be considered to yield a conservative estimate of wave attenuation, because its breaking force is relatively weak compared to other salt marsh vegetation species35. Less vegetation damage leads to longer stems persisting and hence a higher wave attenuation capacity. In addition to this, we tested a single salt marsh zone, whereas salt marshes in general contain multiple zones that enhance the wave attenuation capacity65. Finally, only a small (70 m) part of the marsh was considered for wave attenuation. Our experiment enables future site-specific assessments of wave attenuation through vegetation height. These quantifications require extensive vegetation characterization (for example, spatiotemporal variable height). Effective methods for mapping vegetation heights are available through remote sensing, for example: high-resolution satellite imagery, LiDAR and Photogrammetry66,67,68. However, these techniques still require extensive field validation, especially if vegetation composition is variable.

Global wave attenuation along salt marsh foreshores is estimated by using global data layers as input for the statistical model. The approach to derive coastal profile data is based on a previous global study focused on wave-attenuation on vegetated foreshores29. We incorporated several updates: (1) elevation data from DeltaDTM was used30 instead of MERIT69 and vegetation extent data was updated31; (2) we filtered foreshores that are preceded by waterbodies with a minimal length of 1 km using a global water mask70 to assure that foreshore data are derived at the right location; (3) salt marsh areas sheltered behind higher landscape features, such as dunes, were excluded by flagging vegetation presence as false if the mean elevation in the vegetated zone is lower than the mean elevation of the preceding non-vegetated foreshore data points; and (4) false positives of vegetation presence in areas with steep transitions (for example, steep coastal slopes or cliffs) were corrected by applying an Interquartile Range filter on elevation within vegetated areas with a multiplier of 1.5. In general, this filtering resulted in an equal reduction across continents.

Following our experiment, nearshore waves at the seaward salt marsh edge are estimated to be 50% of the water depth, slightly lower than 55% estimated for very flat nearshore areas29,57,58. Using the global datasets, exposure duration during a single storm with a recurrence time (RPS) of 100 to 1,000 years was calculated for 1 km spaced transects where salt marsh vegetation is present.

Next, we combined the derived elevation profiles, salt marsh extents and significant wave heights with global storm tide hydrographs28, based on Euclidean distance, to calculate inundation depths and consequential exposure duration. Globally observed peak water levels (exceeding 1 m water depth) at the globally derived salt marsh locations remain for the larger part (around 90%) within the water level range applied in the wave flume. Globally observed exposure duration with water levels exceeding the threshold of 1.0 m (with Hm0 of 0.5 m) did not exceed 1,000 min (16.67 h), which is within the duration of the flume experiment (21 h). Subsequently, this information is used to calculate vegetation damage, specifically the expected decrease in stem height. As stem height damage in the Delta Flume experiment started already during the most tranquil hydrodynamic conditions (h = 1.50 m; Hm0 = 0.75 m), the initiation of stem height damage in the global model was set following previous controlled flume experiments28. In those previous experiments, which started at water depths of 1 m and wave heights of 0.1 m, the first significant stem damage was observed after water depths of 1 m and wave heights of 0.5 m on average27. Using the local storm surge hydrographs, exposure duration for vegetation damage is calculated over the time window during which wave heights at the marsh exceed 0.5 m from the start of the storm until the point in time when the highest waves occur, that is, at the peak of the storm surge hydrograph. The maximum incoming wave height at the marsh edge is calculated at the peak of the storm, with the most severe estimate for the wave height being 50% of the water depth29,57,58. Finally, wave attenuation at the peak of the storm is calculated using the GLM, as potentially the highest depth-limited waves occur at this point.

#3224. How do fireworks fly?

Have you ever wondered how fireworks fly? The science behind the flight of a firework is the same as that of a rocket, and a fantastic demonstration of Newton’s Third Law of Motion.

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.

Just like a rocket, a firework needs a force to overcome gravity. The force is provided by chemicals at the bottom of the firework. When the firework is lit, the chemicals combust, producing a large amount of hot gas inside the firework. The hot gas escapes downwards through a small hole at the bottom of the firework.

This strong downward force creates an equal and opposite upwards force, propelling the firework into the sky.

Thrust (upward force ) – this is the upward force created by the expulsion of hot gas from the firework.

Weight ( downward force ) – this is the force of gravity pulling the firework down to Earth. Weight increases with the mass of the firework.

Drag ( downward force ) – this is the force of air resistance acting in the opposite direction to the motion of the rocket. Air resistance increases with speed.

Resultant Force – for a firework to fly upwards, the thrust must be greater than the weight and drag force together.

RESULTANT FORCE = thrust – ( weight + drag )

The forces acting on a firework change as it travels through the air. The weight decreases as the fuel is used. When the firework is at its maximum speed, the drag force is greatest. When the thrust has been used up, the resultant force becomes downwards as the firework slows down and starts to fall.

One of the easiest ways to demonstrate how fireworks fly and Newton’s Third Law is to set up a rocket made from a film canister or a small effervescent vitamin tablet tube.

Adult supervision is needed for this activity.

Now you know how fireworks fly, discover the chemistry behind their colourful display.

A controlled burn in Sequoia National Park. Anthony Caprio / National Park Service

The use of small, controlled burns to clear out flammable underbrush has saved thousands of giant sequoias from devastating wildfires, new research finds.

The largest trees on Earth, sequoias can reach more than 300 feet tall and live for 3,000 years, but they grow only in a narrow band of the Sierra Nevada mountains in California, where hotter, drier weather has left forests increasingly prone to wildfires. With their thick bark and high canopies, the trees can withstand small fires, but the more intense blazes seen in a warmer climate have proved deadly for thousands of trees.

Together, the 2020 Castle Fire and 2021 KNP Complex Fire killed as many as 13,000 giant sequoias, or close to a fifth of all sequoias. According to the new study, the death count would have been considerably higher without the use of prescribed burns.

The new study used aerial and satellite imagery to examine more than 26,000 giant sequoias. It found the trees were roughly four times more likely to survive the Castle and KNP Complex fires if they had been treated with a prescribed burn in the previous decade. In total, prescribed burns saved an estimated 1,900 sequoias, according to the study, published in Nature Communications. Had burns been applied to every tree, the research found, another 3,900 sequoias would have been spared.

“If the goal is protecting giant sequoias, prescribed fire is an essential tool available to managers,” said lead author Dan Dixon, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of California, Davis.

One challenge for forest managers is that wildfire season is growing longer, narrowing the window during which prescribed burns can be safely applied. In 2022, a prescribed burn in Santa Fe National Forest in New Mexico got out of control, igniting the largest fire in state history. #3236. After Decades of Drought, Water Is Rising in the African Sahel

Dogondoutchi, Niger, is heavily reliant on groundwater, which is growing more abundant across the African Sahel. Sergey Mayorov / Zoonar

Warming has brought extreme rain to the parched Sahel, which is seeing aquifers refill as monsoons grow stronger. But heavier downpours alone cannot explain the groundwater revival, say hydrologists, who believe efforts to harvest rainfall may also be paying dividends.

Near-extinct oryx are returning. Farms are prospering as irrigation water reaches fields for the first time in decades. Farmers are even nurturing new trees on their land. Once a byword for drought and famine, the African Sahel region on the southern flank of the Sahara Desert now has more water than it has for decades. Wells are filling as water tables have risen by 13 feet or more in places. Lake Chad, which was one of Africa’s largest expanses of water before shriveling during the droughts, is recovering.

Over years of drought in the late 20th century, the sun hard baked the soils of the Sahel. Now, erratic but extreme rains are returning to this semi-arid region, causing lethal floods but also replenishing rivers, filling desert depressions, restoring water to dried riverbeds known as wadis, and sluicing rainwater off impermeable soils directly into aquifers. The process began in the 1990s but has accelerated in the past five years.

“Across the Sahel, from Ethiopia to Senegal, we have evidence of increased terrestrial water storage,” says Richard Taylor, a hydrogeologist at University College London who has led ground teams investigating the relationships between climate, land use, and groundwater recharge in the region.

But increased rainfall since the drought years of the 1970s and 1980s explains only some of the rewetting of the Sahel. It cannot fully account for the transformation, say researchers. Also driving the rewetting, they posit, are changes to the land surface, ranging from the internationally funded Great Green Wall project to the revival of traditional water harvesting methods and the chaos caused by jihadist militants, which has led to the abandonment of irrigation projects that once emptied rivers of their flows.

Much remains unclear in these remote lands on the edge of the Sahara Desert, but the trend seems set to be long-term, with climate scientists saying more — and more extreme — rainfall is on the way in the 21st century. As underground water reserves revive, some are forecasting an agricultural renaissance.

The Sahel is a band of semi-arid land around 500 miles wide that stretches across North Africa, from Senegal in the west to Ethiopia in the east, with a fast-growing population above 150 million. It is a transition zone between the Sahara Desert in the north and savanna grasslands and coastal forests to the south. Water is everything in the Sahel. Its rains are critical to human survival, but they have been sporadic and unpredictable since the close of a humid era around 5,000 years ago.

Shallow groundwater storage in February 2003 and February 2025, as compared to the average from 1948 to 2012. Source: NASA Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment. NASA / Adapted by Yale Environment 360

How much rain the Sahel gets depends on the West African monsoon, a wet wind that sweeps north from the Gulf of Guinea between June and September, and on how far the monsoon penetrates into the interior. Whether it reaches the 15th parallel north of the equator or stalls at the 10th parallel make the difference between a good year and a failed harvest, say hydrologists.

During the droughts of the late 20th century, as the monsoon repeatedly fell short, up to a million people died of hunger and disease. But now, with a strengthening monsoon, there is talk among scientists of the Sahel being on the verge of a new humid era.

That is good news for wildlife. With wadi systems reviving in Chad’s Ouadi Rimé-Ouadi Achim nature reserve, conservationists are documenting resurgent numbers of aardvarks, porcupines, badgers, and foxes, and the successful reintroduction of the scimitar-horned oryx, which was once declared extinct in the wild but now numbers more than 600 individuals. Elsewhere, West African giraffes are browsing on verdant vegetation in areas from which they had disappeared.

As Africa loses forest, its small farmers are bringing back trees. Read more.

As the rains return, farmers have boosted crop yields by nurturing the natural regrowth of some 200 million trees on formerly treeless fields in southern Niger, according to former World Resources Institute geographer Chris Reij. And, supported by new aid programs such as the World Bank’s regional recovery program PROLAC, they are re-establishing small-scale irrigation systems by tapping groundwater around resurgent oases in Chad, Niger, and Cameroon.

“In the past… there was not enough water to irrigate crops, and our harvests were meager. Now our lives have changed,” Achta, a farmer in Chad, said last year. She now has water to plant on four times as much land and has doubled her onion crop. The Bank says her story “reflects a broader transformation across the Lake Chad basin.”

Most places in the Sahel are seeing more rainfall. And that rain has become concentrated in extreme downpours from clusters of violent thunderstorms, known to meteorologists as Mesoscale Convective Systems, that occasionally bubble up during the monsoon season. Sahelian storm clusters are now rated as some of the most powerful on the planet, and they deliver most of the region’s rainfall. There has been a threefold increase in the frequency of extreme storms since the 1980s, according to a recent analysis.

Why the revival of the rains? An assessment published in 2021 by the U.N.’s Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change found “high confidence” among climate scientists that it is a result of strong warming in the North Atlantic, caused by a combination of global heating and a reduction, in Europe and North America, of sulphate aerosols from coal burning, which once created a thin haze that cooled the ocean. That cooling prevented the northward migration of the West African monsoon into the Sahel, resulting in drought. But the clean-up has warmed the ocean and pulled the monsoon back into the Sahel, strengthening its ability to produce deluges.

Flooding in Dakar, Senegal, following heavy monsoon rains in August 2022. John Wessels / AFP via Getty Images

The effects of these mega-storms are especially challenging in a region more used to disasters caused by the absence of rain. Niger, one of the world’s 10 poorest countries, saw tens of thousands die from famine during the late 20th century droughts. Now, it sees more deaths from floods than droughts. Fierce storms during the 2024 monsoon season inundated the capital, Niamey, and in rural areas washed away more than 150,000 mud-brick homes.

Sudden downpours are initially destructive, but they are a boon to underground water reserves. Falling onto compacted ground, the water does not initially infiltrate the soil but rushes into rivers and dry wadis, which speed the water’s flow into aquifers.

Hydrologists say that this indirect infiltration is most intense after heavy storms. “In some locations, rainfall intensity is more important than the annual rainfall amount in determining the magnitude of groundwater recharge,” says Taylor. This intensity, he says, is a major cause of the rapid rising of water tables — especially since, in the Sahel, most water drains not to the ocean but inland to depressions and lakes.

Not only are new ponds forming across the formerly arid lands; ancient networks of wadis are being restored. And Taylor’s network of researchers has found wells across the region refilling. Mean water tables in much of Niger have risen by some 13 feet, indicating a 15 percent increase in aquifer reserves.

This local data is now being corroborated by analysis from satellites that measure changes in land mass by tracking subtle changes in gravitational force from the land below. NASA’s Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment mission has found a sharp increase in gravitational force across the Sahel in the past two decades that hydrologists attribute to growing terrestrial water storage.

But rainfall and its intensity are only part of the story. Events on land also play a part.

Environmentalists have long held that the encroachment of agriculture into the Sahel during the 20th century caused “desertification” by removing natural vegetation, destroying soils, and allowing the advance of desert sands. But hydrologically, the effect may be the opposite because some crops can actually help the land retain water.

All vegetation absorbs moisture from the soil before transpiring it into the air. But shallow-rooted African grain crops such as millet and sorghum transpire less moisture than the deeper-rooted grasses they replace, leaving more moisture in the soil.

Dead trees in an arm of Lake Chad near Kiskawa, Chad, that was recently flooded. Joris Bolomey / AFP via Getty Images

An amplifying factor is the drought-driven crusting of soils, which has increased runoff by around 50 percent in the central Sahel in recent decades. Analysis by Christophe Peugeot, a hydrologist at the HydroSciences laboratory at the University of Montpellier in France, found that the droughts “have permanently transformed the region’s soils… fueling today’s floods.”

Much of this runoff ends up in Lake Chad, on the border between Nigeria, Cameroon, Chad, and Niger, which drains an area almost six times the size of California. The lake’s dramatic demise became a totemic image of the late 20th century hydrological decline of the Sahel. But less well known is that the lake is now recovering, as water inflows increase. Hydrogeologists at the University of N’Djamena in Chad report that in 2024 it swelled to a peak of more than 9,000 square miles, almost as big as in the 1960s and 10 times its size in the mid-1980s.

This growth is partly masked by large areas of saturated vegetation in the lake that do not show up in satellite images, say hydrologists. But as the lake grows, the aquifer beneath is also refilling.

Some researchers argue that deliberate efforts to capture rain in soils can also help explain the rising groundwaters across the region. One such effort is the Great Green Wall. Originally planned as a barrier of planted trees to prevent the advance of the Sahara, it has morphed into more grassroots efforts to control soil erosion by capturing rainfall and improving soil moisture, all of which can improve infiltration underground.

Many such projects revive traditional water-harvesting techniques, including the placement of stones along hill contours, digging small holes known as “zai pits” amid crops, and forming crescent-shaped mounds around trees.

Alpha Lo of the Climate Water Project, who highlighted the restored Sahelian groundwaters in a recent online analysis, argues that such techniques are “the primary reason.” But most researchers believe that, while locally effective in nurturing trees or crops, they are not happening at sufficient scale to explain the region-wide resurgence.

A final suggestion is that humans are extracting less water from rivers, leaving more to eventually infiltrate into aquifers. One driver of this trend is the decline of big irrigation projects that were constructed during the drought years to boost food production. For instance, Cameroon once largely emptied the River Logone, historically the biggest single source of water flowing into Lake Chad, to grow rice. But the SEMRY irrigation project reportedly diverts much less water now, due to poor management, aging infrastructure, and the outmigration of farmers.

Crescent-shaped mounds created to trap rainwater around gum acacia trees in Ninefouno, Niger. Rodrigo Ordonez / GLF

In areas around Lake Chad, farmers have also abandoned their irrigated fields because of Boko Haram, a militant jihadist group that continues to terrorize communities. Timmo Gaasbeek, food security policy officer at the Netherlands Ministry of Foreign Affairs, says the chaos in northern Nigeria “means that the water in the rivers is not caught upstream, and continues to flow into Lake Chad, where it contributes to higher water levels and more groundwater recharge.”

Most climate models predict a further increase in annual rainfall in the coming decades, due to global heating and the banishing of cooling aerosols from the North Atlantic. Taylor predicts that “the Sahel will experience particularly marked increases in extreme rain” that will “serve to increase groundwater recharge.”

Peugeot says that “the increased availability of water in valley bottoms could promote the development of irrigation, offering new prospects in several Sahelian regions.” Buoyed by such predictions, Nigeria is looking to restore the 165,000-acre South Chad Irrigation Project on the former shores of Lake Chad, which has long been abandoned for want of water.

But the road ahead will not be straightforward. Sahel rains will continue to be erratic on all timescales. Most immediately, climatologists are predicting that this year’s strong El Niño event could deliver drought in the eastern Sahel, but bumper rains and more floods further west.

Carbonylation is an important process used to produce aldehydes, carboxylic acids and esters1,2,3,4,5 that is usually catalysed by homogeneous metal complexes with ligands and iodide promoters6,7,8,9,10,11,12. Koch has reported that protons of mineral acids can also catalyse carbonylation13. Previous studies have demonstrated that Brønsted acidic zeolites can act as a heterogeneous, Koch-type catalyst for carbonylation of alcohols and olefins, but the products in this case are limited to tertiary carboxylic acids14,15,16,17,18. Here we show that targeted use of spatial confinement in a zeolite together with a high CO/olefin ratio can help break this limitation. The propylene and ethylene carbonylation selectivities decrease to 61% and 57%, respectively, as the CO/olefin ratio decreases to 24 and 90, respectively. Experiments in combination with theoretical calculations suggest that T2-O5 in eight-membered ring (8-MR) cavities of FER zeolite are the primary active sites for ethylene carbonylation and T3-O1 in ten-membered rings (10-MRs) are the primary active sites for propylene carbonylation. Our findings open new directions for olefin carbonylation and provide critical insights into the prerequisites for developing new zeolite-based carbonylation processes.

#3250. Russlanddeutsche in Kasachstan und der Umgang mit dem Erbe der Sowjetunion

Kasachstan ringt mit dem Erbe der Sowjetdiktatur. Vor 85 Jahren ließ Stalin Hunderttausende Menschen deportieren. Rund 400.000 Russlanddeutsche wurden nach Kasachstan verschleppt.

Sie will Geschichte lebendig halten. Yunna Kinereish ist 22 und studiert Public Relations in der modernen Glitzer-Hauptstadt Astana. Sie bezeichnet sich selbst als "kasachische Deutsche". "Ich bin Deutsche von meiner Nationalität her. Aber ich fühle mich auch als Kasachin, weil ich die Traditionen in meiner Familie lebe und diese Mentalität habe", sagt sie der DW. Yunna Kinereish ist eine von 223.000 Kasachstan-Deutschen, die heute in der zentralasiatischen Republik Kasachstan leben.

Yunna Kinereish engagiert sich bei der "Republikanischen Vereinigung der Deutschen Kasachstans - Wiedergeburt" in der Jugendgruppe "Diamant". Die Studentin hat kürzlich am Jugendtheater als Schauspielerin das Schicksal ihrer Vorfahren auf die Bühne gebracht. Das Schicksal der Russlanddeutschen, die zunächst ins Land gelockt wurden und ab dem 28. August 1941 unter Diktator Josef Stalin aus ihren angestammten Gebieten in der Sowjetunion zu Hunderttausenden gewaltsam nach Sibirien oder Kasachstan deportiert wurden.

Mindestens 200.000 von mehr als einer Million Menschen verloren durch Aussiedlung, Hunger und Zwangsarbeit ihr Leben. Genau vor 85 Jahren begann das Leid mit einem Erlass des Präsidiums des Obersten Sowjets der UdSSR. Eine direkte Reaktion auf den Überfall des nationalsozialistischen Deutschlands auf die Sowjetunion am 22. Juni 1941.

Menschen, die nichts mit dem Terror der Hitler-Truppen zu tun hatten, wurden kollektiv mitverantwortlich gemacht, sie wurden als Spione und Kollaborateure denunziert.

"Meine Familie ist Teil dieser Geschichte, von diesen schweren Zeiten. Sie wurden vertrieben aus dem ukrainischen Dorf Kleinwärter, einer Siedlung von Deutschstämmigen in der Ukraine." Die Schicksale, die sie und ihre Freundinnen auf die Bühne bringen, berührten sie sehr, sagt sie. "Für mich ist das nicht einfach, weil ich das tief im Herzen alles empfinde. Eigentlich möchte ich weinen. Ich muss aber stark sein, wenn ich im Deutschen Haus auf der Bühne stehe."

Das modern ausgestattete "Haus der Wiedergeburt" in der Hauptstadt Astana ist das Zentrum für deutsche Kultur. Es wird überwiegend durch Fördermittel der Bundesregierung in Berlin finanziert. Hier werden Sprachkurse gegeben, wird Sozialarbeit geleistet und Erinnerungskultur gefördert. Im Zentrum stehen dabei die Themen Deportation und Repression in der Sowjetzeit.

Yunna Kinereish steht nicht nur auf der Bühne, sie dokumentierte die Geschichte auch filmisch. Im vergangenen Jahr hat sie einen Film über inhaftierte deutsche Frauen im Lager Alzhir gedreht. Es war eines der berüchtigtsten Lager des sowjetischen Gulag-Systems.

In Kasachstan entstand während der stalinistischen "Säuberungen" ab 1937 das wohl größte Lagersystem der Sowjetunion. Allein das Lager Alzhir durchliefen 18.000 Frauen. Frauen, die dort inhaftiert wurden, nur weil ihre Männer als Volksfeinde oder Verräter verurteilt worden waren. Unter ihnen waren 167 deutschstämmige Frauen, deren Schicksal die Gruppe "Diamant" im Film zeigt.

Die Gedenkstätte für das Frauenlager Alzhir liegt direkt vor den Toren der neuen Hauptstadt Astana. Die 1,7-Millionen-Einwohner-Stadt ist am Reißbrett entstanden und erst seit 1997 Hauptstadt des größten zentralasiatischen Landes geworden. Kasachstan kann man als einen autoritär regierten Präsidialstaat bezeichnen.

Auf den Straßen von Astana herrscht Dauerstau. Fußgänger sind kaum zu sehen, Fahrradfahrer gibt es nicht. Astana wächst. Überall am Stadtrand: Neubauten. Dahinter öde Steppe, bis man nach etwa einer Stunde Autofahrt das Dorf Akmol erreicht.

Bevor man das eigentliche Museum betritt, durchschreitet man den "Bogen der Trauer"; eine riesige Stahlkonstruktion, die an das Leiden der Frauen und die Zwangsarbeit erinnern soll. Im Museum selbst: persönliche Gegenstände der inhaftierten Frauen, Modelle der primitiven Unterkünfte, Originaldokumente und Fotos.

Unter den Fotos ist auch das von Gertrud Platais, einer Frau mit deutschen Wurzeln, die lange in Alzhir inhaftiert war. Platais stammte aus Niederschlesien, das heute zu Polen gehört. Die Druckerin heiratete Karl Eduard Platais und nahm die sowjetische Staatsbürgerschaft an. Während des "Großen Terrors" in der Sowjetunion wurde ihr Ehemann 1938 verhaftet. Auch Gertrud Platais wurde verurteilt; als "Familienangehörige eines Verräters der Heimat".

Sie wurde nach Alzhir überstellt und musste dort bis 1946 als Stickerin Zwangsarbeit leisten, in einer Region, wo im Sommer 40 Grad Hitze herrschen und die Temperaturen im Winter auf minus 40 Grad fallen. Es folgte eine lebenslange Verbannung in das Großlager Karaganda. Erst 1956 wurde Gertrud Platais rehabilitiert. Ein Einzelschicksal, das den Terror des sowjetischen Systems auf kasachischem Boden dokumentiert.

Das weitaus größte Lagersystem war jedoch der sogenannte Karlag (Karagandinsches Besserungslager). Ein Komplex so groß wie das deutsche Bundesland Thüringen. Der Karlag war nicht nur ein Gefangenenlager, sondern ein riesiger Wirtschafts- und Landwirtschaftskomplex, den zwischen 1931 und 1959 fast eine Million Menschen durchliefen.

Allein zwischen 1940 und 1950 sollen hier rund 10.000 Gefangene gestorben sein: durch Hunger, Kälte, Krankheiten und die Folgen der Zwangsarbeit.

Das Museum liegt mitten in der kasachischen Steppe, in der Nähe der Stadt Karaganda, rund 250 Kilometer südöstlich von Astana. Die Gedenkstätte befindet sich im ehemaligen Verwaltungsgebäude des Lagersystems. Deutsche Historiker und Fachleute runzeln ein wenig die Stirn beim Besuch, denn in diesem Gebäude wurde nicht gefoltert oder drangsaliert, hier wurde verwaltet.

Zu sehen sind nachgebaute Verhör- und Folterzellen. Untermalt wird das ganze durch melodramatische Musik, die überall aus den Lautsprechern quillt: Inszenierung statt Authentizität. Wie auch die Alzhir-Gedenkstätte ist das Karlag Museum nicht besonders gut besucht. Selbst akademisch ausgebildete junge Menschen kennen die Gedenkstätte nicht unbedingt.

"Bei vielen Menschen und Teilen der Politik gibt es in Kasachstan kaum Interesse an einer Aufarbeitung der teils grausamen Geschichte des Landes", sagt Katherine M. Erdmann. Seit rund einem Jahrzehnt beschäftigt sich die amerikanische Anthropologin und Archäologin an der Nazarbayev-Universität in Astana mit dem Gulag-System. "Wir wollten herausfinden, wo die Lager genau waren, und wie man sie heute noch erkennen kann."

Erdman und ihre Studentinnen und Studenten setzten auf moderne Technik wie Drohnenüberflüge. Aber auch Befragungen und Recherchen am Boden gehören dazu. "Die Zwangsarbeit hat in großen Landesteilen die Landschaft geformt", sagt Erdman. Neben den primitiven Unterkünften entstanden oft Betriebe. Häuser wurden errichtet, mit Ziegeln, die in Zwangsarbeit hergestellt wurden. Ihre Erkenntnisse teilen die Forscher direkt mit Nachbarn und den lokalen Verwaltungen vor Ort.

"Oft begegnet uns nur Apathie oder bloße Ablehnung", sagt Erdman. Die breite Öffentlichkeit sei an Geschichtsaufarbeitung kaum interessiert. An die unrühmliche Vergangenheit wollen viele nicht erinnert werden in einer Zeit des wirtschaftlichen und gesellschaftlichen Aufstiegs, beklagt Erdmann.

International wolle man demonstrieren, dass man sich mit den dunklen Seiten der Geschichte befasse, aber oft erlebe sie mangelndes Interesse und sogar Ablehnung. Seit einem Jahrzehnt forscht die Wissenschaftlerin in Kasachstan. Für weitere Lehraufträge fehle die staatliche Finanzierung.
