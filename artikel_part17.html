The number of new homes started in the third quarter of this year fell by 13% compared with a year earlier, according to figures from a warranty and insurance provider.

#803. Wagner brothers begin new NBA chapter on different paths

After five years, brothers Franz and Moritz Wagner are no longer playing together for the Orlando Magic. In the new NBA season, their separation will likely mean the new "German" team is the Charlotte Hornets.

In 2021, when Franz Wagner joined the Orlando Magic to start his NBA career, his older brother Moritz was already on the team. Franz, who is four years younger, was selected eighth overall in the draft by the team who had signed Moritz a few months earlier.

It's not just their joint appearances in the NBA that have since turned the "Wagners" into something of a brand in basketball. Just last year, the NBA had a regular-season game in Berlin, partly because of the Wagner brothers.

Moritz is a successful podcast host and often has his brother on the show. Both have their own Instagram accounts, each with around 300,000 followers. In 2024, the German public broadcaster ZDF aired a four-part TV documentary about the brothers, chronicling their basketball history and close relationship.

In recent years, they've also celebrated success with the German national team. In 2023, they became world champions together and were then at the Olympic Games in Paris a year later. In 2025, Franz also won the European Championship title, while Moritz, sidelined by injury, watched on as a TV analyst.

This summer, their time together came to an end. After Moritz wasn't offered a new contract in Orlando, he accepted an offer from the Brooklyn Nets. He now lives in New York with his girlfriend, Maggie McQuade, who works in marketing for the NBA.

"I've already spent a lot of time in New York. It's kind of like my home in America," Wagner said on his podcast, adding that it reminds him of where he's from. "As far as the US goes, Brooklyn is actually very close to Berlin."

The call from the Nets proved a stroke of luck. "When we hung up the phone and both agreed, we both cried. Because you kind of realize that this move feels right somehow," Wagner said. "We're just staying home."

The Nets are considered one of the weaker teams heading into the upcoming season, leaving Wagner a key force in providing stability and helping to develop younger players.

Franz is staying in Orlando. Alongside Paolo Banchero, Franz is one of the team's two stars. The 25-year-old is on a massive contract that runs through 2030 and will earn him a reported total of $224 million (€199 million).

With Sean Sweeney hired as their new head coach for the upcoming season, the Magic are hoping to finally advance past the first round of the playoffs, having lost at that stage in each of the last three seasons.

Success is expected to come from a faster style of basketball than has been played in Orlando in recent years. In the new system, Franz Wagner will take on the role of point guard more prominently than before — moving the ball up the court, creating his own shots, and driving to the basket with speed — much like he does for Germany.

"We want to play tough, play together, play it fast and enjoy playing together," Wagner said during a preseason interview. "We got a little bit more space for our guys, to get in the paint and make plays, for themselves and for others."

However, he now has to manage without his older brother.

"I think we're both a little sad his time here has come to an end," Franz Wagner said in an interview with sports news site ClutchPoints. "I think, though, we're both really grateful for the time we had [together]. To play with a sibling in the NBA for so many years is super special. So we're just grateful for that."

Now that the Wagners’ time together in Orlando is history, German basketball fans may have to adjust their allegiances a bit for the upcoming season.

While many Germans became fans of the Dallas Mavericks during the era of German basketball icon Dirk Nowitzki, and interest in the Orlando Magic has been particularly high in recent years, that interest may spread in the coming season. #806. ‘You need persistence’: the Norfolk couple who took on environmental regulators three times – and won

Tim and Geli Harris have spent more than a decade fighting to protect Catfield Fen, a rare wetland in the Norfolk Broads

Polluters and regulators who fail to stop pollution must quake in their boots when they receive a letter from the lawyers for Tim and Geli Harris. The couple, who farm 460 acres in the Norfolk Broads, have now scored a hat-trick of landmark legal victories to save an internationally important wetland after they won again in the high court this month.

Lady Justice Lieven found that Natural England failed in its statutory duty to protect Catfield Fen from pollution after nitrates and phosphates washed from arable fields into one of the finest examples of calcareous fen in western Europe, home to 2,500 species including rarities such as the fen orchid, crested buckler fern, breeding bittern and swallowtail butterflies.

In a scathing assessment of Natural England’s inaction since the Harrises first reported the pollution to it in 2021, the judge said the cause of the pollution was “the most basic level of common sense” and required action by the government’s conservation watchdog.

The Harrises have spent 15 years and £1.5m of their own money taking action to hold to account government agencies for failing to protect the fen, which is part of a special area of conservation (SAC), an internationally important wetland protected by law. The marshes lie within the Broads national park.

“This is bigger than a little ditch in Norfolk,” says Tim. He fears that “regulatory inertia” is preventing pollution from being halted on waterways across the country. “Inertia is prevalent throughout all the regulatory bodies. It’s a landmark judgment because it says they can’t just do nothing.”

The couple are big fans of Dirty Business, the recent Channel 4 dramatisation of a maths professor and a retired detective’s battle against sewage pollution, the water companies and the Environment Agency.

“It’s just like that film when you go to a meeting,” Tim says. “The language is from [George Orwell’s] 1984. What we call the light side doesn’t have the money. The dark side does. That’s where the money is. Of course it is.”

Tim held senior roles in the shipping industry, collecting “the wages of sin”, before turning to environmental campaigning in retirement. “She’s the greenie,” he says of Geli, who grew up near the forests around Heidelberg, Germany, and has always loved the natural world.

They bought their farm, which came with a large chunk of Catfield Fen, in 1994, wanting to live closer to nature. They turned their arable fields into pasture to prevent soils and pollution from washing into the fen, and continue to tend English white park cattle, sheep and rare-breed Eriskay ponies.

“It is beautiful,” Geli says of the fen, which requires a careful rotational cut to preserve its rare plants, invertebrates and birdlife. “It’s a little bit of wildness. It’s mysterious. There are places here where we’ve never been – they are pure nature.”

Tim says: “It’s taken 1,000 years to create this and people could destroy it very quickly.” Geli adds: “If you water your plants with the wrong water, they die quite quickly.”

The Harrises take conservation advice from the experts at Natural England. “We get on well with Natural England, apart from suing them,” Tim says. “They all came to tea and we talked about everything but the thing [the legal case]. It was a very British occasion. They have some very good people. The trouble is too many no longer have fire in their bellies. They are apparatchiks. We respect them but we wish they had more ‘go’.”

Natural England said it accepted the court’s verdict that it had not done enough to protect Catfield Fen but it would not comment on ongoing legal proceedings.

In 2016, the couple won a public inquiry that demonstrated that too much groundwater was being taken from the valley by water companies and local farmers, causing Catfield Fen to dry out. They won a second battle in 2022, forcing the Environment Agency to limit the abstraction of water close to other nature-rich sites on the Norfolk Broads.

Their principles have cost them local friendships. They say National Farmers’ Union reps rejected their application for membership of the local branch, saying half their members would resign, with farmers upset at restrictions on their abstraction of water to boost lucrative crops such as potatoes. “We’re farmers, we’re not anti-farmer,” Tim says.

Since their 2022 legal victory, the Harrises have noticed an improvement in the water levels and biodiversity of Catfield Fen’s dykes. Geli says: “We are a Noah’s ark until things get better and nature can spread outwards from here again.”

The fen and its wildlife may be in a better condition under their stewardship, but they are not confident that nature is winning. “The evidence this summer was that the insect numbers are going south, according to the experts who survey here,” Tim says. “We are not complacent; we are very worried.”

The judge is to make further rulings on what Natural England must do to stop the pollution of Catfield Fen. For the Harrises, the answer is simple: arable farmers must be paid to put in buffer strips of grassland between their ploughed fields and the ditches leading to the Broads to reduce the nitrate-filled soils washing from arable fields into the fen.

More widely, Tim argues that regulatory bodies must simply put their resources into monitoring and compliance. He fears most breaches of environmental rules go unnoticed because of a lack of both.

The Harrises say they do not want to spend their time campaigning. “I don’t want any of this, believe me,” Tim says, gesturing at the box files that take up two walls of shelving. “I’m 79. I want to read books.”

He believes the Broads Authority, the government agency responsible for the national park, should be leading the fight against pollution. Nevertheless, they are considering another legal challenge to stop the Broads from being polluted.

Their stewardship of Catfield Fen will continue after they go. Their daughter will take on the farm, and she is a lawyer by training. So is their granddaughter. “The agencies are not getting rid of the Harrises when we die,” Tim laughs.

Do they hope their example can inspire others to believe they can act to protect nature and clean water? “You need enormous persistence,” Tim says.

“You need passion,” Geli adds. “Evil can happen when people do nothing.”

Decades of research show why these guardrails matter. School closures are politically unpopular and have provoked fierce community resistance, including hunger strikes. School closures are also not neutral: In many cities, closures disproportionately affect Black and Latino students and can exacerbate geographic inequality in educational access. Students lose connections with teachers and peers at an age when they need stability the most.

The damage can extend beyond enrolled students. Schools are embedded in neighborhood ecosystems. Their loss affects families, alumni and former staff, producing what scholars call “institutional mourning.” People grieve the loss of schools the way they do people, and school closures can lead to neighborhood blight.

For an equity-focused district, the first question should therefore be whether closure is truly necessary. The second question should ask how to prevent consolidations from inflicting harms. The standard argument for school closures is financial: Fewer campuses should mean lower costs. Yet research shows that, at least in California, school closures do not necessarily improve district finances. Savings can be offset by the costs of receiving more students, providing transition support and transporting children farther from home.

California law requires districts to consider operating costs and savings, yet closure plans often focus only on the immediate savings while overlooking longer-term costs. Before closing a school, LAUSD must consider both the short- and medium-term costs and benefits, and ensure that closures or consolidations do, in fact, lead to savings.

Some consolidation may be unavoidable. In these cases, LAUSD should treat attendance boundaries and assignments as tools for integration. Racist housing and mortgage-lending practices helped produce today’s segregated neighborhoods and schools. In Los Angeles, attendance zones have helped reserve some of the best-resourced schools for affluent children, while segregation perpetuates disparities in academic outcomes. There is strong evidence that desegregation improves student outcomes at a scale few other policies match.

#833. Brutal, revolutionary empire builder: Genghis Khan's legacy

Berlin is host to a major exhibition on the founder of the Mongol Empire and his successors. Here's how Genghis Khan disrupted the course of history.

Genghis Khan is one of history's most radical figures. A blood-thirsty invader whose conquests caused the deaths of an estimated 40 million people, he was also a visionary political thinker who established the largest contiguous land empire in history.

The founder of the Mongolian Empire died in 1227. Ahead of the 800th anniversary of his death, Berlin's James-Simon-Galerie explores his legacy and the "Mongol Century" (1250–1350) in the exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols."

The ruler remains "incredibly relevant" to this day, says Matthias Wemhoff, the director of the exhibition project.

"What the era of Genghis Khan — the 'Mongol century,' if you will — vividly demonstrates is how quickly established structures can collapse, how rapidly vast empires can vanish from the map and how swiftly the entire landscape can be utterly transformed," the archaeologist tells DW.

The charismatic leader's unprecedented impact on the world also evokes present-day disruptive individuals who are triggering sudden, unpredictable societal shifts, says Wemhoff: "There's always been this question: Are world politics shaped by individuals or rather by groups and sociological processes?"

In this case, the answer is clear, says the museum director, who also co-wrote a 2026 book on the nomadic leader.

"If Genghis Khan hadn't existed, the course of world history would truly have been entirely different — so much of it is inextricably linked to his person," Wemhoff says.

Genghis Khan was born as Temujin around the year 1162. His father, a Mongol chieftain called Yesugei, died when he was still a child. His family was abandoned by its tribe. To secure his familial position, Temujin killed his older half-brother, Bekter, when he was still a young teenager.

Thanks to his charismatic personality, he built alliances and found followers who helped him become the sole ruler on the Mongolian steppe. He formally adopted the title of Genghis Khan in 1206.

At its peak, the Mongol Empire covered approximately 24 million square kilometers (about 9.3 million square miles), stretching from China and Korea to parts of Eastern Europe and the Middle East, and accounting for over 16% of the Earth's total land area.

Wemhoff lists various innovations under his rule: For one, Genghis Khan transformed warfare. Commanders were promoted for skill, discipline and loyalty rather than based on aristocratic rank or tribal loyalties. Soldiers were organized into decimal units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000.

Despite being illiterate by modern standards, he also pushed for the introduction of the first written script for the Mongolian language, which allowed to build a state bureaucracy, track population and tax registers, record decrees, issue legal decisions and handle diplomacy.

A set of laws were established that replaced fragmented tribal customs with a unified legal system.

Trade was simplified through his adoption of a paper currency.

A policy of religious freedom was also established across the Mongol Empire, allowing all individuals to practice their chosen faith without state coercion.

Before the Mongol Empire, the Silk Road was fragmented by local conflicts and heavy taxes. Mongol rule placed the entire route under a single authority, making long-distance travel vastly safer.

The Mongol Empire is known as a period of relative political stability, economic growth and cultural exchange, an era historians call the "Pax Mongolica."

But "it all came with incredible levels of cruelty. The millions of people who fell victim to the Mongols — the mass executions, the abuse of women — this happened on a scale previously unheard of," notes Wemhoff.

The exhibition opens with a raid that happened after Genghis Khan's death, but took place just 260 kilometers (around 160 miles) away from Berlin: the 1241 Battle of Legnica, in Silesia, Poland.

It embodies the brutality and intensity of the Mongols' methods.

There came the "highly mobile, agile Mongols on small horses — lightly armored in lamellar gear, wearing helmets that left the face exposed, and wielding bows capable of firing 20 or more arrows in a single minute," points out Wemhoff.

European knights, heavily armored and mounted on large, massive warhorses, didn't stand a chance against their enemies.

"When a hail of arrows rained down on them, the horses eventually went down, and the knights ended up lying on the ground, stuck on their back, much like a June bug," he adds. "The two styles of combat were simply mismatched."

The Mongols' western campaigns shifted historic power centers in a way that still impacts world politics today.

For example, after the Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus', which was the first East Slavic state, its thriving capital Kyiv was reduced to a depopulated ruin. Moscow became a key regional power, partly because it chose to cooperate with the Mongols rather than pursue resistance.

"In our Western European collective memory, the Mongol invasion plays hardly any role. It's something of an anecdote," says Wemhoff, noting that the era of Genghis Khan and his immediate successors is barely mentioned in standard Western European school curricula.

Germans are mainly familiar with the name of the empire's founder through the 1979 Euro-disco pop band Dschingis Khan, which represented West Germany at the Eurovision Song Contest with a campy song of the same name that reduced complex history to extreme tropes.

But in eastern Europe, the Mongol invasions "changed everything fundamentally," says Wemhoff. "And so fundamentally that we still feel the consequences of it today."

The exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols" runs from October 21, 2026, to April 18, 2027.
