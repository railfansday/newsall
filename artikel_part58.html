#2851. In India, a Surge of Solar Is Keeping Coal Power in Check

A solar plant in Rajasthan, India. Jitendra Parihar / Thomson Reuters Foundation

India, a country of nearly 1.5 billion people, has long relied on coal to power its growing economy. But over the last two years, it has seen a remarkable shift: The rapid buildout of renewable energy has halted the growth of coal.

Coal output is no higher than it was two years ago, even though power demand has continued to surge, according to an analysis from the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air. After decades of growth, coal is finally hitting a wall in India. The result is that, since 2024, power sector emissions have stayed roughly flat.

Renewable energy, mostly solar, is meeting rising power demand. Over the last two years, India added enough renewables to power Switzerland, allowing it to meet a 7 percent growth in demand without ramping up coal.

Just a decade ago, solar power was scarcely used in India. Now, its solar capacity is growing by 40 percent a year, and the country is on the verge of becoming the first major nation to power its industrialization largely with solar.

Even though coal output has stagnated, India is continuing to add coal power plants. That means the cost of maintaining the fleet is rising even as the average coal plant runs for fewer hours.

And despite coal power flatlining, India still saw its overall carbon dioxide emissions tick up in the first half of this year, owing largely to the growing production of steel and cement, two sectors that are heavily reliant on coal.

Active time: 30 minutes | Total time: 35 minutes

Trim the base of the cauliflower, and cut the florets and stems into 1 1/2-inch chunks. Place about half the cauliflower into the bowl of a food processor and pulse, chopping to the size of rice. Scrape into a bowl and repeat with the remaining cauliflower. Reserve.

#2854. Natural compound could fight rheumatoid arthritis at its source

Scientists have identified a natural plant compound that could offer a surprising new approach to fighting rheumatoid arthritis. In experiments with rats, the substance reduced joint swelling, protected cartilage, and calmed harmful immune activity. Researchers also discovered that it works through an unexpected mechanism involving the way the body processes fats.

The findings, published in the journal Engineering, center on a compound called obakulactone (OL), a naturally occurring tetracyclic triterpenoid extracted from Phellodendri cortex, a type of plant bark. Tetracyclic triterpenoids are plant chemicals characterized by a structure containing four interconnected rings.

The researchers found that obakulactone helps restore balance to unsaturated fatty acids, which play important roles in cellular function and inflammation. It does this by promoting the breakdown of a protein called acyl coenzyme A thioesterase 1 (ACOT1), using the cell's natural protein disposal system, known as the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway.

By identifying this previously underexplored molecular target, the study suggests that changing how cells process fatty acids could become a new strategy for treating rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

Natural Compound Reduces Arthritis Inflammation and Joint Damage

To investigate the compound's effects, scientists created a rat model of rheumatoid arthritis using complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA), a substance that triggers a strong inflammatory response resembling certain features of the disease.

The animals received obakulactone for 21 days at three different daily doses: low (50 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), medium (100 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹), and high (200 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹). These amounts were calculated according to body weight.

The results were encouraging. Rats treated with the compound experienced significantly less joint swelling, while the structure of their cartilage and synovium returned toward normal. The synovium is the thin membrane lining joints that produces lubricating fluid and can become severely inflamed in rheumatoid arthritis.

The treatment also improved disease-related changes in the thymus and spleen, two organs that play important roles in the immune system.

Beyond these physical improvements, researchers observed major changes in immune activity. Obakulactone reduced elevated levels of CD3⁺ T cells and CD68⁺ macrophages in joint tissues. These immune cells normally help defend the body, but they can contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue damage when the immune system becomes overactive.

The compound also shifted macrophages away from an inflammation-promoting M1 (CD86) state toward a more inflammation-resolving M2 (CD206) state. At the same time, it suppressed the development of CD4⁺ T cells into Th17 cells, a type of immune cell associated with autoimmune inflammation.

Blood tests provided further evidence of these effects. Obakulactone reduced several inflammatory signaling proteins, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α. These proteins, known as cytokines, help immune cells communicate but can also intensify inflammation when produced in excessive amounts.

The treatment also lowered rheumatoid arthritis related blood markers, including rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (CCP-Ab), C-reactive protein (CRP), and matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3). The reductions became more pronounced as the dose increased.

Scientists Uncover a Surprising Link Between Fat Metabolism and Arthritis

One of the study's most intriguing findings involved fatty acids, molecules that are commonly associated with nutrition and energy storage but also influence inflammation throughout the body.

Using several advanced analytical methods, including metabolomics, MALDI mass spectrometry imaging, and proteomics, the researchers examined how obakulactone affected the chemical processes inside tissues.

These techniques allowed them to investigate changes in small molecules, map their distribution within tissues, and measure differences in protein levels.

The analysis revealed that the compound helped correct abnormalities in the production and breakdown of unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., arachidonic acid, linoleic acid, and α-linolenic acid).

These fatty acids are important components of cell membranes and can serve as raw materials for molecules that regulate inflammation. Arachidonic acid, in particular, is involved in producing several powerful inflammatory signals.

In the rats with arthritis, obakulactone helped bring these disrupted metabolic processes back toward a healthier balance. This suggested that its benefits extended beyond simply suppressing immune activity.

A Hidden Protein Emerges as a Potential Treatment Target

To understand exactly how the compound worked, the scientists conducted additional laboratory experiments involving rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (SFs).

These cells help maintain the tissue lining the joints. However, in rheumatoid arthritis, they can become abnormally aggressive, multiplying excessively, releasing inflammatory substances, and contributing to cartilage damage and tissue scarring.

Obakulactone slowed the growth of these cells, reduced their production of inflammatory cytokines, and encouraged apoptosis, the controlled process through which the body eliminates unwanted or damaged cells.

The researchers then investigated which protein the compound was acting on directly.

Using cellular thermal shift assays, microscale thermophoresis, and surface plasmon resonance, they identified ACOT1 as a direct molecular target of obakulactone.

The binding measurements produced a dissociation constant (Kd) of (6.18 ± 0.26) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by microscale thermophoresis (MST)) and (6.34 ± 0.38) μmol·L⁻¹ (analyzed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR)).

These closely matching results provided evidence that the compound physically interacts with ACOT1. A dissociation constant describes how strongly two molecules bind to one another, with lower values generally indicating tighter binding.

Importantly, obakulactone did more than bind to the protein. It promoted ACOT1's destruction through the ubiquitin‒proteasome system, a cellular mechanism that marks proteins for removal and breaks them down.

This process reduced the activity of several downstream mechanisms associated with inflammation and abnormal cell behavior.

How Targeting One Protein Can Disrupt Inflammation

When ACOT1 was broken down, levels of another enzyme called stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) also decreased. This enzyme helps regulate the production of certain unsaturated fatty acids.

The changes subsequently suppressed two major cellular signaling networks: Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT).

These pathways act like internal communication systems, carrying signals that influence how cells grow, survive, and respond to their surroundings. When their activity becomes abnormal, they can contribute to inflammation and excessive tissue growth.

By interfering with these signals, obakulactone reduced inflammation and fibrosis, a process involving excessive buildup of scar-like connective tissue, in rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts.

Additional experiments, including rescue tests and studies using molecular inhibitors, strengthened the evidence that ACOT1 was central to these effects.

The researchers confirmed that the compound's ability to reduce inflammation, slow abnormal cell proliferation, and encourage apoptosis depended on its influence over ACOT1, the arachidonic acid pathway, and the downstream JAK-STAT/PI3K-AKT signaling pathways.

Together, the findings point to a potential way of disrupting the biological processes that sustain arthritis inflammation rather than focusing exclusively on suppressing immune responses.

A Potential New Direction for Rheumatoid Arthritis Treatment

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues, particularly the joints. It affects approximately 1% of people worldwide and can cause persistent pain, swelling, stiffness, and progressive joint damage.

Because it is a systemic disease, rheumatoid arthritis can also affect organs and tissues beyond the joints.

Although existing medications can reduce symptoms and slow disease progression, they do not work equally well for everyone, and some can cause serious side effects. This makes the search for additional treatment approaches especially important.

The new findings suggest that obakulactone could become a candidate for future rheumatoid arthritis drug development. They also identify ACOT1 as a potentially valuable treatment target and highlight the importance of unsaturated fatty acid metabolism in controlling inflammation.

However, the results remain preclinical. The compound has been tested in rats and laboratory cells, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether it is safe and effective in humans.

Even with that limitation, the discovery opens an intriguing possibility: targeting the metabolic machinery that helps drive inflammation could provide another way to combat rheumatoid arthritis and potentially protect joints from lasting damage.

#2855. Why going on a 'say no' retreat is a gift to your future self

To free up more time in your life, writer Caroline Sylge proposes a creative idea: Give yourself a "say no" retreat. It's a day to look at your calendar and thoughtfully decline obligations in the next few weeks and months. Juan Moyano/ Moment RF/ Getty Images hide caption

This story is adapted from Life Kit's weekly newsletter. Sign up here to get expert advice on lifestyle topics like health, money, relationships and more.

How is your to-do list looking for the next few weeks? If it's already full of obligations and tasks you've been dreading, and ramping up toward the holidays, you might need a "say no" retreat.

That's one of dozens of creative ideas in Caroline Sylge's book, How to Retreat: A Guide to Stepping Away from the Everyday to Find Space for Yourself, published this spring.

A "say no" retreat is a day to brainstorm all the things you want to say no to in your life in the coming weeks and months, she says. Then, you'll spend the last part of your day saying no to a few of those things.

Sylge, who has spent the last 30 years attending and organizing health and wellness retreats around the world, developed this exercise to help people reclaim their time.

"We often say yes for reasons that have nothing to do with what's good for us, because we feel like we should, don't want to seem difficult or fear missing out," Sylge says. "Saying 'no' is uncomfortable, but is a brave act of self-respect."

Devoting a whole day to saying "no" may seem like a wild idea, but with the holidays on the horizon, it may make room in your life for the things that matter. Here's how to do it.

Before you start: Pick a day on your calendar to do your "say no" retreat. That might mean calling out of work or moving obligations. Then find a comfy place in your home to reflect.

Right when you wake up, make a list of things you want to say no to in your life. What immediately comes to mind? Name anything obvious you don't want to do. For example, coordinating a big potluck for your book club or making a complicated dish for that dinner party on Saturday.

Then eat some breakfast and get yourself into nature to give yourself some time to think about your list. Maybe take a bike ride or a walk.

After your break, come back to your designated retreat space and build on your list. Add to that initial "say no" list by thinking about your upcoming days, weeks and months. Look at your digital calendar if you need to, but make sure your notifications are turned off, Sylge writes in her book.

Review all your requests, appointments, volunteer work, scheduled phone calls, work and social commitments, and memberships.

Is there anything you've planned that you're dreading? Are there tasks you've given yourself that you don't actually have to do or even want to do?

That might be sitting at a time-consuming hair appointment, hosting a playdate at your house, traveling to spend Thanksgiving with your husband's family or moderating a panel discussion for a work event.

Jot down notes — and if you'd like, reasons why you are saying no to a task.

Take lunch, then spend the afternoon reviewing your list — and take action. It's time to start saying no. You probably won't be able to get to all the nos on your list today, but you can pick a small, easy win, Sylge says. Maybe you just cross the arduous task of planting tulip bulbs off your to-do list … forever. You can just enjoy your neighbor's tulips next spring.

Now, you'll also need to start thinking about the harder nos — the ones that involve saying no to someone you care about or work with.

Sylge says instead of saying no outright, you can try delaying your yes. For example, "I'd love to, but can we plan it for a few weeks later?" You could also suggest alternatives. Instead of cooking for your friends, could you order takeout?

Don't underestimate the power of "crafting a kind decline," Sylge says. It "takes effort, but it saves time in the long run."

#2861. Microplastics in Soil a ‘Trojan Horse’ for Toxins

This article was originally published by The Guardian and is reproduced here as part of the Climate Desk collaboration.

Microplastics act like a “Trojan horse,” delivering pollutants, pesticides, and bacteria through the soil, a five-year multinational research project has found.

The E.U.-funded project, which has so far resulted in 22 peer-reviewed studies, found microplastics in the soil of all 227 agricultural fields it tested across 11 European countries. The researchers said this poses a global threat, with healthy soils essential for life on Earth.

In many cases, this contamination reflected current farming practices as well as historical land use, demonstrating that plastic pollution can persist for many years.

The researchers also found that microplastics could interact with other agricultural stressors such as pesticides and veterinary drugs.

One study in Switzerland found that fields with the highest levels of tire-wear particles also had the highest levels of other toxic chemicals and metals.

Microplastics can create new microbial habitats on their surfaces. Known as the plastisphere, these surfaces form a hotspot for interactions between plastics, microbes, and agrochemicals, the researchers found.

An increase of antibiotic-resistant genes was found within plastispheres when compared with controls. Pesticides appeared to amplify this effect further.

Edoardo Puglisi, a partner of the project and professor of microbiology at the Catholic University of the Sacred Heart in Piacenza, Italy, said the smaller the microplastics were, the more they tended to adsorb pollutants, microbes and DNA, leading to a “Trojan horse effect that can potentially increase the diffusion of pathogens and antibiotic-resistance genes.”

This Trojan horse effect can have an impact on earthworms and soil organisms by disrupting critical processes such as nutrient cycles, one study found.

Earthworms play a vital role in shaping the composition of the soil microbiome as well as the ecological functions of soils.

The E.U.-funded Minagris research project, in collaboration with the Countryside and Community Research Institute at the University of Gloucestershire, also found microplastics could affect plant growth and function. One study found that higher concentrations of microplastics reduced leaf area, chlorophyll content, photosynthetic efficiency, and plant biomass in lettuce.

When drought conditions were added, these effects became even more pronounced, with plants performing less well than under either stress alone.

Environmental assessments often consider pollutants individually, but the findings show pollutants can behave differently when they occur together. The researchers say policy needs to recognize that plastic contamination is part of a wider picture of soil degradation and pollution.

In an effort to reduce plastic pollution, there has been an increase in the use of biodegradable plastics. However, the project found biodegradable plastics were not automatically safer and could still degrade into microplastics, potentially causing ecological damage.

“Once these plastics fragment into the ground, they’re practically impossible to remove, acting as vectors for agrochemicals and altering critical soil ecosystems,” said Esperanza Huerta Lwanga, a research associate in soil physics at Wageningen University in the Netherlands.

“To protect long-term food production and soil health, policy must catch up. We urgently need standardized plastic monitoring, full manufacturer transparency, and risk assessments that evaluate through different species how microplastics interact with co-pollutants.”

People ask me all the time about exercise tech. Do I have any tools, gadgets, gizmos, or devices I’m using or would recommend? After all, the world of fitness tech has exploded. Maybe it’s a kayak. Maybe it’s a standup paddle board. Maybe it’s a surfboard or windsurfing rig. Most people reading this will have some level of water access—and I urge you all to avail yourselves of it. There’s nothing quite so Primal and human as traversing a natural body of water. For as long as hominids have been bipeds, we’ve been supporting heavy loads and walking for miles underneath them. A million years ago, it was the haunch of an antelope we successfully stole from a larger predator thrown over the shoulder. 2000 years ago, it was 50 pounds of armor, weapons, and personal affects piled on our backs on some campaign in the Mediterranean. Whether you were a farmer, explorer, naturalist, prospector, soldier, or parent, you were carrying heavy things while walking across the environment.

Some call this rucking, some call it loaded carries. The point is that we are designed to carry heavy loads and walk. It’s the entire reason why we have opposable thumbs (for grabbing) and bipedalism (to free up our hands and give us an upright posture that can better support a load).

Footwear is everything. It’s how we interact with the world and how we generate force—by pushing off to start the battle against gravity. I recommend Peluva, of course—my own footwear brand that I built from the ground up to be the closest approximation to actually going barefoot. I’d love it if we could go barefoot everywhere, but the modern world we’ve built simply doesn’t allow it. Most of the surfaces we walk, run, and move across aren’t meant for bare feet.

#2868. Achy from sitting at a desk all day? Try doing this

Back pain, eye strain, sluggishness, shoulder aches, restlessness.

If you sit in front of a computer for long periods of time, whether it's for work or leisure, you've probably experienced one of these symptoms.

But there are ways to relieve some of this discomfort — and even combat some of the health risks that may come with prolonged sitting. That includes walking breaks, ergonomic solutions and quick, simple stretches.

Here's a roundup of tips that have been previously featured on Life Kit.

We know that a sedentary lifestyle isn't great for human health. Studies have shown it can lead to fatigue, weight gain and back and joint pain — and it's been linked to chronic diseases like obesity, heart disease and cancer. Yet adults in the United States spend an average of 9.5 hours sitting per day, involved in activities tied to computers, laptops and phones.

But there may be a way to mitigate its harms. In 2023, researchers at Columbia University Medical Center set out to find the least amount of movement you need to counteract the dangers of prolonged sitting. And they found that walking for five minutes every 30 minutes was the most effective strategy.

In 2023, our colleagues at NPR's Body Electric tested out this five-minute walking exercise on our NPR listeners. Over 20,000 participants joined the challenge — and reported higher productivity and lower fatigue.

Want to give this walking challenge a try? Get instructions from Body Electric — and use this worksheet to keep track of your walking breaks.

Anyone who works at a desk knows how easy it can be to slip into a slouch. If we're not careful, that mid-back rounding can creep into all of our sitting time and create pain by putting stress on the middle section of our spine.

This movement above seeks to relieve that stress and encourage flexibility by mimicking the natural movements of the spine — side bending, bending backward and rotating, says Vinh Pham, a physical therapist. The idea is to get the body comfortable with these motions so that when you encounter them in real life, they're not painful.

To do this modified movement, which Pham calls the 3D T-spine rotation, stand up and drop your right foot behind the left, about a foot apart. Starting with the right arm straight up and the left arm behind you, as if you're shaking the hand of the person behind you, simultaneously drop your arms — still in a straight position — and twist toward your right foot. Move them back to their original position and hold for two seconds. Repeat the movement ten times before switching legs and doing the same exercise on the other side.

For more pain-relieving stretches, read this story.

When you stare at a screen for long periods of time, your eyes have to work hard. They must first turn inward to look at a close subject, then adjust the focus for short vision and hold that position.

Digital eye strain, as eye doctors call it, can cause eye fatigue, headaches or blurry vision. Try the exercise below to relieve that strain.

Fidgeting is a sign you need to break out of a stagnant posture, says Katy Bowman, a biomechanist and author of Rethink Your Position and Movement Matters. Even a position that feels comfortable at first can begin causing back pain, headaches or stiffness if you hold it long enough.

So listen to your body and mix things up. Alternate between sitting, standing, reclining and moving. That might mean opting to stand in the back of a conference room during a big staff meeting, taking a call while going on a walk or reading a report with your feet propped up in the break room.

The goal is to put yourself in configurations that contort your body in different ways. They should be "adaptive and dynamic" and easy to "change in and out of," says Leada Malek, a physical therapist and author of the book Science of Stretch.

Don't worry too much about what is "good" and "bad" posture. Both Malek and Bowman agree on a common phrase among physical therapists: "Your best posture is your next posture."

Your monitor screen should be at eye level so you're not craning your head down to see. And, your keyboard and mouse should be low enough so you're not shrugging your shoulders and lifting up your arms like a praying mantis to type and click.

Meg Honan, a professional ergonomist, says this common "head down and arms up" position sets you up to feel "achy, sore and tired." To combat this uncomfortable and oftentimes painful position, try repositioning your tech. Bring your laptop or desktop monitor screen up to eye level with a stack of books or some other platform.

Place your keyboard and mouse at a lower height where you don't have to shrug your shoulders to use them. If your keyboard is attached to your laptop, consider connecting an external keyboard and mouse so you can have the computer screen at your eye level and lower your arms. External mouses don't just help lower our arms, but they also prevent the constant strain of using a trackpad.

#2871. Is Keto Dead?

Keto dieting’s popularity rose and peaked in the late 20-teens, declined for a few years, and has more or less held steady since 2023. So says Google Trends anyway. Plenty of folks, including friends of mine, have been keto for many years, some even predating the keto boom. New books are still being written. Bloggers are still putting out keto recipe guides.

Keto is here to stay. It just looks a little different, as I say in the video.

Remember, the liver starts producing ketones when insulin is low and liver glycogen becomes depleted. Many roads lead to this outcome. I still believe that keto is one of the best tools any of us have in our metabolic toolboxes for losing weight, tackling insulin resistance, and building that all-important metabolic flexibility.

#2872. Why Germany Is Building an Ark for U.S. Climate Data

Hard disks stacked inside the German Climate Computing Center in Hamburg. Axel Heimken / dpa / Alamy Live News

By Christian Schwägerl • September 17, 2026

As researchers around the world begin work on the next U.N. climate assessment, fears are growing that the Trump administration could meddle with critical U.S. climate data. In response, scientists are preparing to transfer vast troves of that data to repositories in Germany.

In a modern five-story building in the center of Hamburg, Germany, technicians are preparing to install several new “racks” — gray metal casings that hold computing hardware — alongside the scores that already line the narrow, dimly lit corridors of the German Climate Computing Center (DKRZ), one of the world’s largest repositories for climate simulation data. The new equipment will add about 5 petabytes of storage capacity to the 254 petabytes of data already stored here. One petabyte of data is equivalent to the storage space needed for roughly 200 million high-resolution images.

Under normal circumstances, the DKRZ’s mission is to provide computing power and storage to the German climate science community. But these new racks will serve a different and highly sensitive purpose: They will store backup versions of climate simulations from the other side of the Atlantic. The operation aims to ensure that essential work performed by U.S. scientists for the next major U.N. climate assessment, due in 2029, cannot be meddled with or compromised by the administration of President Trump, which has slashed funding for climate research, buried scientific reports, removed references to human-caused warming from dozens of government websites, and sought to prevent satellites from collecting key climate data.

Leaders of German science organizations publicly cite these actions as their primary motivation for creating backups. The German Research Foundation, the country’s main science funding body, believes that the Trump administration could block European access to data and research findings stored in the U.S. “The deletion of data has also become a possibility,” says the foundation’s program manager, Kathrin Winkler, “and there is a risk that [data] could be manipulated to skew findings in a desired direction.”

Trust in U.S. science policy has fallen to a low point in Europe, and fears of losing scientific data are mounting. The U.S. government quit both the U.N. Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) and the U.N. Framework Convention on Climate Change in January, though U.S. researchers are still contributing to global climate science. To support them, the German Research Foundation in late 2025 set up a program, with a budget of $35 million, to provide a safe haven for permanently storing copies of U.S. data from public and open sources.

The DKRZ project is called “CMIP7-ark” — a lifeboat for the U.S. contribution to the seventh IPCC assessment. Six other projects for U.S. science datasets, with names like “SHIELD” and “SEND” (Secure Endangered Earth Data), have also received funding. Christopher Kadow, head of the data analysis division at the DKRZ, says, “We cannot simply accept that important contributions might be lost for technical or political reasons.”

Data collections form the foundation of all empirical science. Climate, environmental, and biodiversity research relies on countless historical and contemporary datasets. Some of these collections, such as the iconic time series of atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations made at Hawaii’s Mauna Loa Observatory, have been ongoing for decades and are critical for tracking environmental change. (In 2025, the Trump administration tried to defund and close the observatory, but later Congress rejected its proposed cuts.)

The Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii is responsible for the oldest continuous record of atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations. The Trump administration tried to shut down the observatory last year. NOAA

While detailed datasets are essential for research, it is not standard procedure to back them up in alternate locations. Most scientific datasets are physically stored on hard drives or magnetic tapes at the universities or institutions where they are generated. One simple reason for this is that, until recently, the world seemed safe enough that scientists saw no need for offsite backups. Another reason is that transferring and storing such large amounts of information is complicated. Sending petabytes of data through the internet takes months, and renting faster fiber optic cables can be extremely costly. Often, the most cost-effective way to transfer large volumes of data is to put them on hard drives or far cheaper magnetic tapes and send them through the “sneakernet,” which means they are physically transported by car, plane, or ship.

Still, the process is expensive and arduous, so most datasets have been kept in just one place. But in a world where political upheaval, cyberattacks, and armed conflicts are becoming more frequent, this practice is increasingly considered to be precarious. “If data is stored at only one site, there is a much greater risk of loss from events such as extreme weather or infrastructure failure,” says Eleanor O’Rourke, the U.K.-based director of the International Project Office of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, which coordinates global climate models for the IPCC’s assessments. Climate simulations for the IPCC are now considered so critical that overseas repositories are thought necessary for all contributions.

Until recently, the idea that the U.S. science community would have to track or rescue lost datasets was unthinkable. After all, environmental data generated or funded by U.S. government agencies was paid for by taxpayers and generally remains in the public domain. Yet these datasets exist in physical forms at specific locations and require dedicated experts to maintain them, which makes them vulnerable to human interference, as well as natural disasters, technical problems like short circuits or battery explosions, and neglect. “The data repositories, digital asset management services, and preservation systems that ensure research data remains open and accessible are often overlooked — until they disappear,” data experts Jennifer Gibson and Kaitlin Thaney warned recently.

An EPA researcher details the agency’s assault on science. Read more.

One example of political interference is the recent threat to 300 federally funded datasets carefully built up over decades by NASA’s Socioeconomic Data and Applications Center (SEDAC). Alex de Sherbinin, the former SEDAC manager, said the center was founded to “serve as a bridge between the Earth and social-science research communities,” integrating remote sensing data with information on human population, poverty, and infrastructure.

In early April 2025, de Sherbinin was shocked to discover that SEDAC’s datasets had been completely removed by Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency team. It took immense efforts by various institutions, quickly scraping datasets from active servers while they were still operational, to secure the information and transfer it to a new host: the Harvard Dataverse Repository. Assumptions that U.S. government agencies will store scientific data long-term “are proving tenuous at best, given the anti-science predisposition of the current administration,” de Sherbinin says.

Demonstrators protest staffing cuts at NOAA in Silver Spring, Maryland, in 2025. Chip Somodevilla / Getty Images

Numerous similar projects aim to relocate or duplicate datasets within the U.S. But the U.S. is far from the only country where data is vulnerable, and political interference is not the only risk.

Several research institutions around the world, including a multidisciplinary scientific research center in Croatia and an institute for industrial engineering in Germany, have in recent years been the target of elaborate hacking operations, in which intruders block access to files and demand money to restore them. Victims of cybercrimes rarely disclose publicly which files were targeted, but it’s clear that such attacks can affect scientific data collections.

And then there’s war. Russia’s attacks on Ukraine have not only caused mass civilian death and suffering but also immense damage to the country’s science. “Universities and research institutes have been targeted by Russian bombardments, and in some cases they were completely destroyed,” says Anastasiia Lutsenko, a Ukrainian researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Innovation and Competition in Munich, whose work focuses on how her country’s science system is responding to the war. The Russians, she says, “know how much lasting harm they can inflict by attacking science.”

Lutsenko cites the transfer of data and samples to secure locations as an important step. Even before Russia’s 2022 invasion, a German-Polish-Ukrainian collaboration helped digitize records of early meteorological observations — conducted between 1808 and 1880 at eight Ukrainian meteorological stations — and transferred them to a server at the University of Giessen, Germany. This eliminated the risk of losing valuable historical data in the war. A similar transfer took place after missiles struck near the servers holding Ukraine’s contribution to the Global Biodiversity Information Facility’s records in Kharkiv at the start of Russia’s invasion.

The need for increased resilience — the ability to remain functional despite shocks and fully recover from them — was the focus of the American Geophysical Union (AGU) gathering held in Berlin this March. There, nearly 100 experts, primarily from the U.S. and Europe, discussed how to respond to the Trump administration’s attacks on science.

The AGU represents more than 60,000 Earth and space scientists worldwide, and it has emerged as a leader in efforts to protect science and scientists from the Trump administration’s politically motivated attacks. When the administration refused to send U.S. experts to IPCC meetings in 2025, the AGU formed the U.S. Academic Alliance for the IPCC and nominated nearly 300 U.S. scientists to be authors of the IPCC’s next assessment. And in the summer of 2025, it launched its Data Resilience Project, a global initiative to rethink how science is organized.

AGU president-elect Ben Zaitchik believes that science is at an inflection point. “We have engaged in triage efforts to ensure data remains usable and accessible,” he said at the Berlin meeting. “But we clearly now have arrived at a moment where we must fundamentally reimagine data organization and maintenance for long-term viability and growth.”

#2873. Too much RNA can starve cells of energy, scientists discover

Scientists have uncovered a surprising way that viruses can interfere with the body's cells. When too much RNA accumulates inside a cell, it can damage the structures that produce energy, potentially leaving the cell struggling to function normally.

A new study led by researchers at the Texas A&M College of Veterinary Medicine and Biomedical Sciences (VMBS) found that excess RNA produced during poxvirus infections can impair mitochondria, the tiny structures responsible for generating most of the energy cells need to survive.

Published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, the research reveals an unexpected connection between RNA buildup and cellular energy production. The findings could have implications far beyond viral infections, potentially helping scientists better understand aging-related diseases and improve RNA-based medical treatments, including mRNA vaccines.

"Scientists have long known that RNA degradation helps control protein production and remove defective RNA," said Dr. Zhilong Yang, a professor in VMBS' Department of Veterinary Pathobiology. "Our study reveals another important role: it helps cells maintain the energy they need to function properly."

Cells constantly produce and break down RNA as part of their normal activities. This cleanup process, known as RNA degradation, helps regulate protein production, eliminate faulty RNA, and control immune responses. The new findings suggest it also performs another essential function by protecting the cell's energy supply.

RNA plays several important roles in keeping cells functioning. Messenger RNA (mRNA), for example, carries genetic instructions that tell cells which proteins to produce. These proteins are needed for nearly everything cells do, from maintaining their structure to carrying out essential chemical reactions.

Another type, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), frequently appears during viral infections. Its presence can serve as a warning signal, alerting the immune system that a virus may have entered the cell.

Viruses often generate large quantities of RNA as they reproduce inside infected cells. Ordinarily, cellular cleanup systems help prevent RNA from accumulating to harmful levels. However, when RNA production exceeds the cell's ability to break it down, the excess material can interfere with mitochondria and reduce energy production.

Scientists already understood that excessive double-stranded RNA could provoke potentially harmful immune responses. What surprised the researchers was discovering that too much ordinary messenger RNA could also damage mitochondria.

"MRNA is a normal component of our cells, but it seems like with many good things, if we get too much, that can become a problem for the cell," Yang said. "When too much RNA builds up, including mRNA, it can damage the mitochondria and interfere with the cell's ability to produce energy."

This creates an unusual challenge for viruses. Although viruses depend entirely on the cells they infect to supply energy and manufacture proteins, producing excessive amounts of viral RNA can damage the very machinery they need to reproduce.

According to Yang, poxviruses, a group that includes the viruses responsible for smallpox and mpox, appear to have ways of managing this problem. They use RNA cleanup mechanisms to remove excess RNA, helping infected cells remain functional long enough for viral replication to continue.

"If the cell is not healthy, the virus would not replicate well," Yang said. "The virus needs to keep RNA levels balanced inside the cell."

Scientists Discover RNA Can Directly Damage Mitochondria

One of the study's most unexpected findings emerged when researchers investigated exactly how excess RNA affects mitochondria.

Initially, the team suspected that RNA buildup was damaging these cellular powerhouses indirectly by activating the immune system. Certain types of RNA are known to trigger defensive responses, which can place additional stress on infected cells.

However, experiments revealed that the damage did not require an immune response.

"When we isolated only the mitochondria and added RNA, we still saw mitochondrial damage," said Dr. Djamal Brahim Belhaouari, the study's first author. "That was the moment we realized this could be a previously unknown cellular response to RNA."

The researchers also observed that mitochondrial problems developed before substantial immune activation occurred. Furthermore, the damage did not depend on the immune pathways normally activated during infection.

Together, these results indicate that excess RNA itself can interfere with mitochondrial function, rather than simply causing damage through the body's response to a virus.

The precise mechanism remains unclear, but the researchers have proposed a possible explanation involving electrical charges.

Mitochondria produce energy by maintaining an electrical and chemical difference across their inner membranes. This difference helps drive the production of ATP, the molecule cells use as their primary energy source.

RNA carries a negative electrical charge. The researchers suspect that when unusually large amounts accumulate near mitochondria, they may disturb the electrical conditions required for efficient energy production.

"We think the negatively charged RNA may accumulate around mitochondria and disrupt the electrical balance needed for energy production, although how it happens is unknown," Yang said. "This is a new concept that we are continuing to explore."

What the Discovery Could Mean for Aging and Disease

Although the experiments focused on viral infections, the findings raise questions about whether RNA buildup might contribute to other health problems.

Abnormal RNA accumulation has been observed in several conditions, including certain cancers, neurodegenerative diseases, and disorders associated with aging. Neurodegenerative diseases involve the progressive deterioration of nerve cells, as occurs in conditions such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease.

Because mitochondria are essential for maintaining healthy cells, disruptions to their energy production could potentially contribute to cellular dysfunction in some of these conditions.

The researchers suggest that excess RNA may represent one previously unrecognized source of mitochondrial stress. However, further studies are needed to determine how broadly this mechanism operates and whether it directly contributes to these diseases.

"We think this has quite broad translational implications," Brahim Belhaouari said. "Too much double-stranded RNA or mRNA that cannot be degraded effectively may contribute to disease processes in some contexts."

Potential Implications for mRNA Vaccines and Future Treatments

The discovery could also provide useful information for scientists developing medicines that rely on RNA.

RNA-based therapies, including mRNA vaccines, work by introducing RNA instructions into cells to produce specific proteins. Understanding how cells respond to RNA, process it, and eventually break it down is important for designing these treatments.

By identifying another way excessive RNA can affect cellular health, the findings may help researchers optimize future RNA-based therapies and vaccines. The study does not establish that existing mRNA vaccines cause mitochondrial damage in people.

"Understanding this mechanism could help researchers design and optimize future RNA-based therapeutics and vaccines by better understanding how cells respond to large amounts of RNA," Yang said.

More broadly, the research suggests that controlling RNA levels is important for more than maintaining accurate protein production or managing immune activity. It may also be essential for keeping mitochondria functioning and ensuring cells have enough energy.

For the scientists, the most striking result was that even a molecule cells depend on every day can become harmful when it accumulates beyond normal levels.

"Scientists already knew that too much double-stranded RNA can be harmful," Yang said. "What surprised us was finding out that too much mRNA -- something cells normally need every day -- can also damage mitochondria and interfere with the cell's ability to produce energy."

#2875. Traversing the Saiga Silk Road

As we skidded off the main road and onto a mud path maintained only by tire treads, lines began to blur.

Flanking our two-track trail were a series of parallel lanes no more than a foot wide, which forked, merged, and crisscrossed our own. In the distance, I saw what looked like a ghost train, faintly visible and thrust forward by howling Siberian winds.

Through borrowed binoculars, I soon got my first glimpse of an ancient tableau, seemingly untouched since Neanderthals roamed the earth. On the far horizon, hundreds of nomadic travelers pierced the frigid air with their ringed horns, inhaling the brittle shards through Snuffleupagus snouts as they sprinted across the desolate steppe.

Watching them charge through the treeless plain at full bore, I was reminded of a saying I’d heard shortly before arriving in this remote corner of Kazakhstan: “The saiga is a freedom-loving animal.”

Since time immemorial, saiga antelope have blazed trails across Central Asia while other civilizations rose and fell around them. They once inhabited a world dominated by woolly mammoths, saber-toothed cats, and giant deer with antlers 12 feet across—and outlived them all. When pioneering human caravans first attempted to navigate this unforgiving land, they considered the saigas to be their guides.

Ahead of my arrival, seasonal rains transformed once-green grasslands into sludge punctuated by tufts of withering brush. Driving through such muck is, I would soon discover, akin to controlled hydroplaning. The steering wheel bucked wildly as our tires swerved, shredding deep into the gunk and desperately gripping anything remotely solid enough to push off from.

In the front seats were Alexandr and Albert, two team members from the Association for the Conservation of Biodiversity of Kazakhstan, or ACBK. Between them, they have more than twenty-five years of experience following saigas across the steppe, and I’d arranged to join them for the western leg of one of their field audits.

Our visit was timed to coincide with the rutting season, a critical part of the year for saigas. This is a gathering time, when antelope from across the region sprint together in massive single-file lines, before congregating for a series of competitive battles and subsequent mating rituals.

For males, it’s a time for strutting and headbutting to demonstrate one’s physical prowess to female onlookers—an activity so energy-intensive that many will forget to eat and keel over from extreme physical exhaustion. For females, this is a time of choosing. In addition to assessing fitness, they pay particular attention to one distinctive feature: a potential mate’s floppy, elongated nose. The saigas’ sniffers are essential for filtering out dust in the semidesert plains and for warming frigid winter air before it reaches their lungs. A large nose is the ultimate sign of virility, so as the males marched into these annual gatherings, they were doing everything they could to accentuate their schnozzes.

Because of the saigas’ preference for large-scale migrations across a landscape almost entirely devoid of trees, they have long been a favorite of global wildlife trackers. The isolation and starkness of the saiga world is a strength when it comes to GPS collars, which deliver a reliable stream of daily pings without risk of visual obstruction. Prior to such breakthroughs, conservationists would sometimes spend weeks roaming the boundless steppe unsuccessfully trying to locate the nomadic herds.

The wildlife sanctuary we were traveling through was created only a year prior, with its boundaries informed by tracking data that Albert and his team personally collected. He seemed pleased that the animals were continuing to use the newly designated space as intended. Standing along the roadside, Alexandr spotted a stream of the famously skittish ungulates wrapping around the contours of the earth. As he did, ripping wind and snow started blowing in sideways—the outer ring of an encroaching storm.

These were no fluffy flakes. This was the harsh, crunchy ice of a walk-in freezer, which instantly hardened our truck’s caked-on mud like liquid nitrogen.

Seemingly unperturbed by the dropping barometer, Albert chirped, “Who’s hungry?” Setting up an impromptu picnic, he and Alexandr dug into a prepared meal of fresh bread, tea, and dried meats of unknown provenance. In a muted attempt to thaw my face, I opted to guzzle spicy instant noodles.

A tradition of nomadism has loomed large in this corner of the world since at least the Early Iron Age. The very word kazakh is thought to be derived from the Turkic word qaz, which means “wanderer” or “vagabond.” For millennia, small human groups routinely migrated to higher altitudes in the hot summer months, where melting snow made for abundant water and greenery, before returning to sheltered valleys ahead of winter’s whipping winds. At various times of the year, disparate nomads would also congregate for formal meetings and large cultural festivals, complete with physical challenges like wrestling and tug-of-war. When the gatherings were finished, the nomads dispersed once again.

Historical analysis has shown that nomads’ seasonal migrations and time-honed transit corridors may have helped pave the way for portions of the Great Silk Road, which was less a singular route than an ever-evolving web of pathways and bypasses that linked together reliable stopovers. At the Silk Road’s peak, this corner of western Kazakhstan along the fertile banks of the Ural River became a vital transcontinental crossroads. To this day, the river still serves as a natural boundary between Asia and Europe, meaning this part of Kazakhstan is, quite literally, where East meets West.

The land above the Caspian Sea also served as a main artery that linked together key transit points. Strategic settlements along the riverbank allowed weary caravans to rest their heads, unwind in large public baths, exchange knowledge, stock up on essential supplies, and regroup before moving onward.

Among overland voyagers traversing the continental divide, saigas have long been spoken of with an almost mythical reverence. Cave paintings of the antelope created as far back as the seventh century BCE depict them as resilient, elusive, and charismatic. Expert navigators who embody the true spirit of a most inhospitable terrain.

In human society, traditional nomadic practices persisted even as the land itself was variously claimed and reclaimed by Turkic tribes, Genghis Khan, and the Russian Empire. Only with the rise of the Soviet Union in the twentieth century did mandatory sedentarization efforts successfully manage to lock nomadic people in place, force them to farm, and extinguish a cultural flame that had burned brightly for millennia. Denied the ability to live in perpetual motion, people’s navigational muscles began to atrophy.

This transition was viewed by many as an inevitable shift toward “modernization,” but it also represented a significant diversion from our own history as a species. Of the roughly 300,000 years that Homo sapiens have existed, we spent the first 288,000 of them living in small, mobile groups that followed the migratory patterns of other animals. Forcing nomads into agricultural plots was also a colossal failure because much of the steppe soil was unsuitable for growing crops, which is why so many people were nomadic in the first place. Iconic filmmaker Werner Herzog once remarked that “whatever went wrong and makes our civilization something doomed is the departure from the nomadic life.”

Whether by choice or necessity, most Kazakh people now lead stationary lives in urban centers. The world that nomads once freely roamed has also been feverishly transformed, not only by agriculture but through the construction of new roads and railways.

For the saigas, whose nomadic days never ceased, these new layers now represent an existential threat.

With the weather taking a turn for the worse, Albert and Alexandr ultimately decided to call it a day and start fresh in the morning. After a blistering drive through whiteout conditions, we hunkered down in a tiny village less than 10 miles from the Russian border, where Albert pulled out his laptop and showed me how saiga movements have changed over the years.

“They would routinely trek hundreds of miles as part of an expansive migratory route across the Eurasian steppe,” he said. “Saigas could be found from the Caucasus Mountains to Mongolia, and everywhere in between.”

For millennia, they lived with few barriers and obstructions on the landscape. Then, seemingly overnight, rattling railways and roaring roads began to crisscross the region, fragmenting the saigas into smaller, more isolated groups. As the interior steppe got more accessible to vehicles and passengers, development begat more development.

On his screen, Albert clicked open a recent map that showed how a population of GPS-collared saigas once routinely traveled deep into Uzbekistan before being unceremoniously clipped off by a new rail line in 2014. The data from the years prior to the railway’s construction showed a flurry of squiggly lines representing individual animals zipping back and forth with the seasons. The post-construction slide showed those same animals spinning around despondently on the northern side of the tracks, while the fresh grasses and water they had come to rely on withered away to the south.

Such interruptions can lead to a complete collapse in spatial knowledge—a loss that science writer Ben Goldfarb once wrote is “as thorough as the erasure of a language.” Just as human nomads forced to stay in one place eventually lost connection to the physical landmarks of their ancestors’ journeys, young saigas learn where they can reliably find food and shelter, as well as places to stay away from, through active participation and observation. This cultural exchange keeps their world in orbit and is one of the reasons they have been successful at navigating a terrain fatal to so many before them. As the land they know is cleaved and partitioned, whole stretches of the Saiga Silk Road begin to fade from memory.

To unwind this damage, Albert’s team has partnered with the Kazakh government to construct dozens of saiga-friendly crossing points, where slightly sloping dirt ramps rise up to match the height of the train tracks, making it appear from a distance as though there’s a natural gap in the rail line. Cattle-guard fences positioned far away from the crossings ensure that trains and saigas never actually run into each other at these intersections, which would shatter the illusion of safety.

ACBK has also worked with the Uzbek government to reshape its border fences from a barbed-wire wall to an X-shaped barricade that blocks vehicles from driving through but leaves just enough space for saigas to pass. Over time, Albert hopes such efforts can rebuild the trust necessary for them to confidently return to a plateau they once relied on.

The next morning, I was astonished by how austere the steppe looked compared with the day before. Gone was the slippery muck and browning grasses of shifting seasons, replaced by a blanket of crunchy snow. Against this stark black-and-white backdrop, the fresh crystals functioned like fingerprint dust, illuminating the only remaining pops of color: the rust-red streaks of human and nonhuman pathways in the dirt.

Parked in an otherwise empty tract of prairie land, we watched in stunned silence as thousands of saigas seized the day, flying like banshees across the periphery, their hooves gouging the frozen crust and stamping pigment into an earthen canvas.

As I gazed east toward the colorless horizon, I tried to envision how it might have looked in the thirteenth century, when this corner of the world boasted a vast network of caravanserai, or road inns, where exhausted travelers could rest and recover before continuing onward into the great unknown.

I pondered what it must have felt like for an intrepid merchant to know that they would not see another human being for many days, maybe weeks. To be fearful of what you may soon encounter, but take solace that the space between is not wholly empty. For there is life beyond humanity’s margins, and among nonhuman communities, that in-between space is the center of everything. That, I imagined, must have offered some measure of assurance that the road ahead, perilous though it may be, is well traveled.

There is just one major highway leading to Denali National Park: George Parks Highway, which connects Denali to Fairbanks, about two and a half hours to the north, and Anchorage, about four and a half hours to the south. Both cities have well-connected airports and multiple car rental options.

You don't need a car to get around in Denali National Park, but if you visit during the shoulder season, you can drive your own vehicle as far into the park as road conditions allow. If you don't want to drive, limited motor coach services can get you to Denali during the summer.

The most luxurious option for reaching Denali is the Alaska Railroad, which, like the George Parks Highway, runs from Anchorage to Fairbanks and vice versa. The train runs directly through Denali's entrance and drops visitors off just a five-minute walk from the park's visitor center.

When is the best time to visit Denali National Park?

Denali National Park's peak season is mid-May through mid-September. Most tour, lodging, and restaurant amenities shut down as soon as the peak season ends, but if you're prepared to DIY a trip in winter conditions—or want to book a day tour out of Fairbanks—you can still visit during the winter.

Visiting during the early-May or mid- to late-September shoulder seasons offers a more secluded experience. Amenities and tour options are extremely limited, but September in particular is a beautiful time for exploring the park when the weather cooperates.

What are the best things to do in Denali National Park?

Perhaps the best-known excursion in Denali is taking a bus ride on the lone road running into the park. Narrated tour buses and hop-on, hop-off transit buses used to run all the way down the 92-mile park road—but an ongoing land slump has forced partial closure of the road, so all vehicle traffic currently turns around at mile 43. For a quick (and free) taste of the park road, you can catch the courtesy shuttle that runs between the Denali Visitor Center and Savage River (mile 15 of the park road).

You can also rent a bicycle from Bike Denali, about a (bikeable) mile outside the park entrance, and explore as far as you'd like along the park road, or drive your personal vehicle as far as mile 15 during peak season, although the small parking area at Savage River fills up fast.

It's natural to expect a glimpse of North America's tallest mountain, Denali, when you come to the park that bears its name. But you'll have to venture to at least mile 9 of the park road before the elevation is high enough to reveal 20,310' Denali. If you're feeling adventurous, you can also hike to the top of the aptly named Mountain Vista trail, at about mile 12, for more views.

The mountain is often obscured by clouds, so if you don't get to see it while in the park, don't despair: You can also catch a glimpse from the Alaska Railroad trains or, if you're driving, from several well-marked waysides along the Parks Highway.

Denali is home to a handful of beautiful hiking trails, including the beloved Savage River and Savage Alpine trails, as well as the stunning Triple Lakes Trail, where you might be able to add beavers to your wildlife-viewing checklist. You're also welcome to hike off-trail from the park road—just hop off at any likely-looking pullout and explore.

No matter where you hike, it's important that you carry bear spray—which you can rent from the Alaska Geographic store in the park, next to the Denali Visitor Center—and know what to do if you encounter wildlife. Park rangers are also available to help you choose a hiking destination and coach you on how to handle wildlife encounters. They also lead a variety of group activities, including hikes both on- and off-trail.

Denali National Park is home to the National Park Service's only working kennel of sled dogs. Rangers demonstrate how they work with the dogs two to three times per day throughout the summer; you can walk to the kennels from the park entrance, or catch a free shuttle bus.

You'll see a wide variety of other tour options advertised for Denali National Park, including whitewater rafting, horseback riding, ATV tours, and jeep tours. With very few exceptions, these take place along the boundary of the park, not actually in it. But adventuring alongside Denali can be a fine—and thrilling—way to spend your visit.

Among companies operating just outside the park, Denali Horseback Tours is a perennial favorite. Their reliable steeds take you off into the hills for beautiful vistas overlooking the park and neighboring lands, without any of the noise or exhaust from a jeep or ATV tour. If you do want the thrill of a motor-driven vehicle, though, Denali ATV Adventures is hugely popular.

A last (and favorite) way of seeing Denali at near-distance is from the water. Whitewater rafting tours from companies like the family-owned Denali Raft Adventures take you down the Nenana River, which forms the natural boundary on the east side of Denali National Park. Rafting excursions range from pulse-pounding to relatively sedate, so there’s something for almost everybody.

If you’re ready to splurge on a once-in-a-lifetime experience, Alaska Alpine Adventures is an excellent guide service that does have the required permits to work inside the park. Just note that their custom hiking and backpacking tours demand a fairly high level of physical fitness.

What area should you stay in near Denali National Park?

Denali National Park is notably absent of a traditional gateway town, a characteristic that sets it apart from many other national parks. The National Park Service's focus on preserving the park's natural state has led to policies that limit large-scale development and urbanization. The best areas to stay in therefore include spots directly on the mountain; in Kantishna, a bustling gold mining hub situated 90 miles into the park; and a smattering of hotels, resorts, and Airbnbs between Healy (about 11 miles north of the park entrance) and Cantwell (approximately 30 miles south of the park entrance). You can also choose to stay in Talkeetna, about a two-and-a-half-hour drive from the entrance, roughly halfway between Anchorage and Denali.

Keep in mind that if you plan to head to Kantishna, with the closure of the Denali Park Road beyond mile 43 until at least summer 2027 due to the Pretty Rocks Landslide, the only way to get there is currently by bush plane or helicopter, providing guests with rare, near-exclusive access to the park’s wild interior.

“The Canyon” (otherwise known as “Glitter Gulch,” the hub for tourism just outside the park, with various services, tour companies, shops, and restaurants catering to visitors), is an area that exists almost exclusively for tourism—locals don’t live there.

#2881. A New Strike Against High Protein?

A recent paper is fueling the plant-based crowd. In it, the authors found that protein restriction turned off many genetic and molecular signals of aging.It was a synthesis of over 350 existing studies, a mix of in vitro research, animal research, and human trials, and the overall finding was that restricting protein elicited favorable levels and changes to mTOR, FGF21, oxidative stress, and other things associated with or contributory to aging.

Now, the study had some interesting things, right? There was some nuance there. They readily admitted that protein restriction acts differently in sedentary versus active individuals. Those who are actively training have a higher supposedly “safe” window of protein consumption. Maybe they have a higher tolerance for protein. But overall, the thrust of the paper, and what most people will take away from it, and what your vegan friends are no doubt bragging to you about, is that less protein is better for longevity.

I don’t think it’s as simple as that. Let me explain.

Much of the research rests on the assumption that specific amino acids in excess promote aging. The one they say most is methionine. It’s found in dairy, muscle meat, eggs, and other animal products, and absolutely, excessive amounts of it can promote aging in animal models, and likely humans. No argument there.But what these studies never explore is the reality that methionine is balanced by another amino acid: glycine. Glycine is almost entirely absent from the classic animal protein products like meat or eggs or dairy, and it’s found almost exclusively in connective tissue, bones, and skin.There is a classic study where they put rodents on a high-methionine diet, and sure enough, those rats died pretty young. But they took another group and gave them glycine, same amount of methionine, just with extra glycine, and those guys lived long, healthy lives, just as long as the rodents who lived on a restricted-methionine diet.1-2We see echoes of this in the human literature. Tons of research finds that low glycine levels in the serum predict type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and many of the other diseases classically linked to meat consumption.Now let’s look at Hong Kong, which is one of the longest-living populations on Earth, who also happen to eat the most meat of anyone on Earth. But the meat they’re eating is not just steaks and burgers and roasts. They’re also eating skin, snouts, feet, and other animal foods that are high in glycine.This is why collagen is so essential for the modern meat eater who doesn’t want to eat the obscure stuff like skin and bones. Thirty to 40 grams of collagen a day provides enough glycine to balance out all the muscle meat and other animal protein you eat. And although we don’t have direct human trials of balancing out methionine with glycine and looking at lifespan, the circumstantial evidence is pretty strong that it’s going to have good effects.

Except that in the real world, when you see people who actually practice this, these are people who live this lifestyle, they’ve read the studies, they’ve convinced themselves that protein restriction is key to living long. They are generally pretty unwell. They’re frail, always cold, have low energy, and generally aren’t the picture of health we are imagining for ourselves.

Now, they may be better off and look better than the general population in some ways. They tend to be thinner, at least, and, you know, even their biomarkers look pretty good. But I refuse to believe that this is the path to optimal health. You may limp by a bit longer. You may slowly walk into old age, but can you still run hills? Can you play? Are you strong?And that’s where the study loses me.

Nothing against the authors themselves. #2884. A train was delayed by a solar storm. But something didn't add up.

A mysterious train delay nearly 180 years ago has led scientists to uncover a surprising mistake in the history of space weather. An international research team has solved a 178-year-old mystery involving one of the earliest documented cases of solar activity interfering with electrical technology.

The incident dates back to the 1840s, when electric telegraph networks were beginning to transform long-distance communication. As these early systems expanded, operators occasionally encountered strange electrical disturbances that could disrupt their equipment. Scientists now understand that some of these problems were caused by changes in Earth's magnetic field triggered by activity on the Sun.

Researchers led by Lancaster University have revisited a historical account of a train delayed in Exeter, England, when solar activity interfered with the railway's telegraph system. The investigation involved scientists from RMIT University, the British Geological Survey, Natural Resources Canada, Baylor University, and the UK's national space laboratory, RAL Space.

By piecing together historical documents and scientific observations, the team uncovered an error that changes the timeline of the earliest known technological disruptions caused by space weather.

How Solar Storms First Disrupted Technology

One of history's most dramatic examples of space weather affecting technology occurred in 1859. Known as the Carrington Event, the powerful solar storm involved a massive solar flare and a coronal mass ejection, an enormous eruption of charged particles and magnetic fields from the Sun.

The resulting disturbances produced spectacular auroras, commonly known as the northern lights, that appeared as far south as Hawaii and Central America. In some locations, the sky glowed brightly enough for people to read newspapers at night. The unusual illumination even fooled gold miners into thinking morning had arrived, prompting them to prepare breakfast shortly after midnight.

The storm also wreaked havoc on telegraph networks around the world. Electrical surges caused equipment failures, sent sparks flying from wires, shocked operators, and even started fires. In some cases, the electrical currents generated by the disturbance were so powerful that telegraph systems continued operating without batteries.

Although the Carrington Event remains the most famous example from this period, evidence of solar activity interfering with electrical systems stretches back even further.

In 1871, an anonymous author published an account in the scientific journal Nature describing a "very intense magnetic disturbance" that supposedly occurred on October 18, 1841. According to the report, the disturbance interfered with railway signals in Exeter and delayed the scheduled 10:05 p.m. train departure by 16 minutes.

If the date had been correct, the event would have represented the earliest known instance of space weather disrupting human technology.

However, a new investigation published in the American Geophysical Union journal Space Weather has revealed that the original account contained a significant historical error.

The Railway Mystery That Changed Solar Storm History

Associate Professor Brett Carter, a space weather researcher at RMIT University, helped investigate the historical records. During the investigation, the team noticed a problem that immediately called the original timeline into question.

The Exeter-to-Starcross railway line mentioned in the report did not open until 1846, almost five years after the event supposedly occurred.

"In this study, we effectively investigated what had to be a typo in a Nature paper from 1871. We know it was a typo because the Exeter-to-Starcross train line mentioned in that paper didn't exist until 1846, which is 5 years afterwards," he said.

To establish when the train delay actually happened, the researchers examined a wide range of historical evidence. They compared railway timetables and newspaper reports with observations of solar activity, accounts of auroras, and digitized measurements of Earth's magnetic field.

Together, these records pointed to a different date: October 18, 1848, exactly seven years later than originally reported.

The revised timeline resolves the apparent contradiction and places the incident during an important period in technological history, when electrical communication systems were becoming increasingly widespread.

Carter explained that the 1840s marked a turning point in humanity's relationship with space weather. Solar disturbances had always occurred, but the emergence of electrical technology meant that their effects could now interfere directly with everyday human activities.

"Understanding this historical event is important because the 1840s mark the intersection between the rise of our technological age and space weather, which has always been around," Carter says.

"Since this line was crossed, humans have not looked back."

The findings also change the incident's place in the historical record. Although the Exeter disruption remains one of the earliest credible examples of space weather affecting technology, it can no longer be considered the first.

That distinction now belongs to an earlier documented incident involving telegraph interference on Britain's Midland Railway in March 1847.

Professor Jim Wild of Lancaster University, who led the study, said the findings demonstrate that the dangers associated with space weather began affecting society long before the development of modern power grids, satellites, and electronic communication networks.

"What this research highlights is that space weather is not a new threat but a long-standing natural hazard. Society has been experiencing the effects of space weather on technology for almost as long as electrical technologies have existed," Wild said.

The Exeter incident is especially significant because it occurred when electrical technology was still in its infancy. Early railway operators were beginning to rely on telegraph systems, creating new opportunities for solar disturbances to interfere with essential infrastructure.

"The Exeter train delay is a fascinating story because it sits right at the point where emerging technologies first began to encounter the realities of the space environment. By combining historical archives with scientific observations, we've been able to show that the event almost certainly happened in 1848 rather than 1841.

"Although this means it is not the earliest recorded space weather impact, it remains one of the first clear examples of solar activity disrupting critical infrastructure," he said.

The investigation also shows how historical scientific records can be used alongside newspapers and other archival materials to reconstruct events that occurred long before modern monitoring instruments became available.

"It also demonstrates the value of combining scientific records with contemporary newspaper reports and archival documents when reconstructing historic space weather events."

Dr. Mike Hapgood, a Visiting Scientist and space weather expert at the UK's national space laboratory, RAL Space, described the research process as something resembling a detective investigation.

"It highlights the importance of preserving these older records, which give us the evidence base we need to interpret past events and strengthen future predictions," he said.

Modern Technology Still Faces the Threat of Solar Storms

Almost two centuries after the Exeter train delay, solar storms continue to pose risks to railways and other essential infrastructure. The technologies involved have changed dramatically, but they remain susceptible to disturbances originating from the Sun.

Today, space weather can interfere with electrical power systems, railway signaling equipment, satellite navigation, and communication networks. Powerful solar events can disturb Earth's magnetic field, potentially generating unwanted electrical currents in infrastructure and affecting systems that depend on reliable satellite signals.

Scientists now have far more sophisticated instruments and forecasting methods than were available in the 19th century. However, Hapgood emphasized that modern society's growing dependence on interconnected electrical and space technologies also creates new vulnerabilities.

Understanding how past solar storms affected infrastructure can help researchers assess the dangers posed by future events and develop better ways to reduce potential disruptions.

"Deepening our understanding of these events is essential for preparing for, and mitigating, the impacts of space weather - especially as we look forward to a decade of exciting space developments that will face the challenge of a new solar cycle in the 2030s," he said.

#2886. From Indigenous Loss, a Potent Legal Argument on Climate

On Fiji's Kioa Island, Lotomau Fiafia and his grandson John stand where the shoreline used to be when Lotomau was young. As seas rise, the shoreline is retreating. Eddie Jim / The Age via Getty Images

In an interview, human rights lawyer Julian Aguon explores how climate change is undermining the cultural traditions of Indigenous peoples. He is citing these injuries in legal actions, including in a major victory at the International Court of Justice last year.

Last year, the International Court of Justice unanimously ruled that countries have a legal obligation to limit greenhouse gas emissions. The decision was the culmination of a years-long campaign launched by a group of law students in the Pacific island nation of Vanuatu.

The landmark opinion, which said countries’ failure to address emissions from fossil fuels and other sources could carry legal consequences, provides a potentially powerful new tool for communities, organizations, or governments suing in national courts to compel climate action or win damages. It could also push international climate negotiators toward a more aggressive stance on a danger the judges called “urgent and existential.”

Julian Aguon, an Indigenous Chamorro human rights lawyer from Guam, wore a garland of coconut fronds and cowry shells as he argued Vanuatu’s case in The Hague. He and his colleagues had traveled across Melanesia and Micronesia to gather testimony from residents whose homes, cultures, and livelihoods are being devastated by climate change.

While the impact of climate change is typically measured in degrees of warming, feet of flooding, or monetary losses, for many Indigenous people it is inflicting a profound cultural, spiritual, and emotional injury that the sterile language of science and economics fails to capture, said Aguon, founder of the Guam-based Indigenous rights and environmental justice firm Blue Ocean Law. In an interview with Yale Environment 360, he said that highlighting the harms Indigenous peoples and cultures are suffering proved a potent legal strategy at the United Nations’ main judicial body.

“Climate change is unraveling the very terms by which people know themselves and keep covenant with the natural world.” In the ICJ case, he said, “we brought that to the court’s attention because these are rights violations of the highest order — violations of the right to life, the right to culture, the right to self-determination.”

Julian Aguon addressing the International Court of Justice in December 2024. International Court of Justice

Yale Environment 360: Can you tell me about the testimonies you and your colleagues gathered when you traveled to Vanuatu, Fiji, and other island nations in preparation for the case? How did they shape your arguments in court?

Julian Aguon: There’s no way to translate the urgency and gravity of the climate crisis other than via firsthand accounts of loss and damage, so we wanted to foreground vulnerable communities’ experiences. The process revealed in spectacular ways just how emaciated the global discourse on climate harms is. The harms aren’t just environmental, and people who speak in that limited frame don’t know anything about what’s happening in the Pacific. It’s a collapse of cultural traditions that stretch back millennia and have sustained life in these islands for thousands and thousands of years.

In Yakel village [in Vanuatu], many ceremonies revolve around the yam, which is the most important root crop. Some of the ceremonies haven’t been performed in years because there hasn’t been a successful yam harvest. For example, there’s an elaborate four-day dance by which the people choose their high chief. And they’re unable to perform this dance because of the yam’s absence.

That all causes an enormous amount of stress and distress, and it affects the ability to pass on to future generations these cultural teachings, which are place-based. Because these are cultures that have grown out of a particular piece of soil, they’re tied to their place and the life in it. So we’re talking about a massive rupture to culture and to cosmologies. And the vocabulary of environmental damage, because it remains so one-dimensional and flat, and largely borrowed from Western frameworks, fails to capture the true gravity and the true nature of the harm that climate change is causing.

E360: Pacific island nations are experiencing twice the global rate of sea level rise. For many, the threat is existential. Tuvalu’s prime minister has noted that 50 percent of his country will be regularly underwater by 2050. What did you hear about this threat in the communities you visited?

Aguon: All over Melanesia, the story was the same. In Veraibari, in Papua New Guinea, people have already moved four times due to sea level rise, [with the village, situated between a river and the sea, relocating to safer ground]. It’s one thing to talk about rising seas in terms of the numbers, but it’s something else entirely to hear what it means when you have to move four times, and you’re in the midst of a fifth and final climate-induced relocation [because there is nowhere else to go]. What’s more, people in Veraibari don’t understand themselves as having moved there. According to their cultural teachings, it is one of the birthplaces of humankind. To be forced to keep moving and retreating from your ancestral homeland, which is also, in your cosmology, one of the earliest sites of human civilization — the stakes could not be higher.

E360: How is climate change infringing on the right to self-determination?

Aguon: These island nations struggled for so long to throw off the colonial yoke, to emerge as independent sovereign states. And now all of these self-determined ways of life are being gravely imperiled [by rich countries’ fossil fuel emissions]. It’s the definition of climate injustice, and in some ways it’s the latest iteration of the great game of imperialism, of world-making and world-destroying. It’s laying bare all the same structural inequalities.

The island nation of Tuvalu is highly vulnerable to sea level rise. Mario Tama / Getty Images

E360: Can you explain how the case came about?

Aguon: Basically 27 law students from the Vanuatu campus of the University of the South Pacific rallied behind this call to bring the world’s biggest problem to the world’s highest court. They approached [Vanuatu’s leaders, including] the then-minister of foreign affairs, Ralph Regenvanu, who ran with it, and the government spearheaded a global campaign to get the United Nations General Assembly to refer the legal question to the International Court of Justice. It was a Herculean effort, a really muscular, robust diplomatic campaign. It resulted in this beautiful moment in 2023 when the General Assembly adopted the referral by consensus.

E360: How do you see the opinion’s significance, particularly since it is advisory rather than binding?

Aguon: While it’s technically nonbinding in and of itself, the opinion is an authoritative statement of what the law really is. The court evaluated the legality of the conduct driving climate change, not only under climate treaties like the Paris Agreement and the U.N. Framework Convention on Climate Change, but also under a wider body of international law, including international environmental law, human rights law, and the Law of the Sea.

The opinion says states have binding legal obligations to address the climate crisis. That dispels the ambiguity that has frustrated global progress. And most importantly, it said breaching those obligations is an “internationally wrongful act.” That gives countries and communities new tools, and it has changed the legal terrain for this fight.

It will feed contentious cases, like where one state is suing another state, or one frontline community is suing a carbon major. We understand that our health and the health of the planet are inseparable, that humans are inseparable from the more-than-human world, and the health of one is the health of all. Alongside other organizations, we’re contesting that, and we believe the permitting process the agency envisions violates several federal environmental statutes.

But as a firm, we’re also trying to push newer ideas, arguing that some Indigenous communities here in Micronesia have very unique claims. For example, way-finding communities that have been traversing these oceanic spaces for millennia, they have a very different relationship to the sea. Some of the sacred sites that way-finders have talked about forever in their chants include areas where the U.S. government wants to allow deep-sea mining. These communities also maintain healthy relationships with the more-than-human world, like with certain seabirds, with whales, and other cetaceans that are considered spiritual guides.

These claims don’t sound in administrative law like, “X agency violated Y statute.” We’re saying something much deeper, that certain cultures depend on the health and integrity of the ocean, of the deep-sea environment, and to authorize deep-sea mining in these spaces violates several of their human rights, including the right to culture. What we were talking about earlier happening on land is also happening at sea.

The cultures who have been reared in this part of the world are much older than the United States itself. How do you fit the worldviews and the traditional knowledge systems of ancient civilizations into categories that reflect only the last 200 years? It’s almost an impossible task, but that’s what we need to do to get to where we need to go.

Bright yellow daffodils are among the first signs of spring, along with birds singing and green buds emerging from trees.

Daffodils are great flowers for science activities because they grow from bulbs and have distinct parts. You can learn about transpiration, competition for resources, phototropism and more with these five easy daffodil science experiments.

Warning – daffodil sap can be an irritant. Wear gloves when handling.

Have you ever wondered how water travels from the soil all the way to the petals of a flower? If you add a little food colouring to water and place a daffodil in it, you’ll see how this happens!

When water evaporates from the surface of the leaves, the pressure change pulls the column of water upwards to replace the water lost. This creates a constant stream of water through the plant. This process is called transpiration.

If you see a lovely sunny spot, you can walk over to it. Plants can’t do that, so they have to grow towards the sunlight ( phototropism ).

You can demonstrate this by placing a daffodil in a pot of soil inside a cardboard box with a hole cut out in one side. If you place the box in a sunny spot, you’ll see the daffodil start to grow towards the light.

Phototropism is the growth of a plant towards light. They do this with the help of a hormone called auxin. When sunlight hits the side of a plant, the auxin molecules move to the shady side of the stem, causing the cells on that side to stretch and grow longer. This makes the plant bend towards the light!

Positive phototropism is when stems and leaves grow towards sunlight.

Negative phototropism is seen in roots, which grow away from sunlight.

Find out whether daffodils prefer acidic or alkaline soil with a simple science investigation.

Place one daffodil in slightly acidic water (add a teaspoon of white vinegar), one daffodil in water with a teaspoon of baking soda added, and one daffodil in water (preferably distilled).

You should find that the daffodils prefer neutral or slightly acidic water.

Leave for a couple of days and observe how the flowers change. You should find the flowers with the daffodil in jar 2 start to wilt before the ones in jar 3.

#2895. A simple 15-minute treatment could help patients avoid carpal tunnel surgery

A simple procedure that takes about 15 minutes could offer lasting relief to people with carpal tunnel syndrome, a painful condition that can cause numbness, tingling, and weakness in the hands. A new study suggests that the treatment, which uses ultrasound to guide an injection of ordinary saline around a compressed nerve, provides longer-lasting benefits than traditional steroid injections, without the risks associated with steroids.

The findings, published in Radiology, a journal of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA), point to a promising alternative for patients whose symptoms have not improved with conventional treatments. Researchers say the minimally invasive technique could also help some patients delay or avoid surgery.

Why Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Can Be So Difficult to Treat

Carpal tunnel syndrome develops when the median nerve, which extends from the forearm into the hand, becomes compressed as it passes through a narrow channel in the wrist called the carpal tunnel. Pressure on this nerve can cause pain, tingling, numbness, and muscle weakness, sometimes making everyday activities difficult.

The condition is commonly associated with repetitive hand and wrist movements. Studies suggest that approximately 5% to 10% of people develop carpal tunnel syndrome during their lifetime, and the number of cases is increasing.

One common treatment involves injecting corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and relieve pressure on the nerve. Although these injections can ease symptoms, the benefits often fade, and repeated treatments may be necessary.

"Traditional steroid injection for carpal tunnel syndrome often provides only temporary relief, thus requiring repeats of the procedure," said study co-author Anupama Tandon, M.D., professor in the Department of Radiodiagnosis and Imaging at University College of Medical Sciences and Guru Teg Bahadur Hospital in Delhi, India. "Further, steroids carry risk of localized skin changes, fat thinning and tendon weakening."

A Simple Procedure That Frees a Compressed Nerve

Researchers investigated an alternative technique known as nerve hydrodissection. Rather than relying on medication to reduce inflammation, this approach uses carefully placed fluid to separate the affected nerve from nearby tissues that may be restricting its movement.

Doctors use real-time ultrasound imaging to see the nerve and guide the injection precisely. The fluid helps loosen adhesions, which are areas where tissues stick together, allowing the nerve to move more freely.

Hydrodissection is often performed alongside steroid treatment. However, researchers have had limited evidence about whether the procedure can provide lasting relief without adding steroids.

To investigate, Dr. Tandon joined study lead author Anindita Bose, M.D., assistant professor in the Department of Radiology at Mahatma Gandhi Medical College and Hospital in Jaipur, India, and other researchers in conducting a randomized controlled trial involving 63 patients.

All participants had confirmed carpal tunnel syndrome and had continued experiencing symptoms despite conventional treatment.

The researchers randomly assigned patients to three equally sized groups. One received ultrasound-guided hydrodissection using normal saline, a sterile saltwater solution. Another underwent the same procedure with both saline and corticosteroids. The third received an ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injection without hydrodissection.

Carpal Tunnel Relief Lasted Longer Without Steroids

Researchers tracked each patient's progress using standardized measurements of pain, symptom severity, and hand function. They also performed ultrasound examinations to measure the size of the median nerve, an indicator of swelling.

These evaluations took place four weeks, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks after treatment.

After four weeks, all three groups showed significant improvements. However, differences became increasingly apparent as the study continued.

Patients who underwent hydrodissection continued to experience improvements in their symptoms and hand function. Those who received steroid injections alone initially improved but began experiencing recurring symptoms during later follow-up visits.

"At 12 weeks and 6 months, both hydrodissection groups demonstrated further improvement in clinical symptoms and function, whereas patients who received corticosteroid injection alone experienced a recurrence of the symptoms, suggesting that the initial benefit was short-lived," Dr. Tandon said.

Surprisingly, adding corticosteroids to the hydrodissection fluid did not produce a statistically significant additional long-term benefit. Using ordinary saline alone appeared to work just as well over the six-month follow-up period.

Ultrasound measurements provided additional evidence of the treatment's effectiveness. In patients who underwent hydrodissection, the cross-sectional area of the median nerve decreased by approximately 43% to 45%. Among those treated with corticosteroid injections alone, the reduction was only about 15%.

The greater decrease in nerve size suggested that hydrodissection was more effective at reducing swelling around the compressed nerve.

"Most of our patients had been suffering from carpal tunnel syndrome for months to years and did not get much relief despite trying various treatments like pain medications, splints, lifestyle modification and corticosteroids," Dr. Tandon said. "It came as a pleasant surprise to find that a simple procedure of dissecting the median nerve under ultrasound guidance with normal saline provided them long-term relief."

A 15-Minute Treatment That Could Help Patients Avoid Surgery

Beyond providing sustained symptom relief, hydrodissection offered several practical advantages.

The procedure lasted just 15 minutes on average and was generally well tolerated. Researchers observed no significant complications, either immediately afterward or during the follow-up period.

Patients also reported high satisfaction with their treatment. The procedure was inexpensive, required neither anesthesia nor hospitalization, and allowed participants to return to work within an hour.

These advantages could make hydrodissection an attractive option for people seeking relief without undergoing surgery or receiving repeated steroid injections.

The findings are particularly relevant as carpal tunnel syndrome becomes more common worldwide.

"Overall, the study suggests that ultrasound-guided saline hydrodissection is a safe, minimally invasive treatment that can provide sustained clinical and morphological improvement in patients with refractory carpal tunnel syndrome," Dr. Tandon said. "Thus, hydrodissection merits to be a minimally invasive option for mild to moderate carpal tunnel syndrome and can defer and even alleviate the need for surgery."

Larger Studies Will Test How Long the Benefits Last

Although the findings are encouraging, the researchers emphasize that additional studies are needed before the treatment's long-term effectiveness can be fully established.

The trial included only 63 participants, and patients were monitored for six months. Larger studies involving multiple medical centers and longer observation periods could help determine how consistently hydrodissection works and whether its benefits continue beyond the initial follow-up period.

The research team has already begun another investigation comparing different fluids used during hydrodissection, including saline and dextrose, a form of sugar.

The goal is to identify which approach produces the most durable improvements for people with carpal tunnel syndrome.

"This trial features an extended follow-up period to track long-term patient outcomes and establish the true durability of the treatment," Dr. Tandon said.

#2897. In Lofoten, Norway, a Table Is Set Between Peaks and Fjords

“Now you know what it’s like to be in the eye of a storm,” said our hiking guide, Audun Rasmussen, with a grin.

I tried to nod, but the wind whipped it into a wobble. We were nearly 2,000 feet up a steep, multi-edged mountain face, and simultaneously getting pelted by rain. If views over picturesque towns and freshwater lakes below are what is typically promised in this part of Northern Norway, today there’d be none. Instead, fog clung to every nook and crevice of the glacial hulks surrounding us.

A room at Holmen Lofoten framing the wild ocean and craggy shoreline

There was a sense of achievement, certainly. Rain had turned much of our trails into sinking mud, and rock faces to gripless stone. We had used our hands to push past birch and skidded on moss. And we would still have to make our way back down. But for now, we were in the clouds, hiding under rocky overhangs and finding reprieve in reindeer-meat sandwiches, dry fish bites, and chocolate-covered wafers called Kvikk Lunsj that translates to “a quick lunch."

It’s ascents like these, over rock foundations that vastly predate the Jurassic period, that draw close to a million visitors each year to Lofoten Islands. The tiny Norwegian archipelago, 68 degrees north of the Arctic Circle and two flights from Oslo, is a distinctive and dramatic landscape with spiky mountains rising from the Norwegian Sea and sandy beaches fringed by fjords. In summer, buoyed by endless daylight, adventurers arrive from Norway’s mainland to camp, climb, and swim.

But summer was past its twilight when I arrived in late September, and I had come to discover an entirely different side of Lofoten: one found not on its peaks, but on its plates.

Lofoten's rocky shorelines feature a nutrient-dense ecosystem and seabird colonies.

In a wind-whipped corner of Sørvågen, a village in the southwestern reaches of the archipelago, a cluster of mustard-colored cabins on stilts juts out into the sea. Once home to a cod factory, these buildings have been transformed into Holmen Lofoten, a unique culinary destination and hotel that has welcomed food lovers and adventurers from around the world since 2007. The traditional fishing cabins, known as rorbuer in Norwegian, are distinguished by their corrugated roofs, some of which are topped with insulating grass. Walkways connect the buildings to each other and to wooden decks facing the sea. Inside the guest rooms, large picture windows frame the shoreline. With its simple local furnishings and hand-hewn charm, everything about this place feels elemental.

A cluster of traditional fishing cabins make up Holmen Lofoten, a hotel and culinary destination.

Locally sourced furnishings at Holmen are kept deliberately simple so the views take centerstage.

During my four-day stay, Holmen would play host to Kitchen on the Edge of the World, an extraordinary culinary adventure launched in 2018 that welcomes acclaimed chefs into its kitchen four times a year, and brings guests around the table—and out into the surrounding wild. This, the 30th edition, would be helmed by Matt Orlando, a California-born, Copenhagen-based chef who has worked at some of the world’s most influential kitchens like The Fat Duck in London and Per Se in New York. For our part, we would feast and drink to our hearts’ content, with the landscape that nurtures every dish spread out just beyond the windows.

Ingunn Rasmussen, the warm, chatty matriarch behind Kitchen on the Edge (and hiking guide Audun's sister), took over the property in October 2001 and now runs it with her son, Håvar Holmen. “I wanted to build a space that felt like home, where we could all log off and reconnect with our natural roots,” she said when she picked me up from the airport. A fisherman's daughter, she grew up around this very site—in fact, one of the cabins still bears her sister’s childhood scribbles on the wall. After a period abroad, Ingunn returned determined to breathe life back into this corner of Lofoten. What followed was a meticulous restoration focused on preserving the original structures and historical details, and eventually, the addition of a couple of new buildings in the same vernacular to accommodate larger suites.

In Lofoten, there are often four seasons in an hour.

Traditional bunk style beds in a larger room at Holmen

On the first night of the multi-day event, we gathered in the living room above the kitchen. Like all of Holmen’s shared spaces, it is defined by rustic interiors, exposed beams, and raw, sculptural elements—foraged flora, taxidermy, found objects—that echo the weathered landscape outside. That evening’s dinner would be helmed by Holmen’s own chefs, a young and talented assemblage from as far as Sicily and Piauí, in the northeast of Brazil.

Trays circulated with shrimp croustades and cocktails that looked like Negronis but told a more local story: the vermouth was infused with foraged blackcurrant leaves, and the Campari steeped with dried berries and yarrow flowers. Each sip seemed to capture the autumnal palette I’d seen blanketing the hillside on our drive in. The nonalcoholic version was just as heady: an elderflower tonic with a syrup made from freshly picked wild hops from nearby. “Holmen was created because we want to show our guests the Lofoten Islands,” said Ingunn. “And food is a critical part of understanding it.”

The kitchen is the heart of Holmen, and guests are welcome to pull up a stool and chat with the chefs at any hour.

I wandered over to Mia Hjorth Hansen, co-owner of Gedulgt, a cocktail bar in Aarhus, Denmark, and resident mixologist at Kitchen on the Edge. Shelves around her workstation held glass jars filled with foraged and dried ingredients—cornflower, dandelion, and fireweed—that she was transforming into elixirs. While visiting chefs rarely return, Hansen is an exception: this is her twelfth Kitchen on the Edge. Still, there's been a through-line among the chefs. Whether Ana Roš, the renowned Slovenian chef behind the three-Michelin-starred Hiša Franko, or Simon Rogan, the UK pioneer of the farm-to-table movement, each has shared a commitment to land stewardship. “We take time picking them,” says Ingunn, “because we want [chefs] who will be considerate about the people and wildlife that make this [our] home.”

The first night of the event is always designed to be an icebreaker, and Ingunn warned us we’d be surprised by how quickly we’d grow comfortable sharing close quarters over the four days. We were a motley crew, including a geologist from Brunei, a committed adventurer from Dubai, and a Melbourne retiree who travels for intimate food events like this one. Our conversations followed us outside for dessert, onto a deck where we savored ice cream made with black currant leaves and lovage, topped with mascarpone made from Lofoten cream and finished with a meringue charred over binchotan, a Japanese white charcoal. A tracking app suggested the Northern Lights were tantalizingly close, and Holmen kept watch on the skies. It was a taste of what was to come: an intimate experience shaped by live-fire cooking and an embrace of the Arctic elements.

Chef Matt Orlando working his magic by the tradition wood-fired oven

The next morning, encouraged by Ingunn's invitation to explore every corner of the property, I wandered into the kitchen to watch Orlando and his sous chef, Tom Siebeler, prepare for their first dinner. “Anything that comes out of that wood-fired oven is going to taste amazing,” Orlando told me, working his way through a pile of reindeer ribs from a sustainable farm run by Sámi herders just north of Lofoten. Originally expecting chops, he was improvising garnishes to suit the ribs. Reindeer is one of many ingredients Holmen takes care to source locally, alongside cheeses, Lofotlam (a Geographical Indication-protected local lamb), and vegetables. “For us, knowing where things come from matters, even with the objects we use," Ingunn said. The exquisite tableware we were dining on, for instance, was made by local ceramicist Gunvor Tangrand, whose organic forms pay homage to the surroundings and textured surfaces incorporate old fishing yarn.

Orlando had arrived in Lofoten a few days prior with a case full of his favorite building blocks—barley, Danish yellow peas, several kinds of miso—but was most excited to work with what he could find in the immediate surroundings. He invited me to step outside to forage for his dinner menu, and we returned with chickweed, corn shoots, and sheep sorrel. “I love cooking in remote places,” he said. “Thinking on the fly and exploring the potential in things you’ve never worked with is where the coolest ideas come from.”

The writer foraging for herbs with chef Matt Orlando

Fishbone noodles reflect Matt Orlando's zero-waste philosophy.

Orlando, like Ingunn, is a bit of a visionary. Some years ago, he opened Amass, a circular restaurant in Copenhagen’s industrial Refshaleøen neighborhood, where friendly service and an approachable, low-waste tasting menu were paired with a harborside produce garden diners could wander through. Amass has since closed, but last year, Orlando opened Esse in a restored Nordhavn warehouse, and it received its first Michelin star just a few months later. Signature dishes, including his fermented potato bread, made the move, along with his commitment to environmental responsibility. At Holmen, that philosophy is a natural fit.

Later that night, as we gathered around communal tables adorned with wildflowers and candlelight, we squealed with delight when the potato bread arrived with allium butter, then again for the fishbone noodles, a masterful creation made by pressure-cooking fish bones and puréeing them until smooth. The collagen-rich mixture gives the noodles their slippery texture, which Orlando complements with smoked chili, sunflower-seed miso, and fermented chili cream. Served family-style, the meal was messy and fun, with utensils set aside so we could savor the main course, the reindeer, with our hands. As a pescatarian, I happily dug into my dish: hedgehog mushrooms grilled with chili-strawberry barbecue sauce and served with Lofoten chimichurri.

Foraging for Rowanberries in the hillsides around Holmen

The weather changes quickly in the North. Quiet and calm one minute, then wild and Baroque the next. “Four seasons in an hour,” joked Holmen. It’s this unique confluence of climatic conditions that makes Lofoten such a productive landscape: the interaction between the warmer Gulf Stream and cold Arctic waters supports marine life; the long hours of sunlight send plants into hyperdrive; and the sea spray nourishes lamb grazing pastures. “This land and sea have nourished us for generations,” says Ingunn, who leads guests on foraging walks, returning with fireweed flowers for immune-boosting tea, cloudberries for jam, or lovage for a vibrant, savory butter.

But to really understand this landscape is to understand its enduring connection to one particular food source: skrei, or Arctic cod. Here, tepee-style wooden drying racks known as hjeller are as much a visual fixture as Lofoten’s mountains; their distinctive frames have dotted the terrain for more than a thousand years. The racks are empty when I visit, but come January they will be stacked with skrei that have been caught at the end of their annual migration, from the Barents Sea to Lofoten’s milder waters where they spawn. Transformed into tørrfisk (wind-dried stockfish), this seasonal bounty has sustained the local economy for centuries. However, like many other coastal societies shaped by the migration and economics of Atlantic cod, Lofoten has been no stranger to ecosystem pressures over the decades, from shifting migration patterns to offshore fishing fleets.

Perhaps, it is no surprise then, that a more diversified agricultural movement is taking root. One rainy afternoon, I departed from the cozy confines of Holmen and drove an hour north to the fishing village of Napp, home to Lofoten Seaweed Company, whose products supply Holmen’s kitchens—and line our bathroom shelves. Co-founded by Angelita Eriksen and Tamara Singer, this entirely women-led team dons their wetsuits and hand-harvests seven types of local seaweed, including sugar kelp and nori, from the strong tidal currents of straits like Nappstraumen, transforming them into prized seasonings, even toiletries. Singer showed us around the processing facility inside a traditional rorbu, where Eriksen once cut out cod tongues—"a common way for kids in this region to make a bit of pocket money," Singer explained—and where they now sort, store, and age seaweed for top kitchens like Noma and Geranium in Copenhagen.

Holmen’s prep table, heaped with treats gathered from the wild landscape outside

Norwegian scallops with ocean truffle and shaved hazelnuts

They’re not the only ones. Across the islands, artisanal cheesemakers and farmers growing root vegetables and edible flowers are cultivating the region’s rich ecosystem. Back at Holmen, I spoke with local Bjørg-Elin Kärn, who specializes in food and agriculture systems at Love Food, an organization that connects small-scale producers with local restaurants. “Lamb, stockfish, and seaweed are all remarkably sustainable ways of producing food,” she said. “But conservation only works when the people living on the land and by the sea can make a good living. When a restaurant chooses these products, it helps keep that whole system alive.”

Matt Orlando with Holmen Lofoten's kitchen team

Kärn also works to steer tourism toward local food and agriculture, rather than simply encouraging visitors to “hitch tents and climb mountains.” We talked about the growing toll of [the wrong kind of] mountain tourism—a sore point among residents. Holmen’s own mountaineering guide Audun prefers not to share the names or locations of ascents anymore. He fears that overexposure on social media will draw the wrong kind of attention, not least from underprepared climbers who underestimate both the difficulty of the routes and the whims of the weather. A few years ago, Lofoten banned wild camping, restricting campers to designated grounds.

It is clear that tourism has come knocking on Lofoten’s door. In 2025, inbound visitor growth in Northern Norway as a whole, and Lofoten in particular, outpaced the rest of the country, bringing with it a familiar tension: how to welcome visitors while protecting what makes a place worth visiting in the first place. “Our hope is that people come to Lofoten for the right reasons,” Ingunn told me. “Ultimately, that will be what helps us protect the islands and our way of life.” Food, increasingly an anchor to how people travel, offers a way to bridge that gap, inviting visitors in while helping them understand the place they’ve come to experience. “Here, you can’t separate food production and landscape,” said Kärn. “Much of what visitors come to see, whether they know it or not, is shaped by food.”

In this way, Kitchen on the Edge hopes to sound a call for travelers to rethink why and how they visit places like Lofoten. Hospitality that takes guests beyond the plate and into the fields isn’t a new concept, but here it’s more than a perk: it’s a way of life.

One morning, when the weather cleared just enough, we piled into inflatable boats that cut through choppy waters over to an uninhabited island. Once a thriving fishing village, the island was abandoned when motorboats became commonplace in the mid-20th century, and fishing communities no longer needed to live close to spawning grounds. Today, nature has reclaimed this idyllic spot—where Ingunn spent childhood summer days picking berries and swimming—with waterfalls and carpets of reindeer moss.

While others traced the shore on foot, I sat by the jetty and took it all in: the mountains disappearing into the sea, the pair of white-tailed eagles circling overhead, the tiny berries and subarctic flora underfoot. There’s something that happens to your brain when you’re constantly zooming out to take in the big picture, then narrowing your focus to the smallest details. I later learned there’s a name for it: attentional elasticity, a psychological framework that helps you anchor yourself in the present moment. It might be why I felt such a stillness, despite the fog of jet lag and the pull of the action around me.

The writer standing on an atmospheric edge in Å, a picturesque village at the southernmost tip of Moskenes island

...to Henningsvær, a fairytale fishing village two hours north of Holmen. Its main street is lined with shops selling handblown glass and cozy knits, and colorful boats bob along its winding waterways. Check into Trevarefabrikken, a photogenic boutique hotel and cultural hub in a mossy-yellow building that once housed a cod liver oil factory. Take an early morning stroll to the football field, famous for its dramatic setting, then browse contemporary art at the gallery in the old caviar factory. Warm up with fish soup at Klatrekafeen before hitting the seafront sauna back at the hotel.

The hotel crew built a campfire around which we ate fish soup, taking turns making pinnebrød by winding dough around birch sticks and cooking it over the flames. A flask of aquavit—holy water around these parts—made an appearance.

At Holmen, encounters with nature became part of the daily rhythm. We foraged not just for food but for art, too, gathering moss, mushrooms, and branches heavy with berries to create wild tablescapes with Becky Hindley, a British botanical artist. In a woodworking cabin into which the sunlight poked through optimistically, we spent hours with Lee John Phillips, a woodcarver from Wales, fashioning spoons out of local birch. It’s a particular kind of luxury that’s rooted in knowing how to build a life from what the land gives you.

As in any home, the kitchen here is the heart of the house, so we kept returning to it: to watch chefs pry open Icelandic queen scallops and slice basketfuls of chanterelles and hedgehog mushrooms. Orlando was always within reach, sharing recipes and starting conversations about food systems. Unlike other modern culinary festivals, there was no peeking behind the curtain, because, in fact, there is no curtain. “This is my dream for a restaurant,” Orlando said on the night of his final presentation, as we savored grilled pollock with chard shishito butter and sipped a remarkable oat milk drink flavored with green cardamom, rose, and spiced apple. “To be able to have diners weave in and out and chat as I cook.”

As it happened, the clear blue skies we were hoping for never showed for the four days I was there. But the astonishing dishes served beside a crackling oven, the easy camaraderie around the dinner table, and the ever-present possibility of the Northern Lights left me more awake to the world than I’d felt in months.

#2900. Iceland, One of Three Countries That Still Hunts Whales, Considers Ban

A fin whale being processed in Hvalfjörður, Iceland in June. Micah Garen / Four Corners Media

Iceland is one of just three countries, along with Norway and Japan, that still allows commercial whaling in its waters, but that may be about to change. The Icelandic government will put forward a bill in February that could ban the practice.

The Icelandic government will allow for as many as 134 minke whales and 150 fin whales to be killed this year by its last remaining whaling company, Hvalur. While minke whales are not a threatened species, fin whales are classified as vulnerable to extinction by the International Union for Conservation of Nature. So far this year, 80 adult fin whales have been killed in Iceland, where authorities have found that harpooned animals often endure immense suffering. A 2023 report released by the Icelandic Food and Veterinary Authority determined that some harpooned whales can take up to two hours to die.The government is now looking to update its whaling law, it says, and will consider a permanent ban on whaling. “This is a really significant milestone in efforts to consign to history Iceland’s cruel and needless whale hunts,” said Wendy Higgins, a spokesperson for Humane World for Animals.

Over the past two decades, Icelandic whalers have killed more than 1,000 fin whales, according to Humane World for Animals, which says that most of the whale meat produced is consumed not by Icelanders but by visiting tourists.
