#3302. Risks Loom for Chinese Dam Project Being Built on Active Fault Line

The Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon in Tibet, China, site of a massive dam project. Kobe Jiang via Flickr

As China erects the largest hydropower installation on Earth atop an active fault line in the Himalayas, Chinese scientists are calling on engineers to guard against risks from earthquakes.

The project broke ground last year in the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon in Tibet, the longest and deepest canyon in the world. When completed, the $167 billion installation will consist of a series of five dams and have a generating capacity of 70 gigawatts, roughly the total power capacity of Poland.

But scientists warn that Tibet is prone to earthquakes, putting the project at risk, the South China Morning Post reports. Early last year, a 7.1 magnitude earthquake near the border with Nepal killed more than 120 people and destroyed more than 3,600 buildings.

In a recent paper, scientists noted that the terrain at the construction site is “loose structure and weak cohesion.” Writing in Sedimentary Geology and Tethyan Geology, a scientific journal overseen by the China Geological Survey, they said that “under the influence of fault activity and earthquakes, instability of the slopes on both sides of the reservoir area can be extremely easily triggered.” Scientists called for reinforcing slopes and installing retaining walls to reduce the risk of landslides.

Along with the seismic risks, scientists tell Yale Environment 360 that the project poses a risk to wildlife. The river gorge is home to snow leopards, Bengal tigers, and Tibetan brown bears, as well as a thousand-year-old cypress that, at more than 330 feet, is the tallest tree in Asia.

Further downstream, the Yarlung Tsangpo River runs into neighboring India, where officials fear the dam project will give China the ability to withhold water or unleash floods in a future conflict. To buffer against future “water bombs,” India is planning its own massive dam on its side of the border.

#3305. A farewell to BioWare, maker of some of the best games of the 21st century | Lucy James

The closure of the studio responsible for some of the best games of the 21st century leaves the world of RPGs feeling smaller without the hope of another instalment of the much-loved sci-fi series

Earlier this week, EA announced the closure of BioWare, a game studio with a 30-year history of compelling role-playing games, complex characters and bold, choice-driven narratives. Its forthcoming game in the Mass Effect sci-fi series has been cancelled, and more than 400 people are losing their jobs. As someone who has worked in the video games industry for 15 years and spent almost 20 as a BioWare fan, I am among millions of mourners today.

It’s not terribly hard to see why BioWare got the chop. Its parent company has been saddled with $20bn of debt thanks to a recent leveraged buyout involving Jared Kushner and the Saudi wealth fund. EA was looking for weight to cut loose, and despite its hardcore fanbase and reputation for genre-defining RPGs such as Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic and Dragon Age, BioWare hadn’t put out a critical or commercial hit in over 10 years. Its forthcoming game, titled Mass Effect: Universe according to a leaked trailer, was announced back in 2020. Despite some flashy teases, it was still years away from release.

The three games that comprise original space-faring Mass Effect trilogy count as my favourites of all time. I first played in 2010, shortly after buying my Xbox 360 and being reassured by my friends that the game was a worthy investment, and have replayed them countless times since. Depending on the day, the top spot fluctuates; more often than not it’s the character-driven sequel Mass Effect 2, which ends with one of the most iconic missions in gaming, that takes the top spot.

Released over a five-year period from 2007-12, the trilogy follows the adventures of Commander Shepard: a lawless Spectre special agent who is empowered by the galactic Citadel Council to use any means necessary to protect its interests and get the job done. Shepard’s appointment as the first human Spectre in a galaxy where humans are the new kids on the block aligns with the arrival of the Reapers, an ancient synthetic race that raze all organic life every 50,000 years. Shepard must convince the galaxy that the threat is real and gather its top soldiers, scientists and scoundrels to defeat it.

When I played Mass Effect for the first time, I didn’t get it. Its controls felt janky and awkward, and I was thrown headlong into a galaxy full of history and politics that I didn’t understand: the game didn’t spell things out, throwing out names and jargon and expecting me to follow. After one exceedingly frustrating encounter with the Mako, the game’s infamously awkward land buggy, I quit entirely. Some months later, something drew me back: the feeling of a job left half done. The galaxy was in peril, and I had to save it.

The second time I tried, something clicked. I focused less on the gameplay quirks and went all-in on its story and characters: the naive archaeologist, Dr Liara T’Soni; the browbeaten Citadel security guard Garrus Vakarian; and Kaidan Alenko, a human enhanced by the military, and suffering because of it. Mass Effect’s story was simple – save the galaxy from a tormenting synthetic race with guns and space powers – but you got to determine how it played out. Players were given the opportunity to make meaningful choices that hugely affected how the story unfolded, and that stuck with me (and millions of others).

Throughout the course of the game, you can shape not only Shepard’s gender and what they look like, but how they react to situations. Would they be friendly, and use some inter-species cooperation to defeat the Reapers? Or take the renegade route, tersely shutting down dissenters, sometimes resulting in hilariously out-of-pocket responses? (My apologies to the intrepid space reporter Khalistah al-Jilani.) Regardless of how you played, it was thrilling to have such agency over how Shepard moved and was perceived in the world. But the real fun was in making decisions that altered the story not only in Mass Effect, but its sequels.

I agonised over every choice, large or small. Across three games and the far flung reaches of the galaxy, my influence was felt. Races lived and died at my whim, and the consequences of my actions showed up in the most unexpected ways. It made my playthrough feel truly mine, guided by the stern hand of its development team, but shaped by me, and me alone. Shepard’s plight was mine, and I would save the galaxy. I hadn’t played anything like it at the time, and even now, I struggle to think of moments that compare. Its missions and story beats are so ingrained in my mind that you’d think I actually lived them. Such is the wonder of a video game, the freedom to explore and affect the world you’re playing in.

Mass Effect’s story wouldn’t have hit as hard if it weren’t for its characters: a motley crew of the galaxy’s toughest and cleverest, drawn to your cause for their own, complex reasons. It questioned whether AI had a soul, the sins of the father, and the heaviness of duty. BioWare wrote missions personalised to your squadmates’ causes, asking you to make agonising decisions that could benefit or harm them, and then forcing you to deal with the results. The writers went one further, allowing you to date some of these characters, slowly building from nervous flirting to ratings-friendly romance scenes. Saying goodbye to your squad, who’d been with you through hell, before the third game’s devastating final act brought me to tears over a decade ago. It still does.

Keza MacDonald's weekly look at the world of gaming

It’s been over a decade since Shepard’s story concluded, and the trilogy is still lauded as among the greatest ever made. I play dozens of games a year, and few have stuck with me as Mass Effect has. Its influence is impossible to shake. It set the standard for action-RPGs for years to come, and despite a furore at its original ending (since changed due to public outcry), is still beloved and widely referenced in gaming culture.

BioWare’s ending feels so final, so abrupt. Despite missteps and mismanagement in recent years, most publicly in the form of failed sci-fi shooter Anthem, getting a glimpse at what the new Mass Effect might have been has left fans bereft. The foundation of the universe was so strong, and in the right hands it could have been another genre-defining story. The loss of BioWare is an enormous one for gaming, a bastion of western role playing games brought down. Its influence will continue to be felt throughout the gaming galaxy.

Lucy James is founder and curator of lookingfor.game and co-host of the Friends Per Second gaming podcast.

#3306. What is a Harvest Moon?

A Harvest Moon is what we call the full moon that occurs close to the first day of autumn ( the autumn equinox ).

In 2025, people will be able to see a Harvest Moon on the evening of October 6th. This moon will also be a supermoon, meaning it will be extra bright and big!

Supermoons occur when the moon is at perigee (its closest point to Earth), so it appears bigger and brighter than usual.

The Harvest Moon is named for its bright light, which helped farmers work later into the evening than usual.

A Harvest Moon appears orange when it’s near the horizon, as its light travels through a greater portion of the Earth’s atmosphere.

Harvest moons are sometimes called the Corn Moon or the Barley Moon.

The Harvest Moon can fall in either September or October, depending on when the full moon is closest to the autumn equinox.

#3307. A plan to ensure energy is affordable for all | Letters

Dr Alex Chapman calls for a government-funded price cap on an essential block of electricity and gas, in response to an article by Nils Pratley

Nils Pratley thinks that the secretary of state, Miatta Fahnbulleh, should “keep things simple” and develop a targeted social tariff to protect vulnerable households from surging energy bills (The UK energy secretary is flirting with a bad idea for bills – the ‘rising block tariff, 14 October). Unfortunately, there is nothing simple about such a scheme.

Pratley’s proposal would mean creating a new benefits system inside our privatised energy industry. If the interminable debates about our current benefits system were not enough to put him off, we can look to the failing social care system to see where it might lead. Andy Burnham has made it his life’s mission to dismantle this lottery that can strip pensioners of their savings and the family home. Just as he now plans a universal public provision of adult social care, we at the New Economics Foundation have such a plan for energy.

Every household should be able to afford the energy essentials: enough warmth to survive a winter; enough electricity to switch on the lights and run a fridge. We should recognise this as a universal right rather than waste time tinkering with a targeting system that arbitrarily separates the “worthy” from the “unworthy” (the “rough justice” of the Resolution Foundation’s proposal, which penalises households based on the income of the highest earner, is a case in point).

The long-term design of a universal offer can be debated. But as another energy crisis bears down on us, we should keep it simple: have the Treasury fund a true price cap on an essential block of electricity and gas. This would be a policy from which everyone wins (especially if electrically heated homes are allowed to transfer across their gas allowance).

In the longer term, one way to help pay for this could be to charge households with very high energy consumption more. But there are other ways. The Australian “solar sharer” scheme gives every household three hours of free electricity in the middle of the day, bringing hope that renewables could soon give true energy security. Dr Alex Chapman Head of economic and environmental policy, New Economics Foundation

Nature (2026) Cite this article

Anaerobic degradation of complex organic compounds to methane and carbon dioxide is common in the deep subsurface biosphere and has long been considered to require syntrophic cooperation, with methanogens confined to the terminal use of simple C1 and C2 substrates. Thermodynamic considerations, multi-omics analyses and microcosm experiments suggest that archaeal lineages are capable of methanogenic degradation of complex substrates1,2,3,4,5,6. However, experimental validation in pure culture was lacking. Here we report the isolation of a thermophilic archaeon, Methanoglobus dolicholipophagus AA-75, affiliated with Archaeoglobi, from a Chinese petroleum reservoir. Strain AA-75 not only converts methanol into methane, carbon dioxide and acetate, but also uses saturated fatty acids varying from C7:0 to C18:0 and unsaturated fatty acids including C16:1, C18:1 and C18:2 to produce methane. We term the latter lipotrophic methanogenesis, which integrates β-oxidation of fatty acids with the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway and the canonical methyl-tetrahydromethanopterin:coenzyme M methyltransferase and methyl-coenzyme M reductase complexes, together with an intracellular electron transfer system. A formaldehyde-methanol branch probably functions as an auxiliary route for C1 metabolism during methanogenesis. Methane produced from stearate is moderately 13C-enriched (δ13C–CH4 = −40.0‰ to −36.3‰), distinct from the strong 13C depletion characteristic of canonical methanogenic pathways. These findings demonstrate that methanogens can function as primary degraders of complex organic matter, challenging the classical syntrophic paradigm and calling for refinement of current models of carbon cycling in the deep subsurface biosphere.

#3310. Europe Saw Deaths Spike During Brutal June Heat Wave

Late June was significantly hotter than normal across much of Europe. Copernicus Climate Change Service

Western Europe recorded more than 10,000 excess deaths during an unprecedented June heat wave, an analysis finds.

Last month was the hottest June on record in Western Europe, measuring 5.5 degrees F warmer than the recent average, according to the E.U. Copernicus Climate Change Service. The June data “underscored how profoundly the climate is changing,” said Samantha Burgess, deputy director of Copernicus.

During the late June hot spell, temperatures in parts of Europe were 16 degrees F warmer than usual, according to Copernicus. Britain saw heat surpassing 98 degrees F, while France recorded temperatures up to 109 degrees F.

With the stifling heat came a spike in deaths. Western European countries saw 10,000 more deaths than would usually be expected in late June, according to EuroMOMO, a network backed by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control. Of those, more than 9,000 were among people 65 or older, a group that is highly vulnerable to extreme heat.

The increase in deaths runs counter to typical seasonal trends. Normally, mortality peaks in the middle of winter, as cold settles in and flu spreads, before reaching a low in the summer. “To have this kind of excess at this time of year is unusual. It’s ​really high,” Lasse Vestergaard, of the Statens Serum Institut in Denmark, which hosts EuroMOMO, told Reuters. “It is difficult to explain this high excess mortality by anything but the extreme ​heat.”

A separate study found that in England and Wales alone, the June heat wave was responsible for more than 2,200 deaths. The analysis further estimated the number of deaths that were a result of the excess heat brought about by global warming. It found that 38 percent of mortalities could be attributed to climate change.

“It’s time we woke up to the fact that we now live in a country with dangerously hot summers,” said Clair Barnes of Imperial College London. “To protect people during future extremes, we must urgently adapt to the reality of the climate we now have and double down on global efforts to reach net zero emissions to stop this from getting worse.”

#3311. Indien: Der Mut der Frauen hinter den "Kakerlaken"-Protesten

Trotz Polizeigewalt, Festnahmen und Online-Hetze gehen Frauen in Indien weiter auf die Straße. Sie lassen sich nicht einschüchtern und weigern sich, in die Opferrolle gedrängt zu werden.

Am 20. Juli machten sich Navkiran Natt und ihr Partner auf den Weg zum Büro des Magistrats in Neu-Delhi, um zu heiraten. Dort angekommen, trafen sie auf eine angespannte Lage: Die Polizei war aufmarschiert, um einen von Jugendlichen angeführten Marsch zum Parlament aufzulösen.

Die Mitarbeiter der Behörde ebenso wie die Trauzeugen des Paares, verspäteten sich, da der Weg durch Barrikaden versperrt war.

Die Protestbewegung, bekannt als "Cockroach Janta Party" (CJP), hatte den Marsch organisiert, um den Rücktritt des damaligen indischen Bildungsministers Dharmendra Pradhan zu fordern.

Die Demonstrierenden stießen auf erbitterten Widerstand der Sicherheitskräfte, die Schlagstöcke, Tränengas und Schrotflinten mit Projektil-Munition einsetzten, was zu Verletzungen und zahlreichen Festnahmen führte.

Knapp drei Monate später, am 10. Oktober, verließ Natt frühmorgens ihr Zuhause und verbrachte den Tag damit, die abgeriegelte indische Hauptstadt zu durchqueren – auf der Suche nach einem Protest, dem sie sich anschließen konnte.

"Der Erfolg dieses Protests bemisst sich nicht daran, wie viele Menschen Jantar Mantar erreichen", sagt sie gegenüber der DW – in Bezug auf den Protestort im Zentrum von Neu-Delhi.

"Der Erfolg liegt vielmehr darin, dass die Menschen trotz all der Barrikaden und der staatlichen Gewalt ihre Häuser verlassen, um sich dem Protest anzuschließen."

Die CJP entstand als satirische Online-Bewegung, nachdem der indische Oberste Richter Surya Kant in einer beiläufigen Bemerkung arbeitslose junge Menschen als "Kakerlaken" bezeichnet hatte. Der Name ist eine ironische Anspielung auf die Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) – die Partei von Premierminister Narendra Modi, die derzeit an der Macht ist.

Nachdem die Proteste der Bewegung Ende Juli zum Rücktritt von Bildungsminister Pradhan führten, fordert sie nun den Rücktritt des umstrittenen indischen Chef-Wahlleiters Gyanesh Kumar. Hintergrund ist die Streichung von mehr als 130 Millionen Namen aus den Entwürfen der Wählerverzeichnisse im ganzen Land.

Da sich die Führung der CJP in richterlich angeordnetem Gewahrsam befindet oder ihr die Inhaftierung droht, ist ein Name zum Synonym für die Proteste geworden: Die studentische Anführerin Neha Bora, die für sich das geschlechtsneutrale Pronomen "they/them" verwendet.

Bora ist vor der Polizei durch die Straßen gerannt und vor den Augen der Sicherheitskräfte auf Barrikaden geklettert, hat aus Polizeifahrzeugen heraus Parolen gerufen und gemeinsam mit CJP-Sprechern und anderen an Pressekonferenzen teilgenommen.

Sie ist Vorsitzende der linken Studierendenorganisation AISA, "All India Students' Association", der Studierendenorganisation der Kommunistischen Partei Indiens (Marxistisch-Leninistisch) – Liberation (CPI-ML).

"In Zeiten, die zutiefst enttäuschend, beängstigend und tyrannisch sind, versuchen die Menschen, irgendwoher Hoffnung zu schöpfen… Wenn diese Leute kämpfen, dann können wir vielleicht auch kämpfen", sagte Bora gegenüber der DW.

"Das ist eine Verantwortung, die man den Menschen gegenüber hat", fügte sie/er hinzu.

Die von der "Kakerlaken"-Gruppe angeführten Demonstrationen finden zudem in einer Zeit statt, in der insbesondere Frauen nach der Gruppenvergewaltigung einer 17-Jährigen Ende September in Neu-Delhi mehr Sicherheit und Selbstbestimmung für Frauen fordern.

Nach Angaben des "National Crime Records Bureau" wurden im Jahr 2024 insgesamt 441.534 Straftaten gegen Frauen registriert – das entspricht durchschnittlich 1.210 Fällen pro Tag. Experten weisen darauf hin, dass diese offiziellen Statistiken nur einen Teil der geschlechtsspezifischen Gewalt im Land abbilden.

Doch obwohl sie sich auf der Straße mit sexualisierten Übergriffen durch die Polizei und im Internet mit Hetzkampagnen konfrontiert sehen, wehren sich die Frauen und genderqueeren Personen, die die Bewegung anführen, dagegen, in die Opferrolle gedrängt zu werden.

"Der Slogan 'Beti Bachao' [Rettet die Tochter] zieht bei dieser Generation einfach nicht mehr. Die Forderung ist ganz klar: Betrachtet uns als politische Wesen, als freie Menschen", erklärte die Aktivistin Kavita Krishnan gegenüber der DW.

"Beti Bachao, Beti Padhao" (Rettet die Tochter, bildet die Tochter aus) ist ein zentrales Regierungsprogramm der BJP, das 2015 ins Leben gerufen wurde.

"Widerstand darf kein isoliertes Ereignis sein – kein einzelner großer Tag, nach dem man einfach zu seinem gewohnten Leben zurückkehrt", sagt Gurmehar Kaur gegenüber der DW.

Die 29-Jährige ist Autorin des Buchs "The Young and the Restless: Youth and Politics in India" über Jugendproteste in Indien.

Kaur erklärte, ihr politisches Engagement verschaffe ihr Zugang zu Ressourcen, mit denen die Bewegung unterstützt werden könne; so entstehe ein Sicherheitsnetz für Demonstrierende – "ungeachtet ihrer politischen Zugehörigkeit".

In einer Zeit, in der die Behörden hart gegen die Protestierenden vorgehen, sieht sie für sich und andere Mitglieder der Opposition eine klare Pflicht: "Präsenz zeigen und den eigenen Körper einsetzen."

Kaur berichtet, dass ihre männlichen Kollegen oft "sehr erfreut sind, wenn ihre Fotos in den Medien auftauchen". Für sie hingegen bedeute Aufmerksamkeit vor allem eines: Mord- und Vergewaltigungsdrohungen, Demütigungen und Misshandlungen.

Navkiran Natt, eine Aktivistin, die vor allem durch die Bauernproteste der Jahre 2020 und 2021 bekannt wurde, bestätigt Kaurs Erfahrungen.

"Wenn eine Frau einen Protest anführt, ist ihre Sexualität das erste Ziel von Angriffen", sagt sie. Auf Bildern in den sozialen Medien sind Demonstrierende zu sehen, deren Kleidung bei Auseinandersetzungen mit der Polizei zerrissen wurde.

Offizielle Stellen in Delhi wiesen die Vorwürfe der Misshandlung zurück und erklärten, die weiblichen Demonstrierenden seien "würdevoll" behandelt worden.

Die Studierendenvertreterin Bora widersprach dieser Darstellung der Polizei. Sie wies darauf hin, dass für die große Zahl an weiblichen Demonstrierenden nur sehr wenige Polizeibeamtinnen zur Verfügung standen.

"Mehrere Demonstrierende kamen, um mich zu schützen und zu verhindern, dass ich festgenommen werde", berichtete sie.

Die Behörden haben ein Strafverfahren gegen eine Journalistin eingeleitet, die einen hochrangigen Polizeibeamten in Delhi der sexuellen Belästigung beschuldigt hatte.

Das Verfahren stützt sich auf ein indisches Gesetz, das eigentlich dazu dienen soll, Straftaten gegen Angehörige der sogenannten "Scheduled Castes" (registrierte Kasten) und "Scheduled Tribes" (registrierte Stämme) zu verhindern. Nach Angaben der Behörden werden die Vorwürfe der Journalistin selbst derzeit untersucht.

Zudem kursieren im Internet zahlreiche KI-generierte, sexualisierte Bilder von protestierenden Frauen, die nachträglich in Fotos von Demonstrationen eingefügt wurden.

Dies hat die Protestierenden nicht davon abgehalten, auf die Straße zu gehen. Die Behörden gingen offenbar davon aus, dass sexuelle Demütigungen die Frauen abschrecken würden, so die Aktivistin Krishnan. Doch "diese Generation betrachtet sexuelle Gewalt nicht als etwas, das mit Scham behaftet ist."

Krishnan war bereits nach der Gruppenvergewaltigung im Fall "Nirbhaya" im Jahr 2012 an vorderster Front bei den Protesten dabei – damals waren in der Öffentlichkeit Slogans wie "Hängt die Vergewaltiger" zu lesen.

Diesmal jedoch, so Krishnan, gehe es bei der Wut "nicht um ein männlich geprägtes Ehrverständnis." Es gehe um "Würde und Freiheit... Frauen sehen sich in jedem Land mit verschiedensten Problemen konfrontiert – da bilden indische Frauen keine Ausnahme", fügte sie hinzu.

Laut Krishnan sind die von jungen Menschen angeführten Proteste eine Reaktion auf eine breitere globale Welle des "zivilisatorischen und kulturellen Nationalismus". It’s all rendered in incredible audiovisual detail, delivering the spectacle we expect from the series. I’m not sure it’ll bring in a vast new audience, especially with GTA 6 on the way – Call of Duty remains a gung-ho Hollywood fantasy of armed conflict that glorifies the military-industrial complex. But if it’s a glossy, bombastic thrill ride you’re after, there is plenty here to keep you locked and loaded for months to come.

#3327. Beyond Lithium: New Battery Tech Starts to Break Through

Workers manufacture sodium-ion batteries, an alternative to lithium-ion batteries, at Namey New Energy Technology in Luoyang, China. Zhang Yixi / VCG via Getty Images

As EV sales boom and grids seek more energy storage, researchers are racing to develop batteries that are cheaper, more powerful, and less reliant on hard-to-source materials. Lithium-ion still dominates, but sodium-ion and solid-state technologies are moving from lab to market.

The market for batteries these days is insatiable. Demand has grown more than fortyfold since 2010, thanks mainly to electric cars: Sales of EVs hit 20 million in 2025, or about a quarter of all cars sold globally. Shipping containers packed with batteries are also being called into play to store the electricity from renewables like solar. Storage capacity for the grid has grown twentyfold in just five years.

This boom has fed a frenzy in battery research and development. “In the past five years, innovation went very, very fast,” says Teo Lombardo, a former battery chemist and now an analyst for the International Energy Agency. “In 2024, over 40 percent of energy-related patents were on batteries. That’s never happened before. That tells you how quickly the market is evolving, and how much interest there is.”

Lithium-ion batteries are today’s gold standard for lightweight, high-powered energy storage for laptops, power tools, smartphones, drones, and electric cars. But now, says Lombardo, two new technologies are attacking lithium-ion’s dominance from either end of the cost spectrum: Cheap but bulky sodium batteries promise to run budget electric vehicles and help to power the grid; and expensive but powerful solid-state batteries offer long ranges for luxury EVs. Meanwhile, plenty of other battery chemistries are being tested in the lab, with hopes that new winners might eventually emerge to power the future.

“The battery market is becoming so large that it’s not a matter of one technology replacing another,” says Lombardo. “It’s about specializing to serve different parts of the market.”

Making a battery is fundamentally simple: A child can create one out of a lemon, a galvanized nail, a copper penny, and a couple of wires. Chemical reactions occur between the two electrodes — the cathode and the anode — and the lemon-juice electrolyte that’s sandwiched in between them, driving electrons through the wires to light up a bulb.

But making a really good battery — one that packs a lot of power into a small package, is cheap to manufacture, survives through a decade of charging cycles without degrading or starting fires, works in the cold of winter and the heat of summer, and can be easily refurbished or recycled — is another thing entirely. Creating good batteries is such a delicate work of chemistry and engineering that it typically requires decades of research to get it right. “It’s like a symphony. Everything needs to cooperate,” says Jagjit Nanda, a materials scientist and head of the SLAC-Stanford Battery Center in Menlo Park, California.

Back in the 1970s, the oil crisis spurred investment in energy alternatives. Lithium was an obvious choice for lightweight batteries because it’s the lightest metal on the periodic table. The key innovation was designing electrodes that could safely and efficiently host lithium ions in gaps between their atomic layers, leading to the first commercial lithium-ion battery in 1991.

Source: IRENA. Yale Environment 360 / Made with Flourish

Since then, researchers have painstakingly improved lithium-ion batteries by changing the formulations of electrodes, adding small amounts of various elements to improve battery stability or ion flow, or tweaking the microstructure of interfaces between layers or the architecture of the cell. Since 1991, lithium-ion battery energy density (an important measure of how much charge can be packed into a given weight) has tripled, and the price has dropped up to a hundredfold. Eventually those improvements will tap out — but not yet. Today’s electric car batteries are almost all lithium-ion of one type or another, typically taking your average sport utility vehicle between 250 and 370 miles on a good day.

A decade ago, most EV batteries were nickel manganese cobalt (NMC) variants (named after the primary ingredients in their positive electrode, the cathode), which boast a relatively high energy density. Recently, though, these have been overtaken by cheaper lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries, named after their own cathode, which are catching up in performance and contain fewer critical metals than NMCs — expensive ingredients with limited supply chains.

Although an individual LFP battery has a lower energy density than an NMC, LFPs are more heat stable, so developers have found ways to pack them closer together in a car, counteracting this disadvantage. Other innovations in the architecture of the cell have also accelerated their charging time – some LFPs can be charged in about 10 minutes.

But lithium is difficult to mine — often involving evaporation ponds that spread over thousands of acres — and sourced from just a handful of spots. Combine that with booming demand, and prices can spike. An enticing alternative is sodium: It’s just one step down on the periodic table, with chemistry similar to lithium, but far more common. Swapping lithium for sodium in the battery manufacturing process is relatively straightforward. As it happens, sodium-ion batteries also work well at very cold temperatures.

The main problem with sodium is that its atoms take up more than twice the volume of lithium and weigh three times more. That’s an issue when you’re trying to make a small, light battery. For now, the energy density of sodium-ion batteries is about 30 percent less than their lithium competitors, taking that average SUV only about 220 miles. But work is ongoing to optimize these batteries’ components, chemistry, and architecture. “They are going to improve; it is more or less following the same trajectory as lithium-ion,” says Nanda.

A few tens of thousands of sodium-ion-powered electric cars entered the market in China in 2023, and the first car slated for mass production was unveiled in February. For now, LFPs are price competitive with the sodium upstarts because of economies of scale. But analysts expect sodium to add a cheaper battery variety to the overall mix, with the possibility of domestic supply chains. “The more technologies you have, the more resilient the market will be,” says Lombardo.

The Changan Nevo A06, unveiled in Yakeshi, China, in February, will be the first mass-produced car powered by a sodium-ion battery. CATL

Meanwhile, sodium’s bulk is no issue for stationary applications, which makes sodium-ion batteries an obvious contender for electricity grid storage. Right now, solar farms typically use lithium-ion batteries to store energy for rainy days. The demand for this use is huge and growing: California made history in April 2024 when batteries became, for the first time, the biggest source of power to the state’s grid for a few hours after sunset. Sodium batteries could help fill this booming market with batteries that have easier-to-source ingredients. In May, the world’s biggest battery company, China-based CATL, signed a deal to provide a massive 60 gigawatt-hours of sodium batteries for energy storage in Ningde, Fujian — enough stored-up electricity to run thousands of homes for a year.

Solid-state batteries, on the other hand, are a promising way to pack more oomph into a car, potentially doubling energy densities and getting your SUV more than 600 miles down the road. The idea is to swap out the organic electrolyte liquid with a compact solid, like ceramic. The substitution mitigates a bunch of challenges faced by conventional lithium-ion batteries: It eliminates the most flammable component, for one, and it enables the use of electrode materials that provide a higher energy density — like lithium metal. But sticking the parts of a solid cell together is tricky and the cost of manufacturing is high.

Solid-state batteries already exist for critical, and often non-rechargeable, applications like pacemakers. Now, scaled-up production of affordable, rechargeable, lithium-based solid-state batteries for cars seems to be getting close. Toyota and Nissan both currently forecast that their solid-state batteries will be available by around 2028. Colorado-based Solid Power is working on solid-state batteries for BMW and Ford. QuantumScape, based in California, signed a deal with Honda in June. CATL is working on the concept, too. Some of the variants are entirely solid, while others, like QuantumScape’s, are hybrid systems with a little liquid, or gooey, gel-like alternatives.

“Commercialization [of solid-state batteries] by 2030 is probably realistic,” says Lombardo. But they will be expensive, making niche, high-value applications like robotics a potentially viable starter market.

These two battery types are emerging as near-term winners for EVs, but they are far from the only tech being pursued in industry or in labs. There’s an entire alphabet soup of battery types based on dozens of elements, all with various pros and cons and specific best uses. For grid storage, where space and weight aren’t an issue, flow batteries use huge tanks of liquid electrolytes to store power. In the lab, researchers are working on swapping out lithium not just with sodium but also with easy-to-source magnesium, calcium, aluminum, or zinc. Some advanced batteries ditch their anodes altogether, cutting down on weight and upping energy density. Early-days research is also pursuing entirely different ideas, including designing organic molecules that can store the energy from sunlight in chemical bonds and release it on demand.

Solid-state battery cells now being tested by BMW. Solid Power

Variants like lithium-sulfur, in which electrons move between a lithium metal anode and a sulfur cathode, have long been in the sights of battery researchers — they offer a much higher energy density than lithium-ion, with relatively cheap ingredients — but they suffer from longevity issues and they tend to swell. No one has yet cracked making a commercially viable mass market product. One even bolder option, called lithium-air, aims to “breathe” oxygen in and out to form one of the battery’s key electrodes when needed, so it doesn’t always have to carry the unnecessary weight of a metal oxide. This fiendishly difficult task might prove useful for applications that require extremely lightweight and high-powered energy — like flying taxis.

Any and all of these — just like lithium-ion, sodium, and solid-state — will take decades of work to commercialize. “Unlike other technologies, this is very incremental,” says Nanda. “You don’t expect miracles in batteries; they don’t change overnight.” Artificial intelligence has sped up the search for promising formulations for battery materials by helping to focus or speed up laborious trial-and-error experiments, says Lombardo, but it’s still a long slog.

Meanwhile researchers are also working to make batteries more environmentally sustainable, for example by eliminating toxic solvents used in manufacturing. To slim down waste, partly degraded EV batteries can still be used in energy storage for the grid or in individual homes. When fully depleted they can be recycled. We already know how to do this, but the systems need to be scaled up fast, says Lombardo. The average lifespan of a car is about 15 years, he says, and the big boom in electric cars started around 2020. That means by around 2035 we’ll be flooded with retired batteries which, if left in landfills, could start fires or leach heavy metals into soil and groundwater.

Responsible battery disposal is a matter of economics and regulation, says Lombardo, who is optimistic that both will help to create a robust recycling industry — especially since valuable metals can be extracted and re-used. There’s precedent. Lead acid batteries — the auxiliary battery used in both combustion cars and many EVs — are today one of the world’s most recycled products.

A home battery revolution is reshaping the power grid. Read more.

So, what does the future hold? For now, lithium-ion is still king: Solid-state batteries make up just 1 percent of the existing or funded global battery manufacturing capacity, and sodium just 4 percent. But that may change. “By 2035, lithium-ion will definitely still be the majority, sodium-ion will have very likely a share, solid-state might be there,” says Lombardo. And after that, he adds, “it’s probably too early to say.”

Nature (2026) Cite this article

Plant development is highly plastic, with growth and architecture continuously shaped by environmental conditions. Roots, for instance, adjust their development to local water, nutrient and soil conditions. Despite this plasticity, individual root cell types are generally viewed as largely homogeneous populations that progress along a single developmental trajectory1. Here we show that rather than progressing along a single developmental trajectory, most Arabidopsis root cell lineage trajectories exhibit a stereotyped bifid topology, as revealed by single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcript mapping. One branch is characterized by a transcriptional program that is shared across multiple cell types and enriched for genes associated with environmental responses, including abscisic acid (ABA) signalling. This state occurs reproducibly in a subset of cells across multiple cell types, independent of developmental lineage, thereby revealing an additional layer of root cell identity. It is conserved across Arabidopsis ecotypes and persists despite major perturbations in developmental identity. Moreover, it is reduced in ABA-deficient and ABA-insensitive mutants and becomes more prevalent under water-deficit conditions. Together, these findings reveal an additional layer of root cell identity that is superimposed on developmental lineage and its prevalence is modulated by environmental conditions. These results uncover a previously unrecognized source of environment-associated transcriptional heterogeneity shared across root cell types.

The root of Arabidopsis thaliana is a valuable model for studying cell differentiation in plants. Cell types are arranged in concentric layers around a central vascular bundle and arise from distinct stem cell populations maintained near the root tip in the meristem1. As stem cells divide, their descendants are displaced shootward and progressively differentiate, which creates developmental trajectories in which the spatial arrangement of cells recapitulates the temporal dynamics of differentiation (Fig. 1a).

Cells are coloured by clusters, the identity of which was determined by whole-mount HCR. c, Box plots showing the cluster-specific expression of 15 markers used for determining cluster identity via whole-mount HCR (n = 87,056 cells from 9 independent experiments). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has refined the transcriptional signatures of Arabidopsis root cell types and enabled high-resolution reconstruction of these developmental trajectories2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9. Although these studies have identified transient cell states in individual lineages, they have largely analysed each lineage independently, which may overlook transcriptional states shared across multiple cell types irrespective of developmental identity. Whether such shared transcriptional states exist and how they contribute to root cell identity remain largely unexplored.

To characterize cell states during root development, here we generated a high-resolution scRNA-seq (10x Genomics) atlas of the A. thaliana Col-0 root tip (Fig. 1b). After filtering low-quality cells, doublets and genes significantly affected by protoplast isolation, batch integration generated a reference atlas comprising 87,056 cells from 9 replicates, with a total of 25,784 detected genes (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

We identified 27 transcriptionally distinct clusters and assigned their identities by combining differential expression with optimized whole-mount hybridization chain reaction (HCR)10 and tissue clearing11 to spatially map the expression of the 15 most specific marker genes (Fig. 1b–d). This process resolved all known cell types, including distinct root cap subpopulations (early, tip and lateral) (Fig. 1b–d). One cluster located between the root cap and epidermis could not be assigned directly by marker expression. However, on the basis of its position and developmental origin of these tissues, it was annotated as ‘epidermis–meristem’ (Fig. 1b).

Cells were grouped according to their developmental lineages (epidermis, root cap, cortex, endodermis, xylem pole pericycle (XPP), phloem pole pericycle (PPP), procambium, phloem and xylem), which confirmed that ontogenic programs remain the dominant determinant of root cell identity (Fig. 1b,e). Cells from each lineage were analysed independently to reconstruct developmental trajectories. A meristematic score, derived from genes enriched in the meristematic epidermis cluster, was calculated for each lineage, and the cell with the highest score was defined as the trajectory starting point (t = 0). Trajectory topology was inferred using partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA), and developmental progression was ordered by diffusion pseudotime from t = 0 (Fig. 1e–g).

The root cap lineage exhibited the most complex topology and recapitulated known developmental events. That is, the split of lateral and tip populations and their convergence with columella cells to form the sloughing border-like cells12 (Fig. 1g). These trajectories were supported by stage-specific marker genes13,14,15 (Extended Data Fig. 1a), which further validated the inferred developmental trajectories.

Although some lineages are anticipated to be composed of distinct cell subtypes, such as phloem and xylem, which are made of metaphloem and protophloem and metaxylem and protoxylem, respectively, and the epidermis, which subdivides into root hairs and non-root hairs, each is expected to follow a single developmental trajectory from undifferentiated to differentiated states. Notably, PAGA revealed that all cell types and subcell types instead exhibit two branches (branch 1 and branch 2) that diverge at an early pseudotime point (Fig. 1g). Only the epidermis contained cells preceding this bifurcation and a clearly identifiable meristematic population. In most other lineages, branching occurred directly from t = 0, which probably reflects the difficulty in capturing the rarer inner-layer meristematic cells during protoplasting.

To independently validate the inferred topology, whole-mount HCR was used to detect early and late markers in epidermis, endodermis and XPP (Extended Data Fig. 1b–e). In all three lineages, genes predicted to be expressed near t = 0 were detected closer to the meristem than predicted late markers. Consistent with this finding, lineage-specific meristem, elongation and maturation gene signatures obtained from a previous study8 progressed as expected along pseudotime. This result provides support for the robustness of our t = 0 assignment and inferred developmental ordering (Extended Data Fig. 2a).

Independent trajectory inference using Monocle also recovered the bifid topology (Extended Data Fig. 2b–d). Cell ordering in both branches was highly correlated with diffusion pseudotime and supported by RNA velocity analyses (Extended Data Fig. 2e–g).

Together, these data show that in addition to developmental progression, cells in most cell types exhibit two alternative transcriptional states. This result therefore reveals the existence of an additional, previously unrecognized, level of complexity in cell identity.

To identify transcriptional programs associated with cell type, developmental progression and branch identity, we performed high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA). Of the 6,649 genes assigned to 25 identified co-expression modules (Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Table 3), most were enriched in specific cell types, groups of cell types or developmental stages. By contrast, modules 20 and 21 were specifically enriched in branch 1 across all lineages. This result demonstrates that there is a transcriptional program shared across multiple cell types independently of developmental identity.

a, UMAP plot of our reference atlas showing the different cell identities obtained using the trajectory analyses. b, UMAP plots showing the expression pattern (module score, Seurat) of the 25 co-expressed gene modules (M1–M25) obtained from the hdWGCNA analysis. Modules are grouped by expression pattern, and branch-1-specific modules (M20 and M21) are highlighted by a pink rectangle. c, Number of genes per co-expression module (6,649 genes in total). Across all cell types, branch 1 cells consistently contained more detected genes and higher unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts than branch 2 cells (Fig. 2c), which indicated greater overall transcriptional activity. Modules 20 and 21 were also among the largest co-expression modules identified (320 and 615 genes, respectively; Fig. 2d), which showed that this transcriptional asymmetry involves a broad coordinated gene expression program. Although the PAGA analysis did not resolve two branches in non-hair cells, enrichment of the branch-specific modules and increased transcript abundance suggest that both populations are also present in this lineage.

Given the unexpected transcriptional differences between the two branches of the same cell type, we next sought to exclude possible experimental or analytical artefacts that might have created spurious trajectory bifurcations. Branch separation was not explained by cell cycle phase, experimental batches or genes affected by protoplast isolation (Extended Data Fig. 3a–e). Because roots grown on the surface of agar experience distinct agar-facing and air-facing microenvironments, we also profiled root tips from plants grown fully embedded in agar. The bifid trajectories remained clearly detectable across all cell types, which therefore ruled out these growth microenvironments as a contributing factor (Extended Data Fig. 3f–h).

Another possibility was that the lower UMI branch (branch 2) reflected damaged cells from which fewer transcripts were captured, despite these cells not exhibiting elevated mitochondrial or chloroplastic transcript content (Extended Data Fig. 3i–j). If so, lowly expressed genes would be preferentially detected in the higher UMI branch 1, whereas highly expressed genes would be shared between both branches. Using the XPP, we found that depletion of branch-1-specific genes in branch 2 substantially exceeded that expected from expression level alone (Extended Data Fig. 4a–c). Likewise, branch separation persisted after randomly downsampling branch 1 cells to match the UMI distribution of their branch 2 counterparts and reprocessing the dataset in three independent replicates (Extended Data Fig. 4d–g). Together, these analyses indicate that the lack of activation of the branch 1 transcriptional program in branch 2 cells cannot be explained solely by their lower transcript abundance. Finally, both branches and the branch-1-specific modules were also detected in a previously published Arabidopsis root tip single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset16 (Extended Data Fig. 5). This result rules out technical differences between cells and nuclei, protoplast isolation or cell damage as explanations for the observed bifurcation.

To further demonstrate the robustness of the observed branches, we re-analysed two independent previously published Arabidopsis root tip scRNA-seq datasets7,8 (Extended Data Figs. 6 and 7). In all three cases (one snRNA-seq dataset and two scRNA-seq datasets), we recovered both branches in all cell types, together with the higher UMI and gene content of branch 1 cells and enrichment of modules 20 and 21. Indeed, branch assignment remained robust across a range of label-transfer parameters (Extended Data Figs. 5–7). The first scRNA-seq study7 did not initially identify the branches because many branch 2 cells were excluded owing to their lower UMI content. Conversely, the second study8, which integrates two additional published root scRNA-seq datasets3,4, clearly showed two branches in their uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plots and noted their presence in the epidermis, cortex and endodermis. However, these findings were not explored further.

Some of our libraries were generated from an equimolar mix of cells from Col-0 and a second ecotype, which enabled experimental determination of cell multiplet rates while simultaneously generating root tip atlases for four additional Arabidopsis ecotypes (C24, Cvi-0, Ler and Ws-2; Methods, Extended Data Fig. 8a and Supplementary Table 2). Both branches were detected across all cell lineages in all ecotypes, with the same co-expression modules enriched in branch 1 (Extended Data Fig. 8b,c). Moreover, fewer than 4% of branch-1-specific module genes were differentially expressed in any ecotype, which demonstrated that the transcriptional program underlying the branching is highly conserved (Extended Data Fig. 8e,f). Among the ecotypes analysed, only Cvi-0 showed a significantly lower proportion of branch 1 cells (relative to branch 2) compared with Col-0 and was the ecotype with the greatest overall transcriptional divergence (Extended Data Fig. 8d–f)

A previous snRNA-seq study16 also generated a snATAC–seq atlas of the root tip. Using this chromatin accessibility data alone, we identified cells corresponding to each branch, with branch 1 cells displaying elevated fractions of reads in peaks and increased transcription start site enrichment, consistent with greater transcriptional activity (Extended Data Fig. 9a–c). Notably, this increase was specific to genes belonging to the branch-1-specific modules. Through this orthogonal method, this finding confirms the transcriptional asymmetry underlying the dichotomy identified by the scRNA-seq trajectories (Extended Data Fig. 9d,e).

To independently validate this transcriptional state in vivo, we measured the transcripts of five branch-1-specific genes by whole-mount HCR in intact root tips: AT2G41660 (also known as MIZ1), AT3G04010, AT4G26080 (also known as ABI1), AT5G56550 (also known as OXS3) and AT4G24060 (also known as VDOF1) (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 10a,b). All five genes showed heterogeneous expression in the cell types in which they are expressed, with some cells exhibiting strong transcript enrichment (positive) and others showing depletion (negative). Volumetric fluorescence quantification of segmented epidermal, cortex and endodermal cells confirmed up to 20-fold differences in fluorescence intensity between cells in the same tissue layer (Fig. 3a). Canonical cell-type markers remained detectable in negative cells, which indicated that reduced branch 1 marker detection was unlikely to reflect a general failure of HCR detection (Extended Data Fig. 10c). Although branch 2 lacks a distinct transcriptional program, analyses of epidermal gene expression identified AT3G01720 as largely complementary to the branch 1 marker AT5G56550, a pattern confirmed by HCR in vivo (Fig. 3b–h). Volumetric fluorescence quantification of segmented cells across three independent roots, together with Gaussian mixture modelling of both scRNA-seq scores and HCR fluorescence ratios, identified two discrete epidermal populations corresponding to branch 1 and branch 2 cells. This result provides direct in vivo evidence for the two transcriptionally distinct cell populations in the same tissue layer (Fig. 3e–h).

Together, these independent lines of evidence demonstrate that the bifid topology reflects a reproducible biological state characterized by the activation of a coordinated transcriptional program in a subset of cells across multiple root cell types.

To investigate the biological characteristics and potential regulators of this branch-specific transcriptional program, we analysed gene ontology (GO) term enrichment in the two branch-1-specific modules 20 and 21 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 4). These modules were enriched for responses to environmental stimuli, including nutrients, water and oxygen. Terms linked to positive regulation of RNA synthesis were also enriched, consistent with the observed increase in transcriptional activity of branch 1 cells. Among the signalling pathways analysed, only ABA, a central regulator of environmental responses and the circadian clock (an integrator of multiple environmental signals), were enriched (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 4). Consequently, this cellular state will henceforth be referred to as the environment-regulated state (ERS), with branch 1 and branch 2 corresponding to ERS-positive (ERS+) and ERS-negative (ERS–) cells, respectively. These terms reflect the presence or absence of this transcriptional state at the single-cell level rather than any intrinsic difference in the capacity of individual cells to respond to environmental signals.

To complement this enrichment analysis, we investigated transcription factors (TFs) that could potentially regulate the ERS. Using an Arabidopsis DAP-seq dataset17 and predicted binding similarities from Cis-BP2 (ref. 18), we predicted putative targets for 800 TFs, filtered using hdWGCNA with the XGBoost algorithm. This process produced putative regulons for 186 TFs expressed in our atlas (average expression scores per cell identity are shown in Fig. 4b). Notably, 84 of these TFs (>45%) showed regulon enrichment in ERS+ cells, a result consistent with the elevated activity of this state (Fig. 4b). These TFs included all the profiled ones linked to ABA signalling (ABF1, ABF2, ATHB5, ATHB6, ATHB7 and HAT22)19,20,21,22,23,24,25 or known to be circadian-regulated, such as members of the REVEILLE family (RVE1, RVE2, RVE6, EPR1 and RVE8)26,27,28 as well as cycling DOF factors (CDF2–CDF5). Consistently, two ERS markers validated by HCR (Fig. 3a), AT4G26080 and AT2G41660, are known direct ABA targets29,30,31. This result provides further support that ABA signalling has a role in ERS regulation.

Because ABA was the only signalling pathway identified by GO enrichment, we functionally tested its role in ERS formation by profiling an ABA biosynthesis mutant (aba2-3)32 and a quintuple ABA receptor mutant (pyl11458)33 (Fig. 4c–e). Both mutants showed a reduced proportion of ERS+ cells across the whole root (Fig. 4d) and in almost all cell types independently, albeit not always significantly (Extended Data Fig. 11a). Consistently, the ERS-specific modules 20 and 21 were the most strongly downregulated co-expression modules, which confirmed that ABA signalling is involved in the establishment of the ERS (Fig. 4e).

The ERS is present in only a subset of cells regardless of lineage, which suggests that it is superimposed on, and independent from, developmental programs. To test this idea, we performed scRNA-seq to profile the root tips of the scr-3 mutant, which exhibits major defects in root cell identity through loss of SCARECROW (SCR) function34 (Extended Data Fig. 11b–d). Using our Col-0 reference atlas for cell-type assignment, we observed the expected depletion of endodermis and protoxylem, together with additional alterations affecting the columella and pericycle. This result is consistent with shifts towards root cap and procambium identities, respectively, and uncovers broader cell identity changes than previously described8,35,36,37 (Extended Data Fig. 11d). Despite these major developmental perturbations, ERS+ cells remained detectable across all cell populations, including those with altered identities (Extended Data Fig. 11b,c). These observations support the conclusion that the ERS represents a transcriptional state superimposed on multiple cell lineage identities regardless of cell type or developmental stage.

Our atlas incorporated a set of experiments designed to test the effect of the composition of the growth medium on cell identities. Given the well-established role of ABA in water-related stresses and the enrichment of GO terms linked to responses to water and nutrients, we speculated that larger reductions in water potential would further increase the proportion of ERS+ cells. To that end, we used a hard-agar model of drought to explore this idea. In this model, reduced water content induces a drought response that more closely resembles soil drying than conventional osmotic treatments38.

Seedlings were grown on half MS containing 100%, 50% or 20% water (two replicates each) and profiled by scRNA-seq (Fig. 5d). Cells were assigned an identity on the basis of our reference atlas. Fewer cells were recovered from the 20% water condition (Supplementary Table 2), together with an enrichment of undifferentiated cells and an underrepresentation of larger differentiated cells (Extended Data Fig. 12c). This result is consistent with their increased fragility under severe water deficit.

We then statistically assessed changes in the proportion of ERS+ cells between the conditions, taking into account cells that can either be in ERS+ or ERS– state, using water concentration as a continuous covariate (Fig. 5e). The proportion of ERS+ cells increased substantially when the water potential was reduced, from 0.38 in 100% water to 0.66 in 20% water. This result demonstrates that water deficit increases the prevalence of ERS+ cells (Fig. 5e). This change was significant both across the whole-root level and in individual cell types (Extended Data Fig. 12d), and was unrelated to differences in cell filtering (here and across the study) (Extended Data Fig. 12h). We observed that cells grown under water-deficit conditions contained more detected transcripts than those grown under control conditions (Extended Data Fig. 12e), which suggests that the increased prevalence of ERS+ cells might reflect higher transcriptional output. However, ERS-specific module scores remained elevated in water-deficit cells with comparable transcript abundance, and this difference remained significant after adjusting for log-transformed UMI counts (Extended Data Fig. 12f). Likewise, logistic regression showed that the increased odds of a cell being classified as ERS+ remained significant after accounting for transcript abundance (Extended Data Fig. 12g), which indicated that the increased prevalence of ERS+ cells cannot be fully explained by higher transcriptional output alone. Whether this result reflects changes in the probability of cells transitioning between ERS+ and ERS− states, or more persistent occupancy of the ERS+ state, cannot be determined from the current data.

To further validate this finding, we examined the expression of two ERS-specific markers that we had already profiled (Fig. 3a)—AT3G04010 and AT4G26080 (which are ABA-responsive)29,30—in vivo by whole-mount HCR in intact root tips under both the control (100% water) and water-deficit (20% water) conditions (Fig. 5f). We observed a marked increase in the number of cells expressing both these genes under the water-deficit condition (Fig. 5f). Quantifications revealed an approximate 1.6-fold increase in overall signal intensity and a 2.2–2.5-fold increase in areas showing a positive signal, independent of differences in root length (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 12i). Consistent with these measurements, empirical cumulative distribution functions showed a global shift of per-cell fluorescence towards higher values under water-deficit conditions for both gene markers (Fig. 5h). Together, these HCR analyses provide independent in planta evidence that more cells express ERS markers under water-deficit conditions, consistent with the increased prevalence of ERS+ cells detected by scRNA-seq.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that environmental conditions modulate the prevalence of the ERS, thereby establishing this transcriptional state as a dynamic layer of root cell identity that may contribute to adaptation to water-deficit conditions.

Through deep characterization of root cell transcriptional states coupled with HCR spatial mapping, we identified an alternative trajectory for all cell types above the root meristematic region. This trajectory, which we term the ERS, corresponds to a strong and specific transcriptional signature superimposed on a subset of cells across multiple lineages simultaneously. Cell identity in the root tip can therefore be defined by three factors: (1) the lineage that defines the cell type, which remains stable during development; (2) the developmental stage; and (3) the presence or absence of this additional transcriptional state, the prevalence of which is modulated by environmental conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2). Although detected in parallel with developmental trajectories, the ERS seems to be independent of developmental origin, as it was detected across all cell types throughout developmental progression. Instead, it represents an additional layer of identity strongly associated with ABA signalling. Our analyses also suggest that the circadian clock may influence ERS formation. This aspect will be interesting to explore further, particularly given its known feedback with ABA signalling and stress responses39. We note that although an ERS can be detected across all lineages, it is not entirely equivalent in all cell types. Some ERS-specific genes were enriched in all ERS+ cells, whereas others were restricted to specific lineages, which suggests that there is a degree of cell-type specificity in the occurrence of this transcriptional state, which may be shaped by the underlying regulatory networks of each cell type. Differences in ABA sensitivity or signalling capacity among cells within and between cell types could equally contribute to this variability and represent an important avenue for future investigation.

A notable feature of this discovery is the scale of transcriptional heterogeneity observed in cell types, which was largely undetectable using bulk methods before single-cell technologies. Recent imaging approaches have revealed transcriptional heterogeneity in plant tissues40,41,42,43,44. However, to our knowledge, none has reported the clear and consistent dichotomy observed here. The robust presence of two populations across independent datasets and detection methods, together with consistent detection of the same ERS-specific co-expressed gene modules, strongly supports the interpretation that the ERS reflects a regulated transcriptional program. Nonetheless, important questions remain regarding the mechanisms that establish and maintain this state. Exploring the temporal dynamics of the ERS will be essential, including how rapidly it is induced and how stable it remains under constant or changing environments. Moreover, it will be important to determine whether the proportions of ERS+ and ERS– cells detected by scRNA-seq reflect stochastic transitions between states, the probabilities of which are modulated by environmental conditions. Live imaging of ERS marker genes will be an important avenue to address these questions. Equally important will be determining the signals that govern entry into and exit from the ERS, including whether ABA directly influences the probability that cells adopt this state or whether it permissively sets the proportion of cells competent to do so.

Another open question is the spatial arrangement and coordination of ERS+ cells. We showed that the ERS can occur in all root layers, independent of cell type, but the signal determining which cells enter this state is unclear. The increase in ERS prevalence under water-deficit conditions suggests that this state is part of a physiological response to the environment. If this response is linked to water and nutrient availability, it would make sense that it spans from the epidermis, which senses and absorbs these resources, to the vasculature, which distributes them throughout the plant. As such, ERS formation may contribute to, or result from, a coordinated response across the tissue. The persistence of the ERS across environmental and genetic perturbations suggests that it represents an important physiological state. Correlating ERS occurrence with known pathways or functional markers will be crucial to clarify its role.

The marked increase in ERS prevalence under water-deficit conditions demonstrates that this transcriptional state is dynamically regulated by environmental conditions. Determining which environmental cues regulate ERS prevalence, how these signals are integrated and how the ERS influences cellular physiology will be important next steps. More broadly, our findings establish the ERS as a previously unrecognized layer of root cell identity. We anticipate that the identification of the ERS will facilitate the discovery and investigation of additional environmentally regulated transcriptional states across plant tissues and species. As such, it provides a framework for understanding how environmental regulation shapes cell identity across plant tissues and how plants dynamically remodel cellular states in response to their environment, which will ultimately advance our understanding of plant physiology.

Recent versions of CellRanger identify substantially more cells than earlier releases. This enables the detection of low-UMI root cap cells (which were discarded in earlier releases), but also results in the identification of more cells than expected based on cell loading in the assay. Many of these seem to be low-UMI, low-quality cells, which are probably derived from damaged protoplasts that nonetheless pass the CellRanger threshold. To identify and remove such cells, we developed a filtering approach based on clustering CellRanger-called ‘cell’ barcodes together with barcodes classified as background. The method was applied independently to each library and manually adjusted, which enabled the proportion of filtered cells to vary according to the intrinsic quality of each preparation. The procedure (illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1) is described below.

A Seurat (v.5.0.0)47 object was created from the N barcodes identified as cells by CellRanger, excluding genes expressed in fewer than three cells. Data were log-normalized and scaled. Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using FindVariableFeatures (nfeatures=15,000). Genes affected by protoplast isolation (see the section ‘Identification of genes affected by protoplast isolation’), as well as mitochondrial and chloroplastic genes, were excluded from the HVG set. The first 40 principal components were used for UMAP dimensionality reduction and clustering. Major root cell types (root cap, epidermis, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, phloem, xylem and procambium) were annotated.

The following steps were applied to each cell type individually:

A new Seurat object was created containing the ‘cell type’ cells and the first top N barcodes (ranked by UMI content) that were classified as background by CellRanger. The object was processed as above, with the exception that FindVariableFeatures was run with the parameter nfeatures=5000 and that the first 20 principal components were used for UMAP and cell clustering.

The object was subsetted to retain only the cluster (or clusters) containing the ‘cell type’ cells, which typically also includes some background barcodes. This subsetted object was then re-processed as in the previous step.

In this subset, background barcodes typically form distinct clusters, often together with low-quality cells of the given type. All cells clustering with background were considered more similar to background than to high-quality cells and were reclassified as background and discarded.

Cells retained after filtering across all types were merged into a new Seurat object.

Using this approach, between 8 and 30% of CellRanger-identified cells were typically removed. Preparations processed using 10x Genomics NextGem chemistry tended to generate a higher proportion of discarded cells.

Doublets were identified using scDblFinder48,49 following the recommendations of the Single-cell Best Practices guide (www.sc-best-practices.org). The analysis was performed on the Seurat object obtained after cell filtering, with the parameters artificialDoublets=25000, dims=30, dbr.sd=1 and propRandom=1. All cells identified as doublets were subsequently removed from the dataset.

The few cells that did not cluster with either population were discarded.

Samples were then dehydrated in successive 10 min washes at room temperature: once in 70% ethanol, once in 90% ethanol and twice in 100% ethanol. Samples were washed twice in 100% methanol and left in the second wash at −20 °C overnight (or up to a few weeks).

Roots were rehydrated in a series of 5 min washes at room temperature in 75%, 50%, 25% methanol and in DPBS-T (0.1% Tween 20 in DPBS) and incubated in 1× cell wall digestion solution (10× stock: 250 mg macerozyme, 250 mg cellulase, 125 mg pectolyase and 375 mg pectinase in 50 ml ultrapure water) in DPBS-T for 5 min. After a wash in DPBS-T, samples were fixed in 10% (v/v) formaldehyde (37% solution, Sigma-Aldrich) in DPBS-T for 15 min at room temperature and washed twice in DPBS-T. Roots were incubated in 1 ml proteinase K buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8) and 50 mM EDTA (pH 8) in ultrapure water) with 8 μl proteinase K (10 mg ml–1) for 15 min at 37 °C. After two washes in DPBS-T, samples were fixed in 10% (v/v) formaldehyde (37% solution, Sigma-Aldrich) in DPBS-T for 15 min at room temperature and washed twice in DPBS-T.

We followed the third-generation in situ HCR procedure developed by Molecular Instruments, and as previously described10, but the dextran sulfate used in the amplification buffer had to be replaced by a low-molecular-weight dextran sulfate to preserve the root structure. Roots were pre-hybridized in the 30% probe hybridization buffer for 30 min at 37 °C, then placed in the probe solution (2–3 μl of probe mixture in 500 μl of 30% probe hybridization buffer) and incubated at 37 °C overnight.

Samples were washed twice in the 30% probe wash buffer for 30 min at 37 °C, then washed twice in 5× SSCT (5× SSC and 0.1% Tween 20 in ultrapure water) for 10 min at room temperature. Samples were placed in the amplification buffer for 30 min at room temperature. In the meantime, fluorescently labelled hairpins were prepared by snap-cooling 10 μl of 3 μM stock in hairpin storage buffer (heat at 95 °C for 90 s in a thermocycler and cool to room temperature in the dark for 30 min). We predominantly used HCR amplifiers coupled to Alexa 647 fluorophores in this study, with Alexa 488-coupled amplifiers used for experiments targeting two genes simultaneously. The hairpin solution was prepared by adding the snap-cooled hairpins to 500 μl amplification buffer. Samples were placed in the hairpin solution and incubated overnight at room temperature in the dark. They were subsequently washed 3 times in 5× SSCT for 20 min at room temperature.

Samples were incubated in ClearSee11 in the dark for 3 days.

Cell walls were stained by incubating samples in 1 ml ClearSee and 2 μl calcofluor solution (50 mg ml–1 in DMSO) for 2 h in the dark. Samples were washed in ClearSee for 1 h, in the dark, and mounted in ClearSee in a chamber made with a square coverslip and double-sided tape. The chamber was sealed using top coat nail polish, and samples were imaged on the same day.

Mounted roots were imaged using a Nikon Ti-E inverted motorized microscope with a Nikon A1Si spectral detector confocal system or a Nikon A1RMP confocal microscope, controlled by NIS-Elements software, and a ×60 water-immersion objective. Calcofluor white and the Alex 488- and the Alexa 647-coupled amplifiers were excited at 405 nm, 488 nm and 640 nm, respectively. Seven to eight images were taken per root along the differentiation axis. Images were processed using Fiji57. Images of the same roots were stitched using the Grid/Collection stitching plugin58, and the brightness and contrast were adjusted for each image based on the signal detected. Optical sections were generated using the Orthogonal Views method in Fiji.

All datasets other than the Col-0 atlas were annotated using the Col-0 atlas as the reference. Anchors between the reference atlas and the other datasets were identified using FindTransferAnchors in Seurat (v.5), specifying the Col-0 RPCA-integrated data as the reference assay. TransferData in Seurat (v.5) was then used to calculate, for each cell, a prediction score for every identity, based on the identified anchors and the atlas annotations. Each cell was assigned the identity with the highest prediction score.

We used the CellCycleScoring function in Seurat to assign a cell cycle score to each cell of our reference atlas based on the expression of S phase marker genes (E2Fc (also known as AT1G47870), E2Fb (AT5G22220), DPa (also known as AT5G02470), CDKG;2 (also known as AT1G67580), E2Fa (also known as AT2G36010), DPb (also known as AT5G03415), CYCA3;1 (also known as AT5G43080), CYCA3;2 (also known as AT1G47210), KRP5 (also known as AT3G24810), KRP2 (also known as AT3G50630), KRP6 (also known as AT3G19150), KRP3 (also known as AT5G48820), WEE (also known as AT1G02970), KRP4 (also known as AT2G32710), CYCD4;2 (also known as AT5G10440)) and of G2/M phase marker genes (CYCB1;1 (also known as AT4G37490), CYCB2;1 (also known as AT2G17620), CDKB2;2 (also known as AT1G20930), CYCB3;1 (also known as AT1G16330), CYCA2;2 (also known as AT5G11300), CYCA2;4 (also known as AT1G80370), CYCA1;1 (also known as AT1G44110), CYCA2;1 (also known as AT5G25380), CDKD;1 (also known as AT1G73690), CDKB2;1 (also known as AT1G76540), CKS2 (also known as AT2G27970), CYCB1;2 (also known as AT5G06150), CYCD3;1 (also known as AT4G34160), CCS52B (also known as AT5G13840), CYCA2;3 (also known as AT1G15570), CCS52A2 (also known as AT4G11920), CYCB2;3 (also known as AT1G20610) and CYCB1;3 (also known as AT3G11520)) obtained from a previous study59. This score was used to provide a predicted classification of the cell cycle phase and to regress out cell cycle effects using vars.to.regress = c(“S.Score”, “G2M.Score”) in the ScaleData function. After regression, the top 50 principal components were calculated and the replicates were integrated again. UMAP dimensionality reduction was then performed, and cell identities were visualized in the embedding to confirm that both branches remained present.

The Col-0 atlas was subsetted into nine lineage-specific objects: epidermis, root cap, cortex, endodermis, PPP, XPP, procambium, phloem and xylem. Each lineage dataset was then processed independently.

Subclusters in each lineage were identified using FindNeighbors and FindClusters in Seurat (v.5), with the values of dims, k.param and resolution adapted to each lineage.

For each lineage, a ‘root cell’ corresponding to t = 0 in the trajectory was defined, as required by the diffusion pseudotime analysis underlying the PAGA trajectory inference. In the case of the epidermis and root cap lineages, developmental progression is known to originate at their junction. Therefore, for the epidermal lineage, the root cell was defined as the cell with the lowest coordinate along the UMAP y axis. For the root cap lineage, the root cell was defined as the cell with the highest coordinate along the UMAP y axis. For all other lineages, for which such anatomical landmarks are less obvious, the root cell was defined as the cell with the highest meristematic score (see below).

To perform trajectory inference, we used the SCP package60, which implements PAGA61,62,63 analysis directly in R, thereby avoiding conversion of Seurat objects to AnnData format to run scanpy63. Seurat v.5 assays were first converted to v.3 assays to enable SCP. Trajectories were inferred with the RunPAGA function, grouping cells by the subclusters defined above, with the following parameters: inear_reduction = “integrated.rpca”, nonlinear_reduction = “umap.rpca”, infer_pseudotime=TRUE, n_pcs and n_neighbours matching the values used for subclustering, and root_cell containing the barcode of the designated root cell. The trajectory topology was visualized with the PAGAPlot function, using reduction = “umap.rpca” and an edge_threshold adapted to each lineage. The pseudotime value assigned to each cell was then transferred to the metadata of the original Seurat (v.5) object.

The diffusion pseudotime analysis required the identification of a cell that would serve as t = 0; that is, the starting point of each trajectory. To do so, we developed an approach for identifying the ‘most meristematic’ cell in each lineage.

The epidermis is the only lineage for which the meristematic region can be unambiguously located based on both the topology of its clusters and the known biology of the tissue. It lies at the junction with the root cap, before differentiation into the two epidermal subtypes: hair cells and non-hair cells. This cluster was therefore designated as epidermis–meristem. To identify marker genes for this region, we ran the FindMarkers function on the subsetted Seurat object containing only epidermal clusters, with ident.1 = 5 (corresponding to the ‘epidermis–meristem’ cluster), only.pos=TRUE, and test.use = ‘t’. The resulting gene list was then used with the AddModuleScore function in Seurat (v.5) to assign a meristematic score to every cell in the atlas.

We reasoned that at least a subset of the genes enriched in the epidermal meristem would also be enriched in meristematic regions of other lineages. Thus, the cell with the highest meristematic score in each lineage was taken to represent the most likely earliest developmental state and used as the root cell (t = 0) for pseudotime analysis.

Monocle 3 (v.1.3.7)64,65,66 was used as an alternative method to confirm the developmental trajectories obtained using PAGA. Our Col-0 atlas was converted to a cds object using SeuratWrappers (v.0.4.0) (https://github.com/satijalab/seurat-wrappers). Cells were clustered and partitioned with cluster_cells using resolution=0.0002, and trajectories were computed using learn_graph with learn_graph_control=list(minimal_branch_len=20)). As for the PAGA trajectories, root cells were defined as those with the highest meristematic score per lineage, and these were used in order_cells to assign a pseudotime to each cell in the trajectory. All plots were generated with plot_cells. Concordance between (scaled) Monocle and diffusion pseudotime assignments was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficients computed independently for each lineage.

RNA velocity analysis was performed using the dynamical model implemented in the R package velociraptor67 (v.1.10.0) (wrapping scVelo68) on a per-lineage basis. Spliced and unspliced count matrices were generated independently for each library from velocyto69 (v.0.17.17) loom files and merged after restricting to genes detected across all samples. Because the intronic signal in Arabidopsis scRNA-seq datasets is limited, only genes with sufficient spliced and unspliced coverage were retained for velocity inference (minimum 50 cells with unspliced counts and 300 total unspliced counts; minimum 50 cells with spliced counts and 1,000 total spliced counts globally across the dataset). The top 4,000 variable genes were identified independently for each lineage, and protoplasting-sensitive genes, mitochondrial genes and chloroplast genes were excluded before analysis. The final gene set used for velocity inference corresponded to the intersection between lineage variable features and genes passing global spliced or unspliced filtering criteria. For each lineage, a SingleCellExperiment object containing spliced, unspliced and total RNA counts was generated and analysed using the scVelo dynamical model with the RPCA integrated embedding used as the neighbourhood graph for kinetic inference. Root cells for latent time estimation were defined independently for each lineage as the top 1% of cells with the highest meristematic score. Given the relatively low proportion of unspliced reads in our dataset (about 3% median unspliced fraction), RNA velocity was used primarily as an orthogonal validation of trajectory structure rather than as the primary trajectory inference approach. Concordance between scVelo latent time and diffusion pseudotime was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficients computed independently for each lineage.

To test whether the apparent absence of ERS-associated genes in ERS− cells could be explained by reduced transcript capture, we compared the expression behaviour of genes from ERS+-specific modules (modules 20 and 21) and modules expressed in both branches (modules 13 and 14) in the XPP lineage, the largest lineage exhibiting the ERS dichotomy. Average log-normalized expression was calculated independently in ERS+ and ERS− cells for each gene. To compare genes across a shared expression range, overlapping expression intervals between ERS+-specific and shared-module genes were defined using the 10–90th percentile range of average ERS+ expression for each group. A linear model describing the relationship between ERS+ and ERS− expression was then fitted using genes from modules expressed in both branches. Residual ERS− expression values (observed minus expected expression) were subsequently calculated for all genes relative to this model. Residual distributions were compared between ERS+-specific genes and genes expressed in both branches in the overlapping expression interval using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. This analysis tested whether ERS+-specific genes exhibited systematically lower ERS− expression than expected based solely on their expression level in ERS+ cells.

Because the number of transcripts detected in branch 1 was systematically higher than in branch 2, we tested whether the observed branching events could result from coverage artefacts. To control for this factor, UMI counts were selectively downsampled in ERS+ cells to match the UMI distribution of ERS− cells in each cell type. Specifically, for each ERS+ cell, a target UMI count was assigned based on quantile matching to the corresponding ERS− cell-type distribution, and counts were downsampled using the downsampleMatrix function from DropletUtils70 (v1.20.0). ERS− cells were left unchanged. Three independent downsampled count matrices were generated using different random seeds. Each downsampled matrix was used to generate a new Seurat object, which was processed independently using the same workflow as the reference atlas, including log-normalization, variable feature selection, scaling, PCA and UMAP embedding. Protoplasting-sensitive genes, mitochondrial genes and chloroplast genes were excluded from the variable feature set before dimensionality reduction. A meristematic score was then calculated for each cell using the same gene set as in the reference atlas.

We used hdWGCNA (v0.4.1)71,72,73 to identify modules of co-expressed genes across our Col-0 reference atlas. To determine the list of genes that would be used in the analysis, we first applied the SetupForWGCNA function to obtain all genes expressed in at least 10% of cells in at least one lineage. This list was intersected with the VariableFeatures of the atlas, which contains all genes detected in every replicate, excluding those affected by protoplast isolation as well as mitochondrial and chloroplastic genes. The resulting set of 12,923 genes was supplied to SetupForWGCNA with the parameter gene_select = “custom”.

To reduce data sparsity before WGCNA, we generated metacells (aggregates of small groups of similar cells per biological sample) and their associated expression matrices, which served as inputs for the network analysis. Metacells were generated using the MetacellsByGroups function using group.by=c(“lineage”,“orig.ident”), reduction = “integrated.rpca”, ident.group = “lineage”, k = 20 and max_shared=5, followed by normalization with NormalizeMetacells.

We then set up the expression dataset for consensus network analysis across all replicates using SetMultiExpr with multi.group.by = “orig.ident”. After determining the soft power threshold for each batch with the TestSoftPowersConsensus function, the consensus co-expression network was constructed using ConstructNetworkusing minModuleSize=30, consensus=TRUE and deepSplit=2.

Module scores were computed using ModuleExprScore using n_genes = “all” and method = “Seurat”, and projected onto the atlas UMAP with ModuleFeaturePlot using features = “scores”.

GO enrichment analysis was performed on the gene sets from modules 20 and 21 using ShinyGO (v.0.82; https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/). The analysis was run with the GO Biological Process pathway database, an FDR cut-off value of 0.01 and the number of pathways to display set to 50. Pathways were ranked by fold enrichment, and the results were visualized as a hierarchical clustering tree.

Putative TF target genes were inferred from the DAP-seq dataset17. The ‘Motifs in peaks (P ≤ 1 × 10–4)’ BED file (http://neomorph.salk.edu/dap_web/pages/browse_table_aj.php) was used as a DNA-binding database for the profiled TFs of the DAP-seq dataset.

The 500 bp promoter coordinates for Arabidopsis genes were extracted using the promoters function in GenomicRanges74, genes(TxDb.Athaliana.BioMart.plantsmart28) as an annotation for Arabidopsis genes and the arguments upstream = 500, downstream = 50. For each TF, the binding coordinates were extracted from the Motif in Peaks.bed file and intersected with the promoter coordinates using the subsetByOverlaps function in GenomicRanges. Every gene that had a binding motif for the TF in the 500 bp region upstream of its transcription start site (TSS) was considered a putative target gene. For TFs that were not profiled in the DAP-seq studies but, according to the cisBP2 study18, have a strong similarity in their DNA-binding domains with another TF that was studied by DAP-seq, their target genes were considered to be the same as the TF profiled by DAP-seq.

We then converted the TF target predictions into data to be processed with hdWGCNA. A sparse matrix was generated whereby rows are all genes in the dataset, columns are TFs with putative targets and values are set to 1 if the gene was a target of the TF and 0 otherwise. This was input into the Seurat object using the SetMotifMatrix function of hdWGCNA (v.0.4.1). The list of TFs and their target genes was set with SetMotifTargets, and a dataframe containing the target numbers was input with SetMotifs. Because we were interested in what drives the expression of co-expression modules in the dataset, we restricted the analysis to all genes that were assigned to a co-expression module as well as all TFs with target information. A network of TFs and target genes was built using ConstructTFNetwork, in which the XGBoost regression was used to evaluate how important each TF is for predicting each gene based on single-cell data72,75. The network was then pruned to keep only the strongest TF–gene connections. Specifically, we selected the top ten TFs above a regulatory score threshold as regulators for each gene, using the AssignTFRegulons function with strategy = “A”, reg_thresh=0.01 and n_tfs=10. A positive regulon score was then computed for each cell using RegulonScores, and the scaled average regulon score per cell identity was plotted using pheatmap76.

Three published Arabidopsis root single-cell datasets were used to verify that the ERS and associated ERS-specific genes described in this study are also present in other datasets7,8,16. For ref. 8, we directly used the processed Seurat object provided by the authors. For ref. 7, we reprocessed the raw fastq files using the same pre-processing steps as described for our Col-0 atlas. For ref. 16, we used the count matrices provided by the authors and performed batch integration.

Once the final Seurat objects were generated, cells were annotated using our reference atlas following the same procedure described in the section ‘Cell annotation’ above. Cells were also projected onto the atlas UMAP embedding using the MapQuery function in Seurat (v.5.0.0). Co-expression gene modules were projected onto these datasets using the ProjectModules function in hdWGCNA (v.0.4.1), and module scores were calculated with ModuleExprScore using n_genes = “all” and method = “Seurat”.

To assess the robustness of ERS assignments in external datasets, label transfer was repeated across a range of Seurat parameter settings using the integrated Col-0 atlas as reference. Specifically, projections were performed using 30, 40 or 50 principal components and k.anchor values of 5, 10 or 20, which generated nine parameter combinations in total. For each parameter set, transfer anchors were identified using FindTransferAnchors with the RPCA integrated embedding, and cell identities were assigned using TransferData. To focus analyses on cells robustly assigned to the ERS bifurcation, only cells classified as ERS+ or ERS− in the baseline parameter set (50 principal components, k.anchor = 5) were retained for downstream comparisons. Predicted identities were separated into cell-type and ERS-state components, and stability was assessed independently for both. Cell-type stability was quantified as the fraction of parameter sets in which each cell retained its baseline cell-type assignment, whereas ERS stability was calculated as the consistency of ERS+ and ERS− assignment in the baseline cell type across parameter sets. Global agreement between parameter sets was further quantified using pairwise adjusted rand index (ARI) comparisons across all transferred labels. Furthermore, the proportion of ERS+ cells in each cell type, the fraction of cells retaining their baseline cell type assignment and the frequency of ERS state switching across parameter sets were calculated independently for each external dataset.

The fragment files generated with Cell Ranger ATAC (v.1.2) and mapped to TAIR10/Araport11 reference were obtained from a previous study16, imported to snapatac2 (v.2.6.4) and paired-insertions calculated77 in 500 bp bins78. The cells were initially defined as barcodes associated with more than 3,500 and fewer than 50,000 fragments per barcode and with a TSS enrichment score higher than 2.5. Low-quality cells and outliers were also identified per-sample basis using the 3× median absolute deviation threshold and further removed from analysis79. Following peak calling using macs3 (ref. 80), peak and gene activity matrices were calculated using the paired-insertion counting strategy. For ATAC–seq data integration into our scRNA-seq dataset, the peak matrices were imported to the Signac package81 and the dimensions of ATAC–seq were reduced using latent semantic indexing. ATAC–seq nuclei were assigned identities using our reference atlas and the FindTransferAnchors function with the parameters reduction = ‘cca’ and dims = 1:30, followed by TransferData with weight.reduction = obj[[“lsi”]] and dims = 2:30. The TransferData function and previously computed anchors were also used to generate an ‘imputed’ scRNA-seq matrix for the ATAC nuclei. Using this imputed matrix, the snATAC–seq and scRNA-seq reference atlases were merged into a single object, processed with ScaleData and RunPCA, and the first 30 principal components were used to produce a UMAP co-embedding of the two datasets.

To perform differential expression analysis of our co-expression modules, a Seurat object was created with all conditions to compare, and the data were scaled using ScaleData (Seurat v.5.0.0). The modules were projected into the new dataset using ProjectModules (hdWGCNA v.0.4.1). A module score was then calculated using ModuleExprScore. The differential module scores were determined using the FindDMEs function with features = “ModuleScores” and test.use = “wilcox”. The results were plotted using PlotDMEsLollipop.

All changes in cell composition were tested with sccomp (v.1.99.18) in R82. Specifically, when evaluating changes in ERS+ (and consequently ERS−) proportions, the Seurat object was subsetted to retain only cells that had reached the developmental stage at which they could be assigned to either state to avoid taking into account sampling effects that might affect the total number of mature cells captured. This process excluded the root cap, ‘early’ identities and the non-hair cells, for which the trajectories could determine the point of branching. The test was then run on the subsetted data using sccomp_estimate and sccomp_test, and results were plotted with sccomp_boxplot or with plot in the case of the water-deficit experiment for which the covariate is continuous.

Seedlings were grown vertically on full (1×) or half (0.5×) MS plates, as for scRNA-seq experiments. Pictures of the roots were taken at 6 d.a.g., using a stereomicroscope (n = 16 for full MS, n = 23 for half MS, from two different plates each). A portion corresponding to approximately 2.5 mm from the tip was selected for each image of the root, and root hairs were manually counted using the Cell Counter plugin in Fiji. A significant difference in root hair density between the two conditions was determined using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test (wilcox.test function) in R.

Note that the seedlings used for this quantification were in the top row of the plate (seeds were sown in two rows for scRNA-seq experiments). Roots of seedlings grown in the bottom row tended to have fewer root hairs (in both conditions).

A new Seurat object was created using the Col-0 cells that were grown on full MS (which is all replicates except the three half MS replicates), as well as the cells of the C24, Cvi-0, Ler and Ws-2 atlases. The expression data were then pseudobulked per genotype, cell identity and batch using the AggregateExpression in Seurat (v.5.0.0). For each possible inter-accession replicate pair, a differential expression test was performed on the pseudobulk data using FindMarkers with DESeq2. Genes were considered differentially expressed if the adjusted P < 0.05 and the absolute log2 fold-change was >0.5. For a gene to be called an accession-specific DEG, it had to be systematically differentially expressed in a given accession compared with the other four, but not be differentially expressed in any pairwise comparison between the remaining four accessions. The proportion of ecotype-specific DEGs per identity or per module were plotted using pheatmap.

Module scores for hdWGCNA modules 20 and 21 were calculated using AddModuleScore in Seurat (v.5.0.0). Analyses were restricted to cells classified as ERS+ or ERS− from the 100% and 20% water conditions. Total UMI counts were log10-transformed after addition of a pseudocount. Module scores were analysed using linear mixed-effects models implemented in the lme4 (v.1.1.36) and lmerTest (v.3.2.1) R packages, with water condition as a fixed effect, log10 UMI count modelled using a natural spline with four degrees of freedom (splines v.4.3.1 package) and biological replicate included as a random intercept. For visualization, cells were divided into ten equal-frequency bins according to log10 UMI count, and mean module scores ± s.e.m. were calculated in each bin. Binning was used for visualization only; all statistical inference was performed on the unbinned cell-level data. The association between water condition and ERS identity was assessed using binomial generalized linear mixed-effects models (glmer; lme4) with biological replicate included as a random intercept. An unadjusted model included water condition alone, whereas the adjusted model also included log10 UMI count as a fixed-effect covariate. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for the effect of water condition were obtained using broom.mixed (v.0.2.9.6).

To compare roots grown under different conditions using whole-mount HCR, all roots were grown, processed and imaged simultaneously using identical confocal settings.

First, a mask defining the cytoplasmic area of the root was created in Fiji. A threshold-based mask of the whole root was generated from the calcofluor channel using Image > Adjust > Threshold, followed by Process > Binary > Close and Process > Binary > Fill Holes. A second threshold-based mask was created from the calcofluor channel to define cell walls. Subtracting the cell wall mask from the whole-root mask (Process > Math > Subtract) produced a mask containing only the cytoplasmic regions. This mask was then multiplied by a mask selecting the region from the elongation zone upwards, corresponding to the area where ERS expression begins in single-cell data.

The resulting cytoplasmic mask was multiplied by the channel containing the HCR signal to retain only signal in cells from the elongation zone upwards. A separate threshold-based mask was created from the HCR signal channel to identify areas above a defined signal threshold. Multiplying this mask by the cytoplasmic mask ensured that only signal inside cells from the elongation zone upwards was considered.

Custom Fiji macros were used to automate measurements across z-stacks. One macro measured the surface area covered by a mask in each slice, which enabled calculation of the total cytoplasmic area for normalization and the area of positive signal. Another macro measured the sum of all pixel intensities per slice, which was applied to the cytoplasmic signal image.

Differences in signal between conditions were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Single-cell segmentation was performed in 3D using the cell-wall staining channel. Individual cells were first manually seeded by placing markers in each cell lumen and storing these positions as regions of interest. A binary marker image was then generated from the seed positions. The cell wall channel was processed using the MorphoLibJ83 Morphological Filters (3D) function with the Gradient filter to enhance cell boundaries. The processed cell-wall image and binary marker image were subsequently used for marker-controlled watershed segmentation using MorphoLibJ. Segmentation outputs were manually curated to correct improper segmentations caused by discontinuities or holes in cell-wall staining, either by removing incorrectly segmented cells or merging oversegmented regions. Before fluorescence quantification, signal channels were background-corrected to minimize the contribution of imaging noise and diffuse background fluorescence. In brief, pixel intensities were measured in a region outside the root tissue, and the 99th percentile background intensity value was subtracted from all pixels in the image using the Subtract function in Fiji. The resulting filtered fluorescence images were combined with the curated segmentation masks to quantify volumetric single-cell mean fluorescence intensity using the Intensity Measurements 2D/3D function in MorphoLibJ. Measured fluorescence values were then mapped back onto segmented cells using the Assign Measure to Labels function in MorphoLibJ to generate colour-coded single-cell fluorescence representations.

For the water-deficit HCR experiments, segmentation was restricted to epidermal and cortical cells. Fluorescence quantification was performed on the original, non-background-subtracted images so that identical image processing was applied across control and water-deficit samples, which enabled direct comparison of fluorescence intensities between conditions. Mean fluorescence intensity was measured for each segmented cell. To account for batch-to-batch differences in HCR signal intensity, the mean fluorescence intensity for each segmented cell was normalized to the mean per-cell fluorescence of the corresponding 100% water control sample processed in parallel. Empirical cumulative distribution functions were then generated to compare the distributions of normalized single-cell fluorescence intensities between conditions.

To test whether mutually exclusive marker expression defined two separable cell populations, Gaussian mixture modelling was applied independently to fluorescence quantification data and scRNA-seq expression data using the R package mclust84 (v.6.0.0). For fluorescence imaging data, mean single-cell fluorescence intensities for AT3G01720 and AT5G56550 were extracted from segmented cells following background correction and volumetric quantification. A marker ratio was calculated for each cell as AT3G01720/(AT3G01720 + AT5G56550), and Gaussian mixture modelling was performed on the resulting distribution using Mclust. Cells were assigned to the inferred populations based on maximum posterior probability. The fitted Gaussian components and their relative proportions were visualized over the observed ratio distribution. For scRNA-seq data, normalized expression values for AT3G01720 and AT5G56550 were extracted from hair cells in the reference atlas. Cells with zero combined expression for both markers were excluded from the analysis. A marker score was then calculated as the difference between AT3G01720 and AT5G56550 expression values, and Gaussian mixture modelling was applied to the score distribution using Mclust. Fitted Gaussian distributions were visualized together with the observed score distribution to assess the presence of two transcriptionally distinct populations.

#3331. ‘The audience gets to be in on it’: How narrative-led gameplay is Call of Duty’s greatest weapon

As video games chase spectacle, Modern Warfare 4 makes a case for putting character and consequence at the heart of its biggest battles

Don’t get Pushing Buttons delivered to your inbox? Sign up here

In these strange, subdued weeks before the much-anticipated release of Grand Theft Auto VI, there have still been some welcome surprises. Onimusha: Way of the Sword revived a half-forgotten samurai action series with much aplomb, while Silent Hill: Townfall transported all of the franchise’s bloody, dread-filled psychological horror to … the picturesque Scottish coast. Even more shocking to games writers, though, is the fact that the forthcoming Modern Warfare 4 has one of the best Call of Duty campaign modes for at least six years – and might even be up there with the original Modern Warfare trilogy.

The setting is an invasion of South Korea by its northern neighbour – a setup that could have fuelled a hundred instantly forgettable Netflix films, but has proved controversial in a video game. The authoritarian regime has a new (fictitious) ruler and a powerful experimental weapon, which it’s not afraid to use as the opening gambit in an explosive attack. You take on the role of Park Hyun Jun, a conscripted South Korean soldier who, along with his small unit of similarly inexperienced grunts, gets caught in the firestorm as cities burn and enemy paratroopers fill the skies.

On the other side of the globe, meanwhile, series hero Capt John Price has gone rogue, teaming up with cartel boss Valeria Garza to track down his nemesis, Makarov. How these narrative strands intertwine is the propulsive engine of the story, and along the way this game throws an arsenal of gigantic set-piece battles at you, edging you closer to a layered finale.

Most positive reviews of the campaign mode have mentioned how the story combines its own ideas with themes and plots from the previous titles. So how did the game’s lead developer, Infinity Ward, set about creating a story that could support a giant first-person juggernaut such as CoD? “The DNA of a Modern Warfare story is that it is something you believe could happen in the real world,” says narrative director Jeff Negus. “‘Does it feel grounded?’ is the first question. Additionally, we ask, ‘What is going to resonate with a modern audience?’ It was important to us not to tell the same story again, not to show the same characters being the same people, because what they’ve been through has deeply affected who they are.”

Infinity Ward brought in a range of military and cultural consultants to ensure the story’s accuracy. “Some of our actors were conscripted in Korea, so they’re able to identify with some of what’s happening,” says Negus. “We use a wide array of military consultants across many different branches and types of service. Our dialect coach, June Yoon, was invaluable because he understands the dialectical differences between North and South Korea, and he could help us reword lines in a more colloquially appropriate way.”

One key element of the game is the combination of quieter, character-led narrative moments and apocalyptic action set-pieces. “There is a very healthy symbiosis between both parts,” says Negus. “We start with strong narrative and gameplay, and the very first thing that we ask is, ‘How can we interweave these things in a way that feels intentional?’ One must be complementary of the other, in such a way that we’re able to say, ‘I can’t imagine telling the story without this mechanic.’” Negus compares it to an explosion in an action movie, saying: “It can be spectacular, but if you don’t know the people who are a part of it, it won’t be meaningful. Back when I was working at Naughty Dog, and now with the team here, we talk about player-protagonist parity, and how, when you feel what the character feels, you’re all a part of the same story – the audience gets to be in on it.”

Surprisingly, iteration is an important part of the process – even though this is a game filled with cinematic sequences that may have to be filmed and animated early in the development process. “We certainly map out the story visually,” says Negus. “Justin [Harris], our lead writer, will put in these story beats, much like a season of television or a movie would do, but we do it with levels. And sometimes we’ll need to move things around, particularly in a story where we can shift the timeline in certain ways. When someone comes up with something, it gets moved around, it gets debated and analysed, and it’s road-tested pretty hard before it becomes inked.”

To me, the Modern Warfare 4 campaign is a story of dysfunctional families – the actual North Korean ruling family, Park Hyun Jun’s ragtag squad and Price’s Task Force 141. The tensions between these groups gives the story its human underpinning. Disaster movies usually centre on one or two main characters so that we, the audience, get to experience the drama vicariously through them.

Infinity Ward has successfully brought that structural and emotional toolkit to the finale of the Modern Warfare series, and that’s at least partly why it works. Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 might not be a Pulitzer prize-winning story, but in these odd days before the arrival of GTA VI it certainly gives you something to think about – and people to root for – as you blast away with your rifle, grenade launcher or helicopter gunship.

If you like the look of extreme off-road driving sims such as MudRunner, but are put off by their unyielding difficulty level, Over the Hill is definitely for you. It’s a cosy exploration game where you drive a 4x4 through a bright low-poly landscape of babbling brooks and lush woodlands, occasionally using a winch to get out of muddy puddles or up steep hills.

Along the way, you unlock cabins to allow fast travel and visit a mechanic to customise your vehicle. Spot an interesting bird or critter and you can photograph it, adding it to your scrapbook. The pace is slow and gentle, but there is a fulfilling challenge in mastering the winch and getting yourself out of tight spots.

I wish more games simply gave you a world to explore and the tools to do it and then left you alone – although, if the solitude gets to you, there’s a co-op multiplayer mode too, so you can disappear into the wilderness with pals. Bliss.

This week’s question from Kate via email is something I think all gamers have wondered:

#3333. Äthiopien und Eritrea: Kann Diplomatie den Krieg verhindern?

Nach neuen Kämpfen in Tigray wächst die Sorge vor einem Krieg zwischen Äthiopien und Eritrea. Kann Diplomatie einen neuen Krieg verhindern?

Äthiopiens Ministerpräsident Abiy Ahmed hat eine Übergangsregierung für die nördliche Region Tigray angekündigt. Sie hat sechs Monate Zeit, um Regionalwahlen vorzubereiten und die Stabilität wiederherzustellen.

Die Ankündigung vom vergangenen Freitag ist der vorläufige Schlusspunkt einer Militäroffensive, die die Volksbefreiungsfront von Tigray (TPLF) gemeinsam mit sechs weiteren bewaffneten Gruppen im September gestartet hatte. Die Angreifer wurden schließlich zum Rückzug gezwungen und verloren die Kontrolle über die Region.

"Damit die Menschen in Tigray endlich den Frieden erreichen, der ihnen so lange verwehrt geblieben ist, ihr Recht auf Selbstverwaltung wahrnehmen und endlich die Früchte der Entwicklung genießen können, die ihnen seit vielen Jahren vorenthalten wurden, müssen sie diesen Kreislauf der Gewalt durchbrechen", schrieb Abiy in einer auf X veröffentlichten Erklärung.

Shishay Amare, der neue Bürgermeister der Regionalhauptstadt Mekelle, sagte laut der französischen Nachrichtenagentur AFP, man werde "die Voraussetzungen für Wahlen schaffen, indem man in ganz Tigray Frieden und Stabilität gewährleiste".

Er sei zuversichtlich, dass dies wie geplant innerhalb von sechs Monaten gelingen werde, sagte Amare. Zu den vorrangigen Aufgaben gehörten die Wiederherstellung des Friedens, die Unterstützung der Vertriebenen bei ihrer Rückkehr, die Wiedereröffnung der Märkte und die Sicherstellung humanitärer Hilfe für Bedürftige.

Die jüngsten Kämpfe haben die Sorge vor einem Wiederaufflammen des verheerenden Kriegs in Tigray von 2020 bis 2022 verstärkt. Rund eine Million Menschen sind infolge des damaligen Konflikts noch immer vertrieben.

Die Afrikanische Union (AU) schätzt unter Berufung auf Wissenschaftler der Universität Gent, dass etwa 600.000 Menschen durch Kampfhandlungen, Hungersnot und den Zusammenbruch der Gesundheitsversorgung ums Leben kamen.

Die neue Regionalverwaltung steht vor ungelösten Konflikten um politische Macht und um Gebietsansprüche. Dazu gehört das Gebiet West-Tigray, das sowohl von Tigray als auch vom benachbarten Regionalstaat Amhara beansprucht wird.

Äthiopien und Eritrea waren einst erbitterte Feinde. Sie unterzeichneten jedoch 2018 ein Friedensabkommen.

Während des Krieges in Tigray kämpften eritreische Truppen später an der Seite der äthiopischen Bundesarmee. Seither haben sich die Beziehungen zwischen beiden Ländern allerdings wieder deutlich verschlechtert.

Addis Abeba wirft Asmara vor, die Streitkräfte in Tigray zu unterstützen und auf äthiopischem Territorium präsent zu sein. Eritrea weist die Vorwürfe zurück.

Berichte über Truppenbewegungen Eritreas nahe der Grenze haben die Besorgnis zusätzlich verstärkt. Wie umfangreich eine mögliche aktuelle Stationierung von Soldaten ist, bleibt jedoch umstritten.

Tsedale Lemma, Journalistin und Herausgeberin des renommierten Magazins Addis Standard, sagte Anfang des Monats gegenüber der DW, die Gefahr eines neuen Konflikts sei das Ergebnis jahrelang ungelöster Spannungen und der mangelnden Umsetzung des Pretoria-Abkommens vom November 2022, mit dem der Krieg in Tigray für beendet erklärt wurde.

Die internationale Gemeinschaft "unterschätze die Gefahr eines neuen Krieges völlig", fügte Lemma hinzu. Internationale Akteure hätten es versäumt, die Umsetzung des Abkommens wirksam zu überwachen und angemessen darauf zu reagieren, dass der Friedensprozess zunehmend an Dynamik verlor.

Die Konfrontation fällt in eine Zeit großer Instabilität in der gesamten Region. Dazu tragen unter anderem der Krieg im Sudan und die Spannungen zwischen Äthiopien und Ägypten um ein gewaltiges Staudammprojekt am Nil bei: der Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD).

Äthiopien nahm den GERD im September 2025 offiziell in Betrieb. Dieser Schritt stieß bei den nilabwärts liegenden Anrainerstaaten Ägypten und Sudan auf harsche Kritik.

Auch der Wettbewerb um den Zugang zum Roten Meer verschärft die strategische Konkurrenz in der Region. Äthiopien verlor 1993 mit der Unabhängigkeit Eritreas seinen direkten Zugang zum Meer.

Der britische Journalist Martin Plaut, der sich auf das Horn von Afrika spezialisiert hat, warnt, die Region stehe an einem Scheideweg. Er hat auch die Möglichkeit ins Spiel gebracht, dass äthiopische Streitkräfte in Eritrea einmarschieren könnten. Eine Invasion ist bislang jedoch lediglich ein mögliches Szenario und keine bestätigte Entscheidung der äthiopischen Regierung.

Ein größerer Konflikt könnte den regionalen Handel und die Sicherheit der Schifffahrt gefährden. Das Rote Meer und die Meerenge Bab al-Mandab verbinden die Seehandelsrouten zwischen Europa und Asien. Damit ist die Region weit über Afrika hinaus von strategischer Bedeutung.

#3337. What Do We Actually Know About the Microplastics Inside Us?

Microplastic particles. Peter Dazeley / Getty Images

Pervasive plastic contamination and unreliable methods have clouded the science on microplastics in the human body. In an interview, Australian scientist Cassandra Rauert, who built a plastics-free lab to study human exposure, explores the challenges for researchers.

Scientists have been documenting the presence of microplastics in our oceans and soils, in air, drinking water, and food for more than a decade. More recently, peer-reviewed studies have revealed the presence of microplastics in the human body. But how much plastic do we consume, where is it coming from, and what is it doing to us?

Cassandra Rauert, an environmental chemist at the University of Queensland, in Australia, conducts research to better understand the impacts of such exposures. Her work has shown that current techniques used for detecting plastic in humans are vulnerable to contamination from lab equipment. And last year, she published an attention-grabbing paper that found lipids present in blood can give false positives for the most commonly produced plastic, polyethylene. All of which suggests that some reported levels of microplastics in humans may be overestimated.

In an interview with Yale Environment 360, Rauert describes how, after learning about the potential for microplastics to infiltrate a lab — whether from construction materials, equipment, clothing, or the air — she and her fellow researchers painstakingly rebuilt their workspace, using glass and steel, to drastically reduce the odds of contamination. The hope is that once researchers can accurately measure levels of microplastics in human tissue and blood, they’ll be able to determine what exactly plastic pollution is doing to us.

“I don’t think we’ve got really good evidence at all for what effects [microplastics] might be having,” Rauert says. And the much-hyped finding that we eat a credit card’s worth of plastic each week? “That has absolutely been debunked.”

Cassandra Rauert. Courtesy of Cassandra Rauert

Yale Environment 360: Why is it so hard to study microplastics in the human body?

Cassandra Rauert: It’s quite a new field. And we’re trying to use analytical techniques that were developed for other purposes.

A few years ago, when we first started thinking about human exposure to plastics, we were like, “Oh, we should test some blood and just see what’s in there.” I tested a little bit of my blood and saw screamingly high levels of polyethylene. It just didn’t make sense. When I think about my diet, I’m not eating a lot of plastic-packaged foods. So that’s when we first started to think, “Okay, is there something else going on here with the analysis that we haven’t considered before?”

We assessed how reliable current measures are for trying to find microplastics in blood. And what we found is that lipids and fats will give you a false positive for polyethylene. Lipids are made up of the same building blocks as polyethylene, so when we analyze them, they look identical in our analysis instrument. This means that we can mistake a signal from a lipid for polyethylene if we don’t look at the data carefully.

e360: In a paper you published last year you found that 18 previous studies on microplastics in human blood had this issue.

Rauert: We were putting [the study] out there to say that you need to think about the data that’s coming out of your instrument, because there’s potential that these lipids, these fats, will give you a false positive. To our knowledge, these previous studies didn’t know about this issue and haven’t considered it, and future studies really should.

e360: Another challenge, you found, is that plastic is absolutely everywhere, including in lab equipment.

Rauert: In a normal chemistry lab, you’re surrounded by plastics. You have plastic pipettes. You have plastic Petri dishes. Plastics are quite necessary. They can be sterile. They’re single use. They have their purpose, but it means that you’ve got the potential for a lot of plastics to be shedding and to be in the lab around you.

We’re talking about very small particles and fibers. You can’t see them — that’s how small — and they’re always in the air around us. If you’re not conscious of this, these small particles and fibers can be accidentally falling into your sample. Or if your sample has touched plastic — for instance, a urine sample that’s stored in plastic — there’s a potential for some of the plastic to come off, which can contaminate your sample.

e360: You overhauled your lab to get rid of plastic contamination. What was involved?

Rauert: We worked with an architect, and we built the lab pretty much from scratch. The first thing we had to work out is what to build the lab out of. We tested about 30 different construction materials trying to find some that didn’t contain plastics, but also didn’t contain plastic [additives] such as phthalates, but we couldn’t find any. Everything had either plastics or phthalates in them.

We can’t have any wood or cardboard in the lab. It can get mold. It can get bacteria. So we ended up going with stainless steel. It was the only way to not have any plastics. Even when you put a glass panel in the window, you have silicon holding the glass in. We tested all these different brands of silicon to try to find ones that had low levels of phthalates. It was a crazy amount of detail that we went to, but it was really worth it.

We ended up with three interconnected rooms, and they’re all positive pressure. The idea is that when you open the door, it pushes contamination out rather than bringing it in with you. And then when we commissioned the lab, the first thing we did was put background samplers in there to see what was in the air. In the lab, the plastics and the phthalates are about a hundred times lower than in our normal lab.

The plastics-free lab Cassandra Rauert and colleagues designed at the University of Queensland. The University of Queensland

e360: Is it a little bit disturbing to consider that plastics are so ubiquitous that you have to build a clean room from the ground up just to be able to study them?

Rauert: It’s definitely made me think about my house a lot and all of the plastic consumer products that I have, and all the plastic stuff I have in my kitchen, which I have now been exchanging for metal and wood. You really don’t realize how much plastics you use in your everyday life until you start purposely looking for it. It’s just a given. You just grow up with it.

e360: As far as we know, how does plastic typically enter our bodies?

Rauert: We do know that, in terms of what’s in the air around us, we have higher concentrations inside our houses. We know a lot of these [plastic additives] end up in the dust in your house, so absolutely vacuuming more often is a great way to get rid of these.

e360: And what are the biggest sources of plastic in your home?

Rauert: We’ve been looking at residential balconies, and we’re seeing high concentrations of [particles from] tires [which are made with synthetic polymers]. There’s a potential for tire [particles] to be getting into your house dust. We still don’t have huge amounts of information on that.

In terms of what you’re breathing in, it’s probably going to be mostly from synthetic fibers. If you’re using your dryer a lot, and you’re putting your polyester or your nylon products in the dryer, you’re shedding a lot of fibers. We recommend not using your dryer for those materials. See if you can hang them up.

Inside the plastics industry playbook: Delay, deny, and distract. Read more.

But the biggest sources around your house are probably going to be what you’re preparing your food with. If you’re using a plastic chopping board, small pieces of plastic are being cut out of that and landing in your food. Using plastic kitchen utensils is probably not a good idea. I would change those over to bamboo or wood or metal. Heating your food in a plastic container will shed more of those plastics as well.

e360: Do we really eat a credit card’s worth of plastic each week?

Rauert: That has absolutely been debunked. We’ve been looking at what sheds from plastic food containers under different conditions, and they do shed plastics, and we can measure it, but it’s absolutely not that much plastic.

e360: What are some of the outstanding questions for researchers?

Rauert: I think the first thing is we don’t actually know what we’re exposed to. What plastic food containers actually shed plastics, and what do they look like? What size are they? We know that when we’re doing a load of washing, it releases fibers. Using your dryer will release synthetic fibers. But if we are inhaling those fibers, do we just cough them up again, or can they actually get deeper into our lungs?

Plastic kitchen tools are a source of microplastic contamination. Sergey Ryzhov / Alamy

e360: And while we know a lot about the impact of chemicals added to plastic — such as phthalates, which have been shown to impact fertility, or bisphenols, which have been linked to Type 2 diabetes — we know very little about what effect the plastic particles themselves might be having.

Rauert: Yeah, we do know that we are exposed to a lot of plastic [additives]. And we know that they’re endocrine disruptors. We’ve been monitoring them and analyzing them for decades.

I don’t think we’ve got really good evidence at all for what effects [microplastics particles on their own] might be having on human bodies. If we’re eating plastics, what size and what type of plastic can actually get into the bloodstream?

The majority of plastic [particles] that we know we are exposed to are too big to cross from our gut into the bloodstream — only very small particles can do this — therefore, we excrete them out. This mechanism hasn’t been studied in detail, but analysis of stool samples has shown a wide variety of plastic particles in them.

We still have very little knowledge of the smallest-sized plastics though, so we don’t know how many of these are excreted or how many can cross the barriers in our body. And I think we need to understand that a bit more so that we can then direct toxicology studies as well.

Also, a lot of the toxicology studies, especially in the past, have tended to use [lab-grade] polystyrene spheres as their representative microplastic particle. That’s the only standard that has been available to use in a toxicology study. But we’re not exposed to perfect spheres of polystyrene. If it’s going to be polystyrene, it’s going to be a fragment or a shard. So it’s not really representative.

e360: It has long been a tactic of corporations to resist regulation by saying not enough is known about their products’ health impacts. Do you see that happening here?

I know it is easy to say we don’t have enough information yet, but we do know about [the health risks from] these chemicals that are in all the plastics that your food is wrapped in. From that perspective, it is still a good idea to reduce plastics or eliminate them where possible.

#3341. Collapse of Atlantic Currents May Already Be ‘Locked In’

Ocean currents in the North Atlantic. Warmer colors indicate higher temperatures. NASA

A vast system of Atlantic currents that delivers warmth to northern Europe is at risk of collapse, according to a growing body of research. The latest study to warn of its demise finds there is at least a 10 percent chance that a collapse may already be “locked in.”

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, or AMOC, ferries warm water from the tropics to northern Europe. As the water cools, it becomes more dense and drops to the ocean depths, where it flows south, back toward the tropics. But as oceans warm, those waters are not so cool, or so dense, as they once were, causing the system of ocean circulation to slow down. At the same time, the melting of the Greenland ice sheet is introducing large amounts of fresh water, which is less dense than salty water, further slowing circulation.

By one estimate, if the AMOC were to collapse, its flow slowing to a near halt, temperatures in northern Europe would drop by 9 to 27 degrees F (5 to 15 degrees C).

The new modeling study found that even in a scenario where global emissions peaked in 2025 and the melting of the Greenland ice sheet raises sea levels by just 2 inches this century, there is a 10 percent chance that the AMOC will eventually collapse. In scenarios where the Greenland ice sheet unleashes more meltwater, the probability of collapse rises to 23 percent, according to the study, which has not yet undergone peer review.

Coauthor Tim Lenton, of the University of Exeter, told New Scientist, “What the model is saying to me is, ‘Let’s do everything in our power to get to net-zero as quickly as possible to try to keep this probability down at the 10 percent level.’”

Genutzt werden soll die "ND-Cloud" vom Bundesamt für Verfassungsschutz (BfV), dem Bundesnachrichtendienst (BND) und dem Militärischen Abschirmdienst (MAD) der Bundeswehr. Erstmals wären die Behörden damit in der Lage, schnell und umfangreich Daten bis zur Geheimhaltungsstufe "Geheim" auszutauschen. Perspektivisch sollen auch die Verfassungsschutzbehörden der Bundesländer angeschlossen werden.

Nach Informationen von NDR und WDR soll diese Cloud auf dem Produkt Google Distributed Cloud (GDC) basieren, und zwar auf einer sogenannten Air-Gapped-Version. Das bedeutet, dass das System autonom betrieben wird, ohne eine direkte Verbindung ins Internet oder zu Google. Die Daten sollen in Deutschland gespeichert sein und Google keinen Zugriff haben. Es soll sich demnach um ein getrenntes IT-Netz handeln, das besonders geschützt ist.

Geplant war offenbar, das System bereits im ersten Quartal 2027 in Betrieb zu nehmen, allerdings verzögert sich das Projekt: Mehrere Komponenten der geplanten "ND-Cloud" befinden sich nach Informationen von NDR und WDR derzeit noch in einem Zulassungsverfahren durch das Bundesamt für die Sicherheit in der Informationstechnik (BSI). Erst, wenn diese Zulassungen erfolgt sind, sollen die Haushaltsmittel für das kostspielige Projekt freigegeben werden. Die geschätzten Kosten sollen sich laut Insidern auf mindestens 500 Millionen Euro belaufen.

"Bei einer Zulassung ist davon auszugehen, dass die Voraussetzungen für die Datenhaltung und Datenverarbeitung bei solch sensiblen Inhalten gegeben sind", sagt Sven Herpig, Leiter Cybersecurity & Emerging Threats bei Interface, einer unabhängigen Organisation von IT-Fachleuten und Cybersicherheitsexperten. Er verweist allerdings darauf, dass künftige Updates zu einer Veränderung der Software führen könnten.

"Die Updates kämen weiterhin von Google. Dann stellt sich die Frage: Kann der Betreiber hierzulande überhaupt beurteilen, welche Auswirkungen ein Update möglicherweise hat?", so Herpig. "Daran schließt sich wiederum die Frage an: Wie groß müssen Änderungen sein, damit eine Update-Version wieder neu bewertet und zugelassen werden muss?"

Grundsätzlich wirkt die Wahl eines Google-Produktes durchaus widersprüchlich zu den jüngsten Aussagen von Vertretern der Sicherheitsbehörden im Hinblick auf die digitale Souveränität. "Wir müssen im Endeffekt in der Lage sein, Alternativen zu bieten", sagte Sinan Selen, Präsident des Bundesamtes für Verfassungsschutz (BfV) im vergangenen Jahr auf die Frage, ob seine Behörde US-amerikanische Softwarelösungen zur Datenanalyse einsetzen solle.

Auf Anfrage zur Nutzung des Google-Produkts schreibt das BfV, "die Handlungs- und Innovationsfähigkeit des BfV hängt direkt von der Verfügbarkeit moderner IT-Systeme und Technologieplattformen ab". Zur Stärkung der eigenen "technischen Resilienz" seien "europäische Lösungen dabei von besonderem Interesse". Darüber hinaus wollten weder BND, MAD noch BfV Fragen zu der Cloud beantworten.

Es wächst die Sorge, dass US-Anbieter von Software und Datendiensten zu geopolitischen Druckmitteln werden könnten. mehr

Neben der Software ist insbesondere der Aufbau von Rechenzentren mit ausreichend Kapazität notwendig. Für den Betrieb der "ND-Cloud" wollen die Nachrichtendienste nach derzeitigen Planungen auch auf die BWI, das IT-Systemhaus der Bundeswehr, zurückgreifen. Diese hatte schon im vergangenen Sommer eine Partnerschaft mit Google bekannt gegeben, um künftig auf Google Cloud Air-Gapped Systemen geschützte Daten der Bundeswehr zu verarbeiten.

Nachrichtendienste sind mit immer mehr Daten konfrontiert, künftig sollen sie auch automatisierte Datenanalysen anwenden können, etwa mittels Künstlicher Intelligenz. Der BND soll zudem bald im Zuge der Strategischen Aufklärung mehr Daten erfassen, speichern und verarbeiten dürfen. Dafür sind größere Speicher- und Rechenkapazitäten notwendig.

Auch die Geheimdienste anderer Staaten setzen auf Cloud-Dienstleistungen kommerzieller Anbieter für die Speicherung, Verarbeitung und den Austausch geheimer Informationen. So stellt etwa Amazon mit dem Produkt Amazon Web Services (AWS) Secret Cloud den US-Geheimdiensten und dem US-Militär ein solches System zur Verfügung. Im Jahr 2021 berichteten Medien außerdem, dass in Großbritannien ein Vertrag mit Amazon für die Geheimdienst-Cloud geschlossen worden sei.

#3347. What is a cell?

Cells are the fundamental units of life where most of the essential chemistry and functions that keep us alive happen. Cells are the building blocks of every organism and make up most of the structures within the body.

Prokaryotic cells are simpler than eukaryotic cells and don’t have a nucleus or membrane bound organelles.

Prokaryotic cells are generally smaller than eukaryotic cells and are usually single-celled organisms.

Eukaryotic cells are more complex than prokaryotic cells. They have a defined nucleus and organelles. All animal and plant cells are eukaryotic.

Eukaryotic cells can be single-celled or multicellular organisms.
