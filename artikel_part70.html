#3451. Roma gegen Hitler: Tschechien würdigt Widerstandskämpfer

Mehr als 1.400 Roma kämpften in der Tschechoslowakei gegen die deutsche Nazi-Besatzung. Eine neue Ausstellung in Prag erzählt von Partisanen und Soldaten, deren Schicksale jahrzehntelang kaum Beachtung fanden.

Für mehr als eine halbe Million Roma in Tschechien und der Slowakei war der 15. Oktober 2026 ein bedeutender Tag. An diesem Tag wurde im Prager "Museum der nationalen Wiedergeburt der Roma" eine Ausstellung über ein weitgehend vergessenes Kapitel tschechoslowakischer Geschichte eröffnet - eine Ausstellung über tschechische und slowakische Roma-Widerstandskämpfer während der nationalsozialistischen deutschen Besatzung und Zerschlagung der Tschechoslowakei von 1938 bis 1945.

Bislang tauchten die tschechischen und slowakischen Roma in der Geschichte des Zweiten Weltkriegs fast ausschließlich als Opfer des Holocausts an Roma und Sinti auf. Im sogenannten Protektorat Böhmen und Mähren ermordeten die deutschen Nationalsozialisten während des Zweiten Weltkriegs rund neunzig Prozent der tschechischen Roma.

Weniger bekannt ist, dass eine große Zahl tschechischer und slowakischer Roma als Partisanen in Widerstandsgruppen und Soldaten der tschechoslowakischen Untergrundarmee (Verteidigung der Nation, ON) gegen die deutschen Besatzer kämpften. Dokumentiert sind die Beispiele von mehr als 1400 Personen. Diesem Kapitel des bewaffneten Widerstands der Roma gegen die Nazis ist die Ausstellung im Museum der nationalen Wiedergeburt der Roma gewidmet.

Sie markiert einen Wendepunkt der Sichtweise auf die Roma während des Zweiten Weltkriegs - so hofft es jedenfalls der Museumsdirektor Emil Scuka, Jurist, ehemaliger Staatsanwalt und einer der bekanntesten tschechischen Roma-Aktivisten. "Wir waren nicht nur Opfer, wir waren auch Helden", sagte Scuka bei der Eröffnung der Ausstellung. "Dass wir Roma gegen den Nationalsozialismus gekämpft haben, wissen viele Menschen nicht, nicht einmal unter den Roma selbst."

Der Museumsdirektor Emil Scuka sprach vor mehreren hundert Teilnehmern der Vernissage, bei denen es sich überwiegend um Roma aus Tschechien und der Slowakei handelte. Die Ausstellung zu konzipieren und zu eröffnen, so Scuka, "war etwas, was meine Generation diesen Roma-Helden schuldig war, und diese Schuld begleichen wir heute". Der Museumsdirektor forderte die Anwesenden auf: "Bringen Sie Ihre Kinder und Enkelkinder hierher, damit sie sich ein Bild machen und erkennen, dass auch die Roma ihre Helden haben!"

Scuka wies auch darauf hin, dass dieses Thema in der tschechischen und slowakischen Geschichtsschreibung jahrzehntelang völlig am Rande des Interesses gestanden habe und Roma daher selbst beschlossen hätten, die Schicksale ihrer Kriegshelden eigenständig zu dokumentieren.

Die Ausstellung zeigt die detaillierte Lebensgeschichten von 100 der bedeutendsten Roma-Partisanen und Roma-Soldaten der tschechischen Untergrundarmee und der Widerstandgruppen, die gegen den Nationalsozialismus kämpften. "Wir haben in den Archiven über 1.400 Roma-Kämpfer gefunden. Davon haben wir für diese Ausstellung die hundert interessantesten Schicksale ausgewählt", sagte der Kurator der Ausstellung, Radoslav Scuka, bei der Eröffnung.

Zu diesen Persönlichkeiten gehört beispielsweise Anton Facuna, ein Unteroffizier des amerikanischen Geheimdienstes OSS, der im Oktober 1944 mit einer geheimen Mission per Flugzeug in die Slowakei gebracht wurde - die von 1939 bis 1945 ein Marionettenstaat Hitlers war.

Ein weiterer ist Josef Serinek, bekannt unter dem Spitznamen "Der schwarze Partisan aus der Vysocina", dem die Flucht aus dem nationalsozialistischen Konzentrationslager für Roma in Lety gelang und der anschließend in der Böhmisch-Mährischen Hochebene die große Partisaneneinheit "Capajev-Cerny" gründete und befehligte. Im Jahr 2022 verlieh ihm der tschechische Präsident Milos Zeman posthum die Medaille für Heldentum. Am Ende seines Lebens teilte Serinek seine Erinnerungen mit dem Historiker Jan Tesar, der daraufhin das dreiteilige Buch "Ceská cikanska rapsodie" (Tschechische Zigeuner-Rhapsodie) verfasste.

Mit Ausnahme von Tesars Buch wurde die Geschichte des Roma-Widerstands gegen den Nationalsozialismus von tschechischen und slowakischen Historikern bis heute nicht erforscht. "Mir ist keine wissenschaftliche historische Arbeit bekannt, die sich mit dem Widerstand der Roma gegen den Nationalsozialismus befasst", sagte Prokop Tomek, Historiker am Militärhistorischen Institut in Prag, der DW.

"Die Schätzung von 1400 Roma, die sich in den Reihen der Widerstandskämpfer und der ausländischen Armee am Kampf gegen den Nationalsozialismus beteiligt haben, erscheint mir nicht übertrieben und entspricht dem Anteil der Roma-Bevölkerung in der Tschechoslowakei", kommentiert Tomek die Schätzungen der Organisatoren der Ausstellung im Prager Roma-Museum. "Die Ausstellung über den Widerstand der Roma gegen den Nationalsozialismus während des Krieges ist zweifellos bahnbrechend", fügt Tomek hinzu.

Viele Roma in Tschechien und der Slowakei bekennen sich bis heute öffentlich nicht zu ihrer ethnischen Zugehörigkeit. Dies liegt auch an den über Generationen weitergegebenen Erfahrungen mit dem Holocaust während der nationalsozialistischen Zeit. Tschechische Historiker und Politiker streiten bis heute darüber, inwieweit die tschechischen Behörden des Protektorats an der Vernichtung der tschechischen Roma beteiligt waren. Soviel ist jedoch klar: Die in der Tschechoslowakei seit 1927 gesetzlich verankerte polizeiliche Erfassung aller Roma erleichterte den deutschen Nationalsozialisten die Vernichtung der Volksgruppe.

Nach Schätzungen der tschechischen Regierung leben im Land heute rund 250.000 Roma. Bei der Volkszählung im Jahr 2021 gaben jedoch nur 21.000 Personen diese ethnische Zugehörigkeit an, also weniger als ein Zehntel der tatsächlichen Zahl. Von allen ethnischen Gruppen im zehn Millionen Einwohner zählenden Tschechien gehören die Roma zwar zu den drei zahlenmäßig stärksten ethnischen Minderheiten, sind jedoch sozial am schlechtesten gestellt, verfügen nur über sehr wenige politische Vertretungen und sind daher in der tschechischen Politik nur wenig präsent.

Zwei wichtige Gremien für Roma in Tschechien sind der Regierungsrat für Roma sowie die Regierungsbeauftragte für Roma-Angelegenheiten. Im Juni 2026 traten die Beauftragte Lucie Fukova und sechs Roma-Vertreter des 15-köpfigen Regierungsrats zurück, weil die Kompetenzen für Roma-Angelegenheiten von der gesamten Regierungsebene auf eine niedere Ebene in das Arbeitsministerium verlagert worden waren. Daran hatte unter anderem der tschechische Ombudsmann Kritik geübt. Auch viele Roma-Aktivisten sehen darin eine Schwächung der Roma-Agenda auf Regierungsebene.

Dass die rechtsliberal-populistische Regierung des Premiers Andrej Babis Roma-Angelegenheiten keine besondere Aufmerksamkeit widmet, war auch am Freitag bei der Ausstellungseröffnung zu erkennen: Einen Vertreter oder eine Vertreterin hatte die Regierung nicht entsandt.

#3453. "Nur ein toter Bandit ist ein guter Bandit" - Wahlkampf in Brasilien

Brasiliens Wähler sehen Gewalt und Kriminalität als das größte Problem im Land. Die Macht der Banden wird immer größer. Die Kandidaten bei der Stichwahl ums Präsidentenamt heute stehen dabei für unterschiedliche Lösungen.

Es ist noch dunkel, als am 28. Oktober 2025 Hunderte Polizisten in die Favelas Complexo da Penha und Complexo do Alemão in Rio de Janeiro eindringen. Schon bald brennen Barrikaden, fallen Schüsse. Es sind Szenen wie im Krieg. Mitglieder der mächtigen Drogenorganisation Comando Vermelho leisten mit schweren Waffen und sogar Granaten massiven Widerstand.

Vor allem im bewaldeten Hügelgebiet oberhalb der Favelas kommt es zu heftigen Gefechten. Was dort genau geschieht, ist bis heute nicht vollständig geklärt - Ermittlungen zum Einsatz laufen. Es geht unter anderem um die Frage, ob die Polizei unverhältnismäßige oder rechtswidrige Gewalt angewendet hat.

Am Ende des Einsatzes sind 122 Menschen tot, darunter fünf Polizisten. Es ist der tödlichste Polizeieinsatz in der Geschichte Brasiliens. Die Behörden erklären die 117 Toten zu Mitgliedern des organisierten Verbrechens, in bislang einem Fall ist das umstritten.

Für die einen ist die Operation ein Erfolg im Kampf gegen die Drogenbanden. Für andere ist es ein Massaker, bei dem der Staat seine Macht missbraucht hat.

Tauã Brito hat dabei ihren 20-jährigen Sohn Wellington verloren. Sie scrollt durch alte Textnachrichten. Bis 8.22 Uhr am Morgen tauschen sie Nachrichten aus. Dann bricht der Kontakt ab. Brito weiß, dass ihr Sohn kriminell war. Sie sagt, sie habe ihn zweimal verloren: erst an die Drogenbande, dann durch die Polizei.

"Richtig wäre, dass ich meinen Sohn im Gefängnis besuchen müsste, nicht dass ich ihn begraben musste", sagt sie. Brito mache sich Vorwürfe, dass sie nicht verhindern konnte, dass ihr Sohn sich der Drogenbande angeschlossen hat.

Taua Brito verlor bei einem Polizeieinsatz ihren Sohn. Viele befürworten die Einsätze gegen Banden in Brasilien.

Aber wo der Staat kaum Bildung und Zukunftschancen bietet, gewinnen kriminelle Organisationen an Anziehungskraft. Viele sehen die Favelas aus Ausgangspunkt der Kriminalität, aber das stimme nicht, meint Brito. "Wer verkauft die Waffen, wer bringt die Drogen? Ein korruptes Netzwerk aus Unternehmern, Politikern und Polizisten stehen an der Spitze", sagt sie, getötet würden die am unteren Ende.

Tatsächlich befürworteten mehr als 60 Prozent der Brasilianer den Polizeieinsatz. Inzwischen ist Tauã Brito aus dem Favela Penha weggezogen. Jede Straße erinnere sie an ihren Sohn. Doch die Trauer ist geblieben. Sie leidet unter Depressionen, Angstzuständen und Schlafproblemen. Psychologische Hilfe für sich und ihre Tochter bekommt sie durch eine private Initiative, nicht durch den Staat.

Bernardo Leal verlor bei dem Einsatz sein Bein. Dass er überlebte, sieht er als Auftrag.

Ganz anders blickt Bernardo Leal auf den Einsatz. Der leitende Kriminalpolizist sollte damals mit seinem Team Haftbefehle vollstrecken. Doch Leal gerät in einen Hinterhalt, die Kugel eines Sturmgewehrs durchschlägt seine Oberschenkelarterie. Der Familienvater verliert lebensgefährlich viel Blut, liegt eine Woche im Koma und muss neunmal operiert werden.

Er überlebt, für viele eine Wunder, doch sein Bein kann nicht gerettet werden. Leal spricht nicht gerne darüber, aber eines seiner Kinder leidet seitdem unter großer Verlustangst.

Einen Tag nach dem Einsatz hat die Bande Commando Vermelho die Macht in Penha wieder übernommen. Die Sicherheitskräfte riskieren ihr Leben, doch nachhaltig sind die Einsätze nicht. Ob das frustriert? Es brauche Pläne, wie der Staat die Kontrolle dauerhaft zurückgewinnen könne, sagt Leal. Immerhin aber habe der Einsatz gezeigt, dass der Staat stärker sei als die Kriminellen.

Bolsonaro geht als Favorit in die Stichwahl um die Präsidentschaft Brasiliens. Seine Wähler hoffen auf einen wirtschaftlichen Neustart. mehr

Den Vorwurf, die Polizei komme, um zu töten, weißt er zurück. Die Polizei werde mit schweren Waffen angegriffen und müsse sich verteidigen. Wer sich ergebe, werde verhaftet.

"Es kann keinen Staat geben, der keine Souveränität hat. Und wir haben keine Souveränität mehr über diese Gebiete, die von den Kriminellen besetzt wurden", sagt Leal. Die Polizei müsse Aufträge ausführen und den Rechtsstaat durchsetzen, sagt Leal. Wenn sie dabei erfolglose bleibe, sei der Staat verloren.

Tatsächlich weitet sich die Macht der Drogenorganisationen in Brasilien immer weiter aus. Die Ohnmacht frustriert die Menschen. In Umfragen werden Gewalt und Kriminalität als größtes Problem des Landes wahrgenommen. Der Satz "nur ein toter Bandit ist ein guter Bandit" findet viel Zuspruch.

Bolsonaros Familienname bleibt sein wichtigstes politisches Kapital - und gleichzeitig seine größte Schwäche. mehr

Der rechtskonservative Präsidentschaftskandidat Flávio Bolsonaro hat Kriminalität ins Zentrum seiner Kampagne gestellt, kündigt einen kompromisslosen Kurs an. Er fordert unter anderem härtere Strafen und zusätzliche Gefängnisse. "Ich erkläre den brasilianischen Narco-Terroristen den Krieg. Entweder ihr geht ins Gefängnis oder unsere Polizisten neutralisieren euch", so Bolsonaro.

Präsident Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva setzt vor allem auf eine bessere Zusammenarbeit der Ermittlungsbehörden und will die Finanzströme der kriminellen Organisationen bekämpfen. "Wir werden weder 100 noch 200 Menschen töten, wir werden sie verhaften", verspricht er.

Bernardo Leal empfindet sein Überleben nach der Verletzung als Auftrag. Er kandidierte für die konservative Partei Progressistas und wurde ins Parlament des Bundesstaates Rio de Janeiro gewählt. Es brauche mehr Bildung, mehr Zukunftschancen, aber auch härtere Gesetze und ein entschlosseneres Vorgehen gegen das organisierte Verbrechen, sagt er. Leal unterstützt Bolsonaro.

Kommt Bolsonaro, werde es noch mehr Tote geben, sagt dagegen Tauã Brito. Auch sie hat aus ihrem Verlust eine Mission gemacht. Sie hat eine Hilfsorganisation für Mütter gegründet, die ihre Kinder durch Gewalt verloren haben. Gemeinsam wollen sie einander Kraft geben.

Nature (2026) Cite this article

Magnetic tunnel junctions based on ferromagnets are canonical devices in spintronics, with wide-ranging applications in data storage, computing and sensing. They simultaneously exhibit mechanisms for electrical detection and control of magnetic order through the tunnelling magnetoresistance (TMR)1,2,3,4,5 and spin-transfer torque6,7,8,9 effects, respectively. It was long assumed that neither of these effects could be sizeable in all-antiferromagnetic tunnel junctions (AATJs), considering that they have no net magnetization. However, it was recently shown that AATJs based on chiral antiferromagnets do exhibit TMR due to their non-relativistic momentum-dependent spin polarization and cluster magnetic octupole moment, which are manifestations of their spin–split band structure10,11,12,13. However, the reciprocal effect, that is, the antiferromagnetic counterpart of spin–transfer torque, has been assumed non-existent due to the total electric current being spin-neutral. Here we report nanoscale AATJs that display this reciprocal effect, which we term octupole-driven spin-transfer torque (OTT). We demonstrate current-induced OTT switching of PtMn3|MgO|PtMn3 AATJs, with a TMR value of 363% at room temperature and switching current densities of the order of 10 MA cm–2. Our theoretical modelling explains the origin of OTT in terms of the imbalance between intra- and inter-sublattice spin currents across the AATJ, and, equivalently, in terms of the non-zero net cluster octupole polarization of each PtMn3 layer. This work establishes a new materials platform for antiferromagnetic spintronics and provides a pathway towards deeply scaled magnetic memory and room-temperature terahertz technologies.

#3457. Albanians Mobilize Against Jared Kushner Plan for Resort on Pristine River Delta

Protests in Tirana, Albania, earlier this month. Terfili via Wikipedia

In Albania, a mass protest movement has emerged to challenge a plan, spearheaded by Jared Kushner, to build a sprawling resort along the delta of the last wild river in Europe. Tens of thousands of demonstrators took to the capital city of Tirana last week, raising signs that said “Albania Is Not for Sale,” with marches continuing over the weekend.

The Vjosë River Delta is home to Mediterranean monk seals, loggerhead turtles, endangered Albanian water frogs, and a rare colony of Dalmatian pelicans, which are among the largest freshwater birds in the world. But coastal wildlife is at grave risk from the resort project, as Yale Environment 360 reported after plans for the resort were first unveiled in 2024. “These developments are highly alarming, and we are strongly concerned about losing the last natural coastal areas in the Mediterranean,” Annette Spangenberg, of the group EuroNatur, told e360.

An investment firm helmed by Jared Kushner, son-in-law of President Donald Trump, has shared plans for thousands of hotel rooms and villas along the outer edge of the Vjosë Delta and the nearby island of Sazan. Both areas are theoretically protected. But in 2024, Albanian lawmakers empowered the prime minister to permit construction of upscale tourist resorts in protected areas.

That law was ratified just three days after plans for the Vjosë resort went public. Earlier this month, SPAK, the office of the special prosecutor combating corruption, announced that it had opened an investigation into the legislation.

Recently, workers began erecting a barbed-wire-topped fence at a site on the Vjosë Delta, and heavy machinery began rolling over sand dunes and pine forests to make way for access roads, The Guardian reported. Private security guards have allegedly assaulted protesters at the site, according to Albanian journalist Isa Myzyraj.

The first signs of construction have sparked a protest movement that has been dubbed the “Flamingo Revolution,” owing to the inflatable pink flamingos brandished by protesters. The delta is the only breeding ground for flamingos in Albania.

“From start to finish there has been a total lack of transparency,” Aleksandër Trajçe, head of the group Protection and Preservation of the Natural Environment in Albania, told The Guardian. “We have seen no public consultation or public documentation regarding permits, and so now what we are saying is, if they remove the bulldozers, remove the fence, and restore the habitats to what they were, then we can start talking.”

#3463. Summer Science Challenges for Kids

Our Summer holidays are about to start, so I thought it would be a good time to spruce up this post full of Summer Science Challenges for kids. All the activities are very simple and use mostly things you probably already have around the house.

For an ultimate summer of science, try one activity each day, or just pick and choose the ones you like when you have a bit of spare time!

Science challenges are great as they give children the opportunity to work with more freedom than a traditional science experiment. Open-ended challenges can be great for teamwork and independent creative thinking. They’re also great for a parent/child project, too!

The lovely Mrs Mactivity has created some fun printable science challenges that you can cut out and use as prompts. Scroll to the bottom of the post to download.

If you haven’t heard of Mrs Mactivity, do go and take a look at her website; she has some wonderful primary resources available.

A sugar cube tower is a fantastic, inexpensive STEM challenge. Children can build tall towers or bigger structures.

All you need to make a lava lamp is oil, water and an Alka Seltzer or effervescent vitamin tablet. These are great as they can be used over and over again. Just wait for the reaction to stop, add a bit more Alka Seltzer, and it’ll start all over again!

Use coffee or tea to colour paper and create a fun treasure map.

This is great for experimenting with different concentrations of liquid. A more concentrated tea or coffee solution will give a darker coloured map.

Mix a little lemon or lime juice with water and write a message on a piece of paper.

Ask an adult to put it in the oven, and the message will appear.

Can you build something functional with a cardboard box?

We added a kitchen roll holder and space for a sink to our cardboard kitchen.

Make a delicious dessert with a solar oven! This is a great activity for learning about reflecting and absorbing materials.

Design a sinking and floating experiment. This is a great paddling pool science activity on a warm summer day.

Children can make predictions first, then investigate to see if they’re correct. Some items might surprise them!

Use cardboard discs to make spinning pens. You can make these with paper and thinner or thicker card to see which works best.

Drop a Mento into a bottle of fizzy drink and watch what happens. Remember to stand back and drop the mento in as fast as you can!

As an extra challenge, kids can investigate whether low sugar drinks erupt better than full sugar varieties.

The coke and mento reaction is a science activity everyone should try at least once.

Can you predict which substances will react with baking soda? Were your predictions correct?

Looking for bugs is a fantastic summer science challenge that doesn’t cost anything. Download my FREE bug hunt template to make it even easier!

Something like our Humpty Dumpty egg drop experiment always works well. The idea is to place an egg in a bag filled with a protective material and then drop the bag to investigate which material best protects the egg.

Blow up a balloon with Alka Seltzer or an effervescent vitamin tablet. Alka Seltzer and water react together, releasing carbon dioxide gas. The gas fills the space in the container and then fills the balloon, inflating it!

Warning – marshmallow and straw towers can be a bit sticky, but is great fun and a fantastic team building science activity.

Design and build a LEGO marble maze? Or use marbles inside a cardboard box.

The volcano below is made using papier mache, but sand also works well!

Check out our baking soda volcano ideas for inspiration.

Set up some simple baking soda reactions outdoors. These could be fairy themed, witchy potions or just simple messy play.

Play a tune using water and glass bottles. If you don’t have glass bottles, make a guitar by stretching elastic bands over a cardboard box.

You can’t really create a square bubble, but you can create something that looks like a square bubble using a cube frame dipped in bubble mix.

Build a house from sweets. We usually experiment with different types of “glue” to find out which works the best.

Once the house is built, the fun bit is testing how strong it is!

Try some ice painting, simply freeze a sheet of ice or an ice shape and paint over the top. Once finished, you can wipe away your creation and start over.

Search for the colours of the rainbow in a bubble. When light from the sun shines through the bubbles, it is reflected and dispersed, splitting white light into its different wavelengths and revealing all the colours of the rainbow!

Can you split light into a rainbow using a prism?

Use ice and salt to make ice cream from milk. This is a delicious activity for a sunny day. The salt lowers the freezing point of the ice, which then draws heat from the milk mixture, causing it to freeze.

Can you dissect a flower and separate the parts? Do you know the function of each?

Make a fairy potion, collect flowers and herbs to make a lovely smelling potion. Does the water change colour? Which smells are the strongest?

Make jumping frogs from tissue paper to learn about static electricity? The tissue paper frogs jump up to a balloon charged with static electricity.

Make a volcano with sand. We covered this one with cling film to keep the sand clean and then made a food colouring, baking soda and water mix for the eruption.

Create a fizzy baking soda explosion using baking soda, vinegar and food colouring. This is a fantastic way to learn about chemical reactions in a fun, hands-on and visual way.

Build a raft with sticks and test to see if it floats. Children can try to waterproof the bottom of the raft and add toy animals or other objects as an extension task.

Design and build a cork boat, and then test it to see if it floats. This is a great challenge for learning about buoyancy.

Can you spot a rainbow in a spray of water? This is an easy way to see light split into its constituent colours!

Use a salad spinner to make a spin art picture. These look amazing and are super easy to make.

Create colourful slime using cornstarch, water, and food colouring.

Scrunch it to make it solid and then watch it run through your fingers as it turns into a liquid!

Make a football in a box game by blowing down a straw to move marbles covered in paint. Try blowing softly and then harder to see how the marble moves.

Can you make a playdough brain? This is a fantastic way to learn about the various parts of the brain and their functions.

Try a bit of science magic and make an egg float. All you need for this simple science trick is an egg, jug, water and salt.

Do you know why pizza dough rises ? Find out while making a delicious meal!

Make raisins dance around a glass with some clever science. Adding baking soda causes the raisins to move around, as if they’re dancing!

We used our giant catapult as a tennis ball launcher, but you could also try using water balloons or table tennis balls.

New for 2018 are these amazing Science Challenge printable cards, created by the wonderful Mrs Mactivity for us.

If these aren’t quite what you’re looking for, try one of my other fun science experiments for kids.

#3466. Dead Organisms Shape the Living World Long After They Perish, Research Shows

This article was originally published by Inside Climate News and is reproduced here as part of the Climate Desk collaboration.

A new paper details how the remnants of dead organisms strongly influence the fate of survivors.

Death casts a shadow over life, not only for people but also other animals, plants and entire ecosystems.

In some ways, the phenomenon is well known. A fallen tree sprouts a plethora of mushrooms while also hosting ants, beetles, and the creatures that feast on them.

But a new paper argues that these forces play important yet poorly understood roles in nearly all ecosystems, with the remnants of certain species exerting significant influence long after they die.

The study, published Wednesday in Science Advances, claims to be “the first continental-scale assessment of how live foundation species are influenced by their dead counterparts.”

These “foundation species” generally provide the physical architecture of an ecosystem and tend to be its most abundant organisms — trees, grasses, corals, oysters. The study examined data from 10 ecosystems, ranging from the tropics to subpolar, from montane to marine.

In all but one of those ecosystems, the paper found, the dead remains significantly altered the growth, survival, or makeup of living organisms of the same or similar species. In some cases, dead material gave an assist, while in others it posed restraints. Either way, the impacts are far more widespread than previously understood, the authors argue.

“It’s this surprisingly common thread,” said Kai Kopecky, the lead author and a postdoctoral research fellow at the University of Colorado Boulder’s Environmental Data Science Innovation and Impact Lab.

Climate change and human development are driving increased disturbance across the globe, from more frequent and severe heat waves and wildfires to stronger storms. These disturbances are leading to more death, so a deeper understanding of the relationships between the dead and the living could help scientists and others better protect and restore ecosystems as they come under increased pressure, Kopecky said.

“Manipulating the dead remains of foundation species might be this very nature-based way of intervening,” Kopecky said.

Some of the dynamics included in the study are well understood and have already been incorporated into ecosystem management. After dredging, for example, scientists and others will add oyster shells to a damaged reef to promote new growth. Prescribed burns have long been used in tallgrass prairies to burn away dead grass, which inhibits new shoots.

But the authors found similar dynamics at play nearly everywhere they looked. Eastern hemlocks, when left standing after death, help new hemlock seedlings thrive by moderating the microclimates around them. After wildfires in boreal forests, allowing skeletal trees to remain standing translates into higher seed density in the surrounding soil.

Some deaths have the opposite effect. The remnants of branching corals, killed by marine heat waves, accelerated the decline of their remaining relatives. Their skeletal structures, it turned out, host macroalgae that compete with the coral. In salt marshes, dead vegetation damages live grass.

Andrew Dobson, a professor of ecology at Princeton University who was not involved in the study, said that while it is obvious, on one level, that trees and other species alter their environments even in death, he was surprised to learn how long-lasting and pervasive those effects were. He also stressed that the study shows the importance and utility of long-term research now under threat as the Trump administration slashes funding for science.

The study drew on data collected as part of the National Science Foundation’s Long Term Ecological Research Network, which includes multi-decadal datasets on ecosystems across the United States. The network was in the crosshairs of the Trump administration’s budget-cutting plans last year, The Seattle Times reported, while the administration’s budget request this spring calls for a nearly 60 percent cut to the National Science Foundation’s total research and related activities.

“Understanding how the natural world works is essential to our survival,” Dobson said, adding that the new study’s use of these tools underscores the point.

In some cases, the researchers drew on observational data collected at these sites. In others, the data came from earlier experiments that explored similar questions on the impacts of dead material.

The only ecosystem where the scientists did not find a strong relationship between the dead and the living was a giant kelp forest. Kopecky said one of the main drivers of mortality there are waves, which tend to wash away the remains, leaving little behind to alter new growth.

For Kopecky, the study is part of a broader passion for exploring what he thinks is the sometimes overlooked role of death in ecological systems. This study raised more questions than answers, he said. The remains of species could play different roles over time, for example. While detritus from hurricanes initially inhibits new growth in the tropical forest they studied, it is possible that it eventually helps fertilize new seedlings as it decomposes, serving the opposite effect.

Some of the datasets the researchers drew on spanned decades, while others included just a few years of information. Another uncertainty is whether the dynamics they identified are universal across a given type of ecosystem or vary from place to place.

Ultimately, Kopecky said he’d like to be able to develop a sort of template that scientists could apply broadly, so they might be able to predict the dynamics of any foundation species based on the characteristics of a given ecosystem.

He’s currently seeking funding to expand on the work. “This is probably going to be a more important thing that we should be paying attention to,” Kopecky said, “especially in light of climate change.”

Die Menschenrechtsorganisation habe die Formel 1 bereits im Februar 2025 angeschrieben und gefragt, wie die Rennserie die menschenrechtlichen Auswirkungen ihrer Aktivitäten bewerte, insbesondere im Zusammenhang mit Ruandas Bewerbung um einen Grand Prix. Eine Antwort habe es nicht gegeben.

Mudge fordert deshalb, dass die Formel 1 die Menschenrechtslage sorgfältig prüft, bevor sie Rennen in Ruanda endgültig genehmigt.

"Ruanda hat wie andere Staaten in der Vergangenheit hochkarätige Sportveranstaltungen genutzt, um von seiner katastrophalen Menschenrechtsbilanz abzulenken", sagte er. Zudem sei das Land maßgeblich an der Unterstützung der M23-Rebellengruppe im Osten der Demokratischen Republik Kongo beteiligt. Ruandische Streitkräfte würden mit Vertreibungen und Todesfällen von Zivilisten in Verbindung gebracht, die gegen das humanitäre Völkerrecht verstoßen könnten.

Auch Mohamed Keita von der Human Rights Foundation begrüßt grundsätzlich die Rückkehr der Formel 1 nach Afrika, sieht die Wahl des Gastgeberlandes jedoch kritisch.

"Dass die Formel 1 den zweitgrößten Kontinent der Welt drei Jahrzehnte lang aus ihrem Rennkalender ausgeschlossen hat, ist an sich schon nicht zu rechtfertigen und ein Makel für einen Sport, der sich als global versteht", sagte Keita der DW.

Gleichzeitig bezeichnete er die Entscheidung für Ruanda als "äußerst umstritten".

"Ruanda ist eine brutale Diktatur. Gegen das Regime bestehen teilweise US-Sanktionen wegen seiner militärischen Aktivitäten im Osten der Demokratischen Republik Kongo. Ruanda unterstützt und finanziert eine Miliz, die an Vergewaltigungen, Massakern, Zwangsrekrutierungen und der gewaltsamen Ausbeutung von Rohstoffen beteiligt ist."

#3475. Efforts to Save Kelp Forests from Ocean Warming Are Ramping Up

Researcher Rilee Sanders surveys a kelp forest off the coast of San Clemente, California. The Bay Foundation

At one time, kelp forests — which shelter fish, slow erosion, and sequester carbon — grew along a third of the world’s coastlines. Now, scientists are working to bolster heat-stressed kelp by attacking the urchins that prey on them and transplanting hardier kelp varieties.

In the coastal waters off British Columbia, tribal volunteers from the Haida Nation dive for purple sea urchins amid a dense forest of rippling golden-brown kelp fronds. Sunlight filters through the canopy, creating a mesmerizing dance of light and shadow, as rays and sea lions wend through the kelp maze, sharks glide past, and bright orange garibaldis dash between the swaying fronds.

Kelp forests are biodiversity hotspots teeming with a colorful variety of seaweeds, sponges, crustaceans, and other small ocean animals, many of them found nowhere else. At one time, vast kelp beds grew in nutrient-rich shallow waters along roughly a third of the world’s coastlines, where they helped to reduce the strength of waves, minimized coastal erosion, and provided shelter to fish, invertebrates, and marine mammals.

Today, however, many kelp forests are on life support, victims of water pollution from terrestrial agriculture and coastal development, bottom trawling for fish, and an explosion of kelp-devouring urchins, like those the Haida volunteers are collecting as part of an eradication program. But perhaps the most important driver of kelp decline is the rapid warming of the ocean.

Healthy kelp forests need cool, nutrient-rich seawater to survive. As ocean waters warm, kelp can no longer inhabit parts of their former range. The crisis is escalating quickly. Kelp forests are vanishing twice as fast as coral reefs and four times faster than tropical rainforests. An estimated 40 percent to 60 percent of kelp forests worldwide have been lost or significantly degraded in the last 50 years. These precipitous declines typically received far less scientific scrutiny than higher-profile ecological crises. But kelp has gradually been getting more attention as scientists and the environmental community come to recognize the value of the carbon that coastal ecosystems, including kelp forests, can capture.

A 2023 literature review of more than 180 papers that examined the potential for kelp to store carbon suggested that the climate benefits of these underwater forests may have been “grossly underestimated,” says Albert Pessarrodana, a research fellow at the University of Western Australia and the review’s lead author. “Kelps are one of the fastest growing plants on the planet,” he said in an email interview, “uptaking as much carbon as tropical rainforests per unit of area.”

Much of the carbon that kelp sequesters ends up being released back into the marine environment in the form of leaf litter (kelp, which is a macroalgae, has leaflike structures called blades that are its organs of photosynthesis.) This detritus is typically ingested by fish and other marine organisms and excreted in a matter of days. Still, a small percentage of it ends up in the deep ocean where it remains for centuries, or even millennia. Roughly 62 million tons of carbon is carried into the deep ocean by coastal currents each year, according to two studies published in 2024.

A diver collects urchins from a barren near Tromso, Norway, to allow kelp to recover. Peter Leopold / Urchinomics

“Unfortunately,” Pessarrodana says, “excessive warming can either kill kelps or severely curtail their growth, reducing their ability to uptake carbon.”

To counter the decline, scientists around the world are breeding new kelp varieties that they transplant as saplings into kelp habitat. The Scripps Institution of Oceanography and the San Diego Zoo have begun using artificial intelligence to digitally replicate kelp ecosystems and assess their vulnerability to climate change. Their findings will help focus resources on areas that are most likely to be saved. The organizations have also established a biobank to preserve kelp varieties for potential use in kelp farming and restoration projects. Researchers at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution on Cape Cod are using selective breeding to develop kelp strains with a higher tolerance to warming waters. Similar efforts are underway in China and Australia.

The Nature Conservancy’s Scott Breschkin has been working for the past year to eliminate urchins and replant beds of golden kelp on Australia’s Great Southern Reef, an interconnected system of rocky kelp reefs spanning about 5,000 miles of coastline across southern Australia and the island of Tasmania. Though less famed than the Great Barrier Reef, it is equally biodiverse, boasting thousands of species, some of which are still unknown to science.

As Australia’s coastal waters warm, long-spined urchins are expanding their ranges, leaving virtually lifeless urchin barrens in their wake. “Once the reef transitions to an urchin desert, it is very hard to flip it back to a productive kelp habitat,” Breschkin explained, adding that urchins can persist for decades in a zombie-like state, awakening only occasionally to mow down any kelp sprouts that may appear, which makes it virtually impossible for kelp forests to recover. Eradicating sea urchins, he says, is a critical first step for kelp restoration.

Jono Wilson, the director of ocean science for The Nature Conservancy’s California chapter, works with KelpWatch.org, a partnership of academic institutions and government agencies that is using satellite imagery and drones to monitor the distribution of canopy-forming kelps along California’s Pacific coast and assess where kelp restoration efforts have been successful. These undersea forests are often characterized by boom-and-bust cycles, flourishing and retreating as ecological conditions change, Wilson says. But recent climate-driven losses have been unprecedented. A 2013 to 2015 ocean warming event known as “the Blob” reduced kelp populations in Northern California by 95 percent.

Since 2015, water temperatures in California’s kelp forests have not dropped below 57 degrees F, a rough threshold beyond which kelp cannot thrive. Higher temperatures disrupt the kelp reproductive cycle, affecting their ability to produce viable offspring. Like corals, kelp bleaches when stressed, losing the chlorophyll that allows it to photosynthesize.

Bull kelp grow on a deep reef in Monterey Bay, California, that had been a sea urchin barren the previous year. Michael Langhans

Kelp diebacks transform their ecosystems. “Kelp provide habitat and food for thousands of species,” says Wilson. “They are nurseries for abalone and economically important fish species like cod and rockfish. They are meccas for kayakers and recreational scuba divers.” The Nature Conservancy estimates that kelp forests contribute $250 million in economic value to California annually.

Sea star murder mystery: What’s killing a key ocean species? Read more.

While kelp forests in Southern California are faring reasonably well — the dominant species, called giant kelp, grows quickly and can reach up to 200 feet tall — purple sea urchins are wiping out kelp beds dominated by bull kelp along the coasts of Central and Northern California. Wilson and his team are exploring ways to control those urchins. They are developing new kinds of more efficient urchin traps — round mesh devices baited with fish — and working with fertilizer companies to create a viable market for urchin shells, which contain calcium, nitrogen, and other plant nutrients. The Norwegian seafood firm Ava Ocean is currently using crushed urchin shells to produce a mineral-rich alternative to traditional bone-meal fertilizers.

There have also been attempts to support the native sunflower sea star, a voracious consumer of sea urchins. Populations of this predatory starfish, which can grow as large as a car tire, have been reduced by 90 percent since the outbreak, in 2013, of sea star wasting disease.

Scientists recently isolated the bacterium responsible for the epidemic, giving them a better shot at helping sea stars recover. Aquariums in California and Oregon have successfully treated their affected sea stars with antibiotics, and scientists are hoping to learn how to breed disease-resistant sunflower starfish that can be released into the wild.

There have also been efforts since the late 1980s to breed and reintroduce sea otters, another urchin predator, to the coastal waters where they once flourished. Driven to the edge of extinction by the fur trade in the 19th and early 20th centuries, otters have now substantially recovered in parts of their former range. They now number well over 3,000 individuals in Northern California. Otters are also staging a comeback along the coast of Washington and British Columbia. Kelp forests where the otters have been released are faring noticeably better than kelp forests without otters.

So far, kelp restoration projects in the U.S. have been small scale, with most covering less than a hundred acres. Such projects are “very expensive and subject to zoning laws that make [them] hard to initiate,” says Kyle Cavanaugh, a coastal geographer at the UCLA Institute of the Environment and Sustainability. “These localized projects need to be scaled up if we hope to turn the tide on the loss of kelp habitat.”

A kelp farm in Rongcheng, China. VCG via Getty Images

Kelp restoration is happening on a far larger scale in East Asia. More than half of South Korea’s kelp forests have been lost or badly degraded over the past century. But thanks to the world’s largest kelp restoration projects, the nation now boasts 71,660 acres of kelp forest. Its goal is to eventually re-green 75 percent of its coastline. Farmers in South Korea harvest nearly a billion dollars worth of seaweed each year, cutting only the upper fronds and blades of the macroalgae. There are also more than 700 restoration projects in Japan where kelp, known as kombu, is a staple in Japanese cuisine, most commonly used in soups.

Kelp’s value as a food source, and to a lesser extent as an ingredient in cosmetics, skincare products, and biodegradable packaging, may be one key to its survival. But if we don’t find a way to slash emissions of greenhouse gases, experts say, the long-term prospects of kelp and other key ocean ecosystems may be bleak. “It’s likely that we’ll see more destructive marine heat waves and warmer waters overall,” says Cavanaugh. “Beds of far less-productive turf algae will replace giant kelp and prevent it from becoming reestablished.”

Supertrawlers are taking Antarctic krill that whales depend on. Read more.

In Maine and in other coastal regions around the world, carpet-like turf algae is already replacing kelp, according to a paper published last month by the Bigelow Laboratory for Ocean Sciences in Maine. This is “a radical ecological transformation,” says the University of Maine’s Shane Farrell, the study’s lead author. “The good news is we now understand what’s driving this shift, and this will help us predict when and where it will happen next and create different conservation strategies to combat it.”

Group 3 (2010, 2015, 2016, 2023 and 2024) has the highest carbon emissions (0.49 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1) and comprises warm and dry years (high climate stress), such as El Niño and the warming of the Tropical North Atlantic Ocean, associated with biomass burning.

Amazonia, the largest tropical forest in the world, has lost substantial carbon stocks due to large-scale deforestation and other land-use changes. Remaining forests in the region have offset some of this carbon loss through carbon sequestration processes. However, this capacity is declining, primarily because of increased tree mortality6,7,8,18 and changes in regional climatic conditions modified by deforestation1,3,9,13. The Amazon forest stores approximately 123 ± 23 billion tonnes of carbon (PgC) in vegetation and soils19,20. The interactions among the forest ecosystem, hydrological cycle and regional climate are complex, with atmospheric moisture recycling by evapotranspiration accounting for up to half of precipitation16. Direct human activities within Amazonia, including deforestation, forest degradation and biomass burning, have profoundly altered the carbon balance of the region and climate11. Forest loss reduces precipitation, raises temperatures and lengthens the dry season, delaying the onset of the wet season1,3,4,14,16. During periods of extreme climatic variability, such as El Niño or anomalously high Tropical North Atlantic (TNA) sea-surface temperatures (SSTs), these impacts are amplified through the interaction of accumulated forest loss, global warming, changing circulation and large-scale anomalies9,14,16.

Many questions remain about the main drivers of carbon emissions and variability in Amazonia. Studies based on modelling and bottom-up techniques show increased emissions during extreme climate (warm and dry) years, pointing to extreme events as the reason for increased carbon emissions. However, our direct atmospheric measurements show a more complex situation. Here, we use our 15-year history of Amazonian carbon fluxes derived from atmospheric CO2 measurements to answer important questions about the main factors driving increased carbon emissions and variability in the Amazon and whether they are caused by global extreme climate events or direct human activities (deforestation, biomass burning and degradation) in the Amazon.

In 2000, deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon represented a total area of 474,770 km2 (12.5% of the Brazilian Amazon, 3.8 × 106 km2). The Brazilian Amazon represents 58% of the Pan-Amazonia region (Fig. 1). By 2004, the PRODES programme21 of Brazil for monitoring native vegetation had recorded cumulative deforestation of 612,225 km2 across the Legal Amazon, equivalent to 15.5% of the original forest cover of the region. Between 2004 and 2012, the annual rate of deforestation fell 84% as a result of public policies designed to curb clearing22,23, reaching a cumulative loss of 697,319 km2 (18.4% of the total forest area). The rate began to rise again from 2013 to 2018, when total forest loss reached 19.5%, and accelerated sharply after 2019, reaching 791,835 km2 (20.9%) in 2022. Although the annual rate of deforestation declined strongly in 2023 and 2024 (30% and 47%, respectively) relative to the 2019–2022 period, cumulative forest loss still reached 807,301 km2 (21.3%). From 2010 to 2024, pasture area expanded by 19%, soybean plantation area by 193% and mining by 272% (ref. 24) to estimated values of 561,416 km2, 59,368 km2 and 3,956 km2, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1).

This study includes 1,017 vertical profiles conducted between 2010 and 2024 at four Amazonian sites, whose upwind areas of influence represent nearly the entirety of Amazonia (Fig. 1 and Extended Data Fig. 2). Vertical profiles were typically performed twice per month, from 4.4 km above sea level (a.s.l.) to near the surface (about 0.3 km a.s.l.), sampling air at 12 heights in a descending helical trajectory at each of the four sites between 12:00 and 13:00 local time. Air samples were analysed for CO2 and CO at the Greenhouse Gas Laboratory (LaGEE/INPE), São José dos Campos, Brazil. The Amazon exhibits a dominant lower-tropospheric circulation pattern, with air masses originating over the tropical Atlantic Ocean, crossing the Amazon towards the Andes, then to the southern regions, and returning to the Atlantic in the southern regions. This circulation enables integration of both natural and anthropogenic processes by computing atmospheric mass balance across vertical profiles of sites. To better understand the variability in Amazonia annual carbon balance, we use k-means clustering analysis to group years, taking into account the following parameters: annual carbon balance (TCF), precipitation, temperature, deforestation (annual increment and accumulated), burned area (integrating all vegetation types)25, fire spots, Oceanic Niño Index26 (ONI) and TNA warming27. The cluster analysis identified the 15 years as belonging to three clusters: group 1 (2011, 2012, 2013, 2014, 2017 and 2018); group 2 (2019, 2020, 2021 and 2022); and group 3 (2010, 2015, 2016, 2023 and 2024). See the statistical analysis in Methods, Extended Data Fig. 4, Extended Data Tables 2 and 3, Supplementary Discussion 2 and Supplementary Table 1. Group 1 is characterized by wetter years and low human activities. Group 2 reflects a period with intense human activity—deforestation and degradation (biomass-burning, selective logging, mining and edge effects), and low climatic stress. Group 3 comprises years characterized by intense global climate events, such as El Niño and TNA events with associated biomass burning.

Group 1 (Fig. 2c, Extended Data Table 3a and Extended Data Fig. 5), representing the wettest years with low levels of human disturbance (deforestation and degradation), shows a near-neutral annual mean total carbon flux (TCF) of 0.11 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1 and ∆VP (VP – BKG) of 0.14 ppm, resulting from a carbon sink (−0.20 ± 0.12 PgC yr−1) that offsets 64% of fire emissions (0.32 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1). The annual deforestation and burned area were 6,389 km2 and 55,115 km2, respectively. This result indicates that Amazonia did not significantly contribute to the global carbon balance during wet years and with low human disturbance.

Group 2 years (Figs. 2c and 3, Extended Data Table 3b and Extended Data Fig. 5) were characterized by a period with higher levels of human disturbance and weak governance13. During this period, the annual deforestation rate rose by 97%, whereas the burned area increased by 25%, resulting in a TCF of 0.43 ± 0.09 PgC yr−1 and a ∆VP of 0.53 ppm, representing increases of 291% and 379% relative to group 1, respectively. The annual mean temperature was the same as in group 1, and precipitation was 134 mm (6%) lower (mainly due to the 2020 wet season), indicating low climatic stress during this period. In August 2020, a strong and prolonged La Niña event began and lasted until February 2023 (Fig. 4). During this period, carbon uptake gradually increased in the first quarter of each year (Fig. 4), offsetting an ever-greater proportion of anthropogenic emissions. As climate stress was very low during this period, it mainly represents the carbon emissions from human activity in Amazonia.

Group 3 (Extended Data Table 3c and Extended Data Fig. 5) comprises years characterized by intense global climate events, such as El Niño and TNA warming. The mean ONI and TNA values for group 3 were higher than those for groups 1 and 2. Any years from groups 1 and 2 presented El Niño and/or warming TNA events. The El Niño events occurred in 2015–2016 (strong–very strong) and 2023 (strong; Fig. 3c), and the warming TNA events occurred in 2010 (moderate) and 2023–2024 (moderate–strong). We used the same ONI classification criterion28 for the TNA events (Methods). We also observed in Fig. 2c that the group 3 vertical profile showed an increase in the free-troposphere layer, unlike the other two; this is due to convective transport induced by the fire. Both El Niño and TNA warming contribute to reduced precipitation and increased temperatures in Amazonia. On average, group 3 received 308 mm (13%) less annual precipitation than group 1, with 20% less during the wet-season peak (January–March), and 17% less during the dry-season peak (August–October). The annual mean, wet-season peak and dry-season peak temperatures were higher than those of group 1 by 0.5 °C, 0.4 °C and 0.8 °C, respectively. These drought and warming conditions in Amazonia increased the flammability of the forest. We observed a 92% increase in burned area25 compared with group 1, whereas deforestation21 was 22% higher. TCF was 0.49 ± 0.07 PgC y−1, and ∆VP was 0.61 ppm, both of which were 325% higher than Group 1. However, for this group, NBE was −0.06 ± 0.10 PgC y−1 (nearly neutral sink), indicating that the main source of carbon is not the forest itself, but, again, human influence that promotes fires. Furthermore, recent climatic conditions interfere with the consequences of an intense El Niño year on the forest, as can be observed in 2023 and 2024 (Extended Data Tables 2 and 3 and Extended Data Fig. 7). FCF was 0.55 ± 0.10 PgC yr−1, 72% higher than group 1 and 53% higher than group 2, showing that fire emissions are an important characteristic of group 3. This finding highlighted the importance of clarifying the origin of fires, whether natural or anthropogenic. Therefore, a study was conducted to identify the relationship between lightning and forest fires in group 3. During the years of this group, it was found that lightning-induced ignition for fire events represented 1.6% and 1.0%, respectively, for the years 2023 and 2024 (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 6), allowing us to conclude that the emissions from group 3 originated primarily from human-induced fires, which were amplified by drought conditions. The years 2023 and 2024 were chosen to ensure gathering a more accurate cloud-to-ground lightning dataset, as higher-quality and more reliable data are only available from 2017 onwards.

These findings underscore the dominant role of direct human disturbance in Amazonia (Fig. 5, a schematic grouping summary), driving year-to-year changes in carbon fluxes and highlighting the urgent need for mitigation to prevent the approach of the region to a climatic tipping point29,30. Climatic stress amplifies the effects of human-induced fire, because drought conditions substantially increase forest flammability1,9,10,11,13,15,31,32,33.

The 2023 wet season exhibited the largest carbon sink over our study period of 15 years (Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 7), which occurred after three consecutive La Niña years (Fig. 4). The 2023 Amazonia carbon balance was similar to that of group 1 (0.11 ± 0.09 PgC yr−1), because the wet-season sink offset 75% of the carbon emissions from deforestation and burned area, which were, respectively, 38% and 52% higher than in group 1. The annual mean temperature was similar to that of group 1, starting colder than normal and warming progressively as strong TNA and El Niño events became unprecedented drought and heat wave34,35 (Fig. 4).

While 2023 began after 3 years of La Niña, 2024 began in the middle of strong El Niño and TNA events, which made its outcome completely different. The year 2024 exhibited the highest carbon emissions (0.81 ± 0.09 PgC yr−1) in the 15-year record (Extended Data Tables 2 and 3 and Extended Data Fig. 7), 605% higher than that of group 1. It was also the hottest, driest and most strongly degraded by fire over the past two decades36. Annual temperatures were 1.2 °C higher than in group 1, and annual precipitation was 462 mm lower than in group 1. The burned area was 182% higher than in group 1, producing the most extensive burned area observed since 2010 in Amazonia (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Tables 2 and 3). Following the reinstatement of public policies to curb deforestation in 2023 (ref. 13), fire became a widespread tool for deforestation. In 2024, PRODES identified that 38% deforestation was caused by fire37. Most Amazonian wildfires are intentionally set, and severe droughts exacerbate their extent and intensity15,29,31,37.

The 15-year annual mean Amazonia carbon balance was 0.32 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1 (FCF, 0.40 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1; NBE, −0.08 ± 0.12 PgC yr−1). The pronounced interannual variability (−0.12 PgC yr−1 to 0.75 PgC yr−1) underscores the need for decadal-scale analyses to accurately define the role of Amazonia in the global carbon cycle, and it is also important to consider spatial representativeness, given the large variability across the subregions studied. Long-term deforestation and degradation have reduced annual precipitation by about 8% over the past 46 years and dry-season peak precipitation by about 20%, while increasing annual mean temperature by 1.13 ± 0.13 °C and dry-season temperature by 1.81 ± 0.18 °C. The grouping of years, in three distinct scenarios (Fig. 5), showed what Amazonia represents for the global carbon balance in each of these scenarios: (1) wet years, with low human activities (0.11 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1); (2) low climatic stress years with high human activities (0.43 ± 0.13 PgC yr−1); and (3) warm and dry years, associated with intense increase of fires, essentially human-caused (0.49 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1). It is important to note that groups 2 and 3 do not differ significantly in carbon emissions, and both are dominated by deforestation and biomass burning, respectively. It is also noteworthy that the NBE across all three groups is close to neutral, indicating that direct human activities are the main risks to the Amazon. Drastic fire reduction is therefore essential to avert a tipping point, especially as escalating climate stress amplifies human impacts, and vice versa. Based on the consequences of the very strong El Niño 2015–2016 in the southeast Amazon region, which has become an increasing source of carbon, similar consequences are expected after the strong El Niño and TNA events in 2023–2024 for Amazonia. These results highlight the urgent need to implement a zero-deforestation policy, strengthen controls on biomass burning and prioritize forest restoration programmes.

Here, we report CO2 and CO measurement results from the four Amazonian aircraft vertical profile sites of the CARBAM project38 (SAN: 2.86° S 54.95° W; ALF: 8.80° S 56.75° W; RBA: 9.38° S 67.62° W; in 2010–2012 for TAB: 5.96° S 70.06° W; and since 2013 for TEF: 3.39° S 65.60° W, and since 2022 for CZS: 7.00° S 72.50° W), detailed in refs. 1,13. The regions of influence for the four sites representing the northeast (SAN), southeast (ALF), southwest (RBA) and northwest Amazon region (TAB, 2010–2012; TEF, 2013–2021; CZS, 2022–2024) are shown in Extended Data Fig. 2c–h. Our samples were typically collected twice per month, totalling 1,017 vertical profiles over 15 years, in a descending spiral from 4,420 m to about 300 m a.s.l. In 2015, the vertical profile sampling was stopped in April at all sites and resumed in November at RBA. In 2016, vertical profiles were performed only at RBA and ALF. The vertical profiles were usually taken between 12:00 and 13:00 local time. Air samples were analysed by a non-dispersive infrared analyser for CO2 and by gas chromatography with HgO reduction detection for CO. The detailed analytical and sampling methods were presented in previous studies1,13.

We defined the Amazonia study area according to subregions from ref. 39 and biomes from ref. 40, in which the studied area in Amazonia was determined to include only the forest ecosystems subregions: Amazonia stricto sensu, Guianas and Gurupi, with a total area of 6,700,798 km2.

The annual mean VP for each site was calculated by first producing the monthly mean for each height and then the annual mean for each height. The annual mean ∆VP for each site and for Amazonia was calculated by subtracting the corresponding background (BKG) for each VP and each height (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Fig. 3). To calculate the Amazonia annual mean VP, we applied the same method used to obtain the Amazonia annual mean flux and balance. To scale to the whole of Amazonia, we divided it into three regions (see ref. 13 and Supplementary Fig. 5). To compose the VP for region 1 (SAN + ALF), we calculated the weighted mean CO2 concentration at each height, proportional to the area of each region. To calculate the VP for region 2 (RBA + TAB, 2010–2012; RBA + TEF, 2013–2021; RBA + CZS, 2022–2024), the same procedure was used as in region 1. And for VP of region 3, the remaining Amazonia area not covered by the regions of influence of the vertical profile, we used the same VP as region 2. To compose the VP for Amazonia, the weighted mean for each height CO2 concentration was calculated, considering the three regions for each height (Supplementary Fig. 5).

We used a column budget technique to estimate TCF, FCF and NBE, which consists of the difference between CO2 mole fraction measured in the vertical profile and the estimated background mole fraction41 (∆CO2), considering the travel time of air parcels along the trajectory from the coast to the site (equation (1)), following the methodology in refs. 1,13,42.

Background mole fractions were estimated according to the methodology described in ref. 41, using the geographical position of each air-mass back-trajectory from the HYSPLIT model43 when it intersects two virtual limits: (1) a latitude limit, from the Equator southwards at 30° W and (2) a line between two observation sites at Ragged Point, Barbados (RPB), and Ascension Island, the United Kingdom (ASC), from NOAA/GML. We used CO2 (ref. 44) and CO (ref. 45) concentrations from the NOAA/GML Carbon Cycle Cooperative Global Air Sampling Network to determine the background.

We calculated fire emissions according to the methods published in refs. 1,13. We used observed CO/CO2 ratios by site from 2010 to 2024 (CO/CO2, ppb CO per ppm CO2) from measured CO mole fractions. The CO/CO2 ratios were calculated from clearly identifiable plumes in the vertical profiles during the dry season along period study (39 VP ALF CO/CO2 = 60.7 ± 30.7 ppb ppm−1; 35 VP SAN CO/CO2 = 56.1 ± 20.4 ppb ppm−1; 44 VP RBA CO/CO2 = 67.7 ± 24.1 ppb ppm−1; and 19 VP TTC (TAB_TEF_CZS) CO/CO2 = 74.2 ± 29.1 ppb ppm−1), which represents how many ppb CO is emitted for each 1 ppm CO2 during burning process. To calculate CO2 from fire, we used the CO flux and subtracted it from natural CO emissions (equation (2)). After the step \({F}_{\mathrm{CO}}-{F}_{\mathrm{CO}}^{\mathrm{Natu}{\rm{r}}\mathrm{al}}\), we used the CO/CO2 ratio to estimate the amount of CO2 from fire. NBE represents all processes from natural sources and sinks and all anthropogenic non-fire emissions (or after fire, without CO emissions) (NBE = total − fire).

Flux uncertainties were estimated using Monte Carlo error propagation (1,000 iterations). CO2 measurement uncertainty was 0.03 ppm, and background (BKG) uncertainties were derived from residuals to the fitted CO2 smooth curve, according to ref. 1. Uncertainties in back-trajectory travel times13 were also included. For carbon fire emissions and NBE, uncertainties in CO/CO2 ratios, CO fluxes and a 25% uncertainty in natural CO emissions were considered. VP flux uncertainties were derived from the Monte Carlo ensemble. Monthly uncertainties were estimated using pooled standard deviation to account for intra-month variability within the annual mean flux, and period uncertainties were calculated using the quadratic mean.

An additional source of uncertainty is the limitation on sampling to 4.4 km a.s.l. Transit times of air parcels from the coast to sampling sites, based on HYSPLIT, can vary, with mean trajectory times of 2.6 days for SAN, 5.3 days for ALF, 6.6 days for RBA, 5.7 days for TAB, 5.0 days for TEF and 7.6 days for CZS. The convective process, together with long travel times of air masses, can represent loss of carbon sources and sink contributions from the surface, because our vertical profiles start at 4.4 km. Comparing the background concentration and the top of vertical profiles is one way to verify potential information loss1,13. According to the method we use, the flux is obtained by subtracting the measured CO2 mole fraction in the VP from the background concentration, accounting for the integration time (equation (1)). During the dry season, the loss of information is larger to positive (VP > BKG), because during the burning season (peak of the dry season), the top of VP starts with a higher CO2 concentration than BKG due to convective processes driven by biomass burning.

A previous study46 proposed a correction of our gas concentration measurements based on a comparison of laboratory analyses of samples collected on 12 vertical profiling flights above Manaus in 2017–2019. The in situ measurements were made with a cavity ring-down spectrometer (CRDS), and discrete flask samples (PFPs) collected on the same flights were analysed in the laboratory. Some of the continuous CRDS measurements differed from those determined in the laboratory from flask samples (PFP) collected on the same flights (Figure A4 in ref. 46). Based on linear regression of N = 158 paired CRDS-PFP measurements, ref. 46 reported that water vapour content explained only 13% of the variance in the CRDS-PFP differences (Figure A7 in ref. 46). Nevertheless, ref. 46 proposed applying this regression relationship to adjust all laboratory measurements. Applying such an adjustment would conflate multiple methodological differences between the two sampling and analytical systems. The regression in ref. 46 compares independent instruments sampling distinct air parcels under different pressures, flow rates and materials. The proposed correction assumes that the in situ CRDS measurements are accurate, that the laboratory analyses are biased, that discrepancies arise solely from water vapour, and that the regression relationship can be extrapolated to approximately 13,319 laboratory measurements collected over 15 years. For these reasons, the proposed adjustment was not applied in this study. LaGEE measurements used in this study are internally consistent across the full temporal and spatial domain.

An additional source of uncertainty is addressed in the calculation of fire carbon emissions and NBE. Over time, the forest is becoming drier and hotter. These changes promote a lower CO/CO2 ratio. The use of a single mean CO/CO2 ratio is another source of uncertainty, leading to lower fire emissions than those observed in recent years because the ratio tends to decline over time. It is likely that carbon emissions from fires were higher than our averages for 2023 and 2024 indicate, because both were drought years.

We used the missForest algorithm to impute missing monthly TCF and FCF data at the ALF, SAN, RBA and TAB_TEF_CZS (TTC) sites. These gaps resulted from sampling and logistical issues in the laboratory. The missForest method is a non-parametric imputation approach based on random forests47,48, and we implemented it in R language49 through the missForest package50. To estimate missing values for TCF and FCF1,13, we included the following monthly variables in the imputation process: temperature (T), precipitation (P), vapour pressure deficit (VPD), ONI, TNA index, EVI, burned area (BA) and fire spots (FS). The missing data gaps were imputed using average values calculated from 1,000 runs of missForest. The normalized root mean square errors obtained for FCTotal and FCFire were 0.0014 and 0.0012 for ALF, 0.0011 and 0.0010 for RBA, 0.0055 and 0.0053 for SAN and 0.0018 and 0.0016 for TTC, respectively. NBE missing data were calculated by subtracting the fire from TCF.

We define regions of influence as those areas covered by the density of back-trajectories51. We updated the calculations for the regions of influence used in our published CO2 papers1. In this study, the regions received two treatment steps. In the first step, we calculated back trajectories for altitudes lower than 1,300 m. As we have only four heights below 1,300 m, the density of trajectories could be reduced, compromising the representativeness. To better characterize atmospheric circulation over the quarters and years, we calculated trajectories at each sampling height (>1,300 m) twice a week, at the same locations and time of day, over the 15 years for each VP sampling site. A second treatment in the grid cells was done to increase the weight for the grid cells near the sample site. We normalized the weight for the region of influence to be from 0 to 1. First, the grid with a value of 1 was calculated by disregarding 2.5% of the grids with the highest values, thereby increasing the area of the grid region with a value of 1. It was considered a regular grid of 1° × 1° generated in the WGS 84 georeference system (EPSG:4326).

We used the quarterly regions of influence maps as spatial weighting functions for all studied parameters to determine how each parameter influenced the carbon flux and for the missing data imputation process (missForest), following ref. 4. We used the GPCP database (http://eagle1.umd.edu/GPCP_ICDR/GPCP_Monthly.html), v.1.3, for precipitation analysis (described in refs. 52,53,54), which contains daily data at a 1° × 1° latitude–longitude resolution. We used 2-m temperatures from ERA-5 that are monthly means of daily means since 1959, and which were used with a resolution of 0.25° × 0.25° latitude–longitude, obtained from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF)27,55. The VPD product is a measure of the indirect vapour pressure deficit in kPa (resolution of 2.5 arcmin) of monthly means of temperature and humidity, provided by the Climatic Research Unit Ts4.0 (ref. 56). The dataset was resampled to a 1° × 1° spatial resolution using the monthly mean. The EVI is a vegetation index that aims to estimate the fraction of photosynthetically active radiation (fPAR) from terrestrial vegetation. The EVI product used57 was the MANVI: Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) multiangle implementation of atmospheric correction nadir-solar (see the methods in ref. 13).

Two products of burned area (BA) were used: (1) for all annual burned areas, the BA from MapBiomas was used, integrating all types of vegetation that were burned (primary and secondary forest, pasture, agriculture and others), from the MapBiomas Fire Collection 4 (ref. 25; https://brasil.mapbiomas.org). MapBiomas Fire was validated with a global accuracy of 89% (ref. 33); (2) for monthly BA (used only for missForest calculation), MODIS products were used: MODIS Collection 6 MCD64A1 burned area product13,58.

The procedures to retrieve deforestation as geographic data built by PRODES/INPE21 based on a historical series of Landsat images provide deforestation annual increments in the Brazilian Amazon. Detailed information on the PRODES methodology is available and can be accessed at http://urlib.net/rep/8JMKD3MGP3W34R/443H3RE (ref. 59). The dataset accuracy is 93.5 (ref. 60). We used the data period from 2010 to 2024. Using QGIS software, a 0.25° × 0.25° grid was generated for the entire Brazilian Amazon, which was filled with the absolute values of deforested area calculated for each cell and each year of the series. The mean deforestation area was calculated for the period 2010–2024 within each grid cell. Annual deforestation data correspond to the deforested area mapped by the PRODES project in a given year for Amazon forests.

Monthly deforestation and degradation data correspond to either the deforestation alerts (clear cut, deforestation with vegetation, and mining) or degradation alerts (ordered selective logging, disordered selective logging, burns and degradation) captured by the DETER61 Amazon system in a given month. This dataset is produced daily by the visual interpretation of medium spatial resolution optical imagery of National Institute for Space Research (INPE) and, although not a full estimate of deforestation and degradation in the Amazon, can indicate variations in deforestation patterns over time33,62. We used the raw version of the dataset provided by INPE that included both password-protected data and data collected from August 2015.

Fire spots in Pan-Amazonia between 2010 and 2024 and burned area in Brazilian Amazon were retrieved from the BDQueimadas wildfire monitoring program of INPE63 (see the methods in ref. 13).

Warm (El Niño) and cold (La Niña) periods based on a threshold of ±0.5 °C for the ONI, 3-month running mean of ERSST v.5 SST anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region (5° N–5° S, 120° W–170° W), based on centred 30-year base periods updated every 5 years (ref. 26). To analyse the intensity of the events, we used the ONI classification anomaly28—for El Niño events, weak (0.5–0.9 °C), moderate (1.0–1.4 °C), strong (1.5–1.9 °C), and very strong (≥2.0 °C).

The TNA index was computed as the mean sea-surface temperature (SST) anomaly over the region 23.5° N–5.5° N and 57.5° W–15° W (ref. 64). Monthly SST fields were obtained from ERA5 (0.25°), and anomalies were derived relative to a 30-year climatological baseline (1971–2000), consistent with standard TNA calculations27. We used the same ONI anomaly classification to analyse the intensity of the TNA warming events.

Annual data (2010–2024) for the Amazon included TCF, precipitation (P), temperature (T), ONI, TNA index, fire spots (FS), burned area (BA), annual deforestation (DEF) and accumulated deforestation (DEF_acc). Variable distributions and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests (Supplementary Table 1), respectively. Although most variables met normality assumptions, robust statistical approaches were adopted because of the small sample size (n = 15) and potential deviations from Gaussian behaviour typical of environmental datasets (Supplementary Discussion 3). Principal component analysis was applied to standardized data, with the first two components considered to represent the dominant variability. The k-means clustering (k = 3) was performed in the principal component analysis space, with cluster characteristics summarized in a standardized heatmap. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the average silhouette width (Extended Data Fig. 4).

Group differences among clusters were assessed using Welch’s analysis of variance (Supplementary Table 1), which is robust to heteroscedasticity and unequal sample sizes. When significant differences were detected (P < 0.05), pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Games–Howell post hoc test (Supplementary Table 1), which does not assume equal variances and appropriately controls the type I error rate under these conditions. This approach provides a consistent and reliable framework for identifying differences among clusters in environmental variables (Supplementary Discussion 2). All analyses were conducted in R language49.

We obtained annual estimates of pasture, soybean cropland and mining area across Amazonia from the MapBiomas Collection 1024 dataset (30 m spatial resolution). Annual land-cover maps were used to isolate class-specific pixels (pasture, soybean and mining) and to compute their corresponding areas (km2) within the Amazonia carbon study area. This approach provided a consistent time series of land-use expansion and persistence for 2000–2024.

After 2017, in group 3, the years with accurate data were 2023 and 2024, when a more accurate cloud-to-ground lightning dataset was available. We used cloud-to-ground strokes from the Earth Networks Total Lightning Network (ENTLN)65. In South America, particularly in Amazonia, ENTLN lacks a dense sensor network comparable to that in other regions of Brazil. Even so, it operates with a stroke detection efficiency above 50% (corresponding to a flash detection efficiency above 80%) and a mean location accuracy below 1 km. Just for reference, in regions with high sensor densities, after the 2021 upgrade, the ENTLN reaches cloud-to-ground flash detection efficiency levels above 95% and median location accuracy below 100 m (ref. 66). Although performance depends on regional sensor coverage, ENTLN is considered one of the most advanced and reliable lightning networks globally67. Active fires used in this study were provided by the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite on the Suomi National Polar-Orbiting Partnership satellite, with a spatial resolution of 375 m. The Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite 375 m active fire product provides geolocation accuracy of the order of one pixel (about 375 m), with typical sub-pixel geolocation uncertainties (about 70 m), ensuring reliable spatial representation of fire locations. All overpasses, day and night, were used from the Wildfire Monitoring Program of the INPE, available at http://www.inpe.br/queimadas/ (ref. 63). To assess lightning-associated wildfire among all cloud-to-ground lightning strokes, we identified probable lightning candidates that occurred up to 72 h (3 days) before active fire detection, using a fixed buffer radius of 1 km around each active fire and accounting for the location accuracy of both datasets. A detailed description of lightning candidate characteristics in Brazil is provided in ref. 68.

#3480. Cool Ice Cube Experiments for Kids

Beat the heat or embrace the cold with my collection of cool ice cube experiments. From exciting colour changes to gravity defying tricks and icy engineering challenges, get ready for some surprisingly simple, hands-on science that’s sure to make your summer cooler.

You only need ice and salt for most of these investigations, making them fun and inexpensive!

Find out how to lift ice cubes with string ( and a little salt ). This is a brilliantly visual science trick that always looks impressive.

Use ice cubes and salt to make ice cream from milk! This tasty science experiment uses salt to lower the freezing point of water, drawing heat from the surroundings ( the milk ) to do it.

When salt is added to the ice in the outer bag, the ice starts to melt. Melting needs energy, which in this case comes from the milk mixture in the inner bag. Heat energy is absorbed from the milk, causing ice crystals to form, which then turn the milk into ice cream.

Ice decorations are fun to make in winter or summer. Children can hang these from trees or around a garden and observe how long it takes for them to melt. We like to use shaped ice cube trays and add small petals and flowers to decorate them.

Set up an ice excavation. Ice excavations can be made to any theme, and allow children to experiment with warm water, cold water and salt to try to melt the ice.

If the weather is very cold, you can leave water in a tray out overnight to see if it freezes!

Use ice cubes to make frost appear on the outside of a tin can. This activity visually demonstrates to children how adding salt to an ice and water mixture lowers the temperature of the water.

Pop an ice cube in salty water and an ice cube in freshwater to see which melts the fastest.

Building a tower from slippery ice cubes might sound impossible, but if you leave the ice cubes to melt a little and add a sprinkle of salt to each one. Salt makes the water melt, then refreeze, sticking the ice cubes together!

From simple melting ice to impressive towers, these ice cube experiments prove that you don’t need fancy equipment for exciting science experiments!

Keep kids engaged and cool while learning about fundamental scientific principles, all from the comfort of the garden!

Im Sudan haben sich die Kämpfe zwischen der sudanesischen Armee (SAF) und der paramilitärischen Miliz Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in Nord-Kordofan und Teilen des Bundesstaates Blauer Nil verschärft. Tausende Menschen mussten fliehen, zu einer Zeit, in der die Bauern ihre Felder bestellen.

Die reguläre sudanesische Armee kontrolliert den Norden und das Zentrum des Landes einschließlich der Hauptstadt Khartum. Die RSF sind vor allem in der westlichen Region Darfur und Teilen des Südens präsent.

#3489. Easy Ideas for a School Gardening Club

A school gardening club is a great way to get kids outside while learning about plants and nature. There’s nothing better than picking fruit or vegetables you’ve grown yourself, and the experience is a unique educational opportunity. A school gardening club is more than just planting seeds, it cultivates curiosity, patience, teamwork and provides a valuable insight into where our food comes from. Children can get a real seed to kitchen table experience, all while spending time outdoors learning in nature.

Gardening club isn’t just for summer, either. Kids can grow plants all year round, as well as think about how to protect them from pests and the nutrients they need to grow successfully.

Planting and growing fruit and vegetables is the obvious garden club activity, but there’s so much more that children can do in the garden.

The taxonomy and phylogeny of late Middle Pleistocene hominins in East Asia remain highly debated1. Xujiayao, a key representative assemblage with many fossils and a clear stratigraphy dating to around 199–138 thousand years ago, has been extensively studied, but its evolutionary position remains uncertain. Here, to clarify this, we analysed Xujiayao material through both palaeoproteomic analysis and morphological comparisons with other East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo. Proteomic analysis of two specimens identified 18 endogenous proteins, and the Denisova-specific COL1A2-996 R>K mutation strongly supported their affiliation with the Denisovan lineage. Extended analysis identifies a set of shared morphological traits between Denisovan fossils (Xujiayao and Harbin) and other East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo (such as Jinniushan, Dali and Hualongdong), indicating that they all belong to the Denisovan lineage. This analysis provides a comprehensive view of Denisovan features, showing both traits reminiscent of Homo erectus (such as a thick cranial vault) and those characteristic of modern humans (for example, a strongly arched temporal squama). They also share features with late Neanderthals, such as the structure of the temporal labyrinth and the presence of a suprainiac fossa. Notably, Denisovans are distinctive, with a low cranial profile with near-parallel lateral walls, a massive supraorbital torus, a flat zygomaxillary region and weak prognathism.

Late Middle Pleistocene Homo systematics in eastern Eurasia remain poorly defined compared with the better-established evolutionary scenarios in western Eurasia, with East Asian hominins variously classified as archaic Homo sapiens, archaic Homo, China archaics or archaic humans2,3,4,5. Recent studies have revealed greater morphological diversity in this group than previously known, prompting their assignment to distinct species1. Homo longi, defined by the Harbin cranium (about 309–146 thousand years ago (ka)) and now classified as Denisovan, is characterized by a massive, long, low cranium; a low, flat face with a shallow canine fossa, reduced alveolar prognathism; a relatively rounded occipital contour with weak torus; a robust supraorbital torus overhanging near-square orbits; and a large, forwardly and inwardly inclined mastoid process, clustering phylogenetically with Jinniushan, Dali, Hualongdong and Xiahe6,7,8,9. However, despite some primitive traits, Hualongdong has been designated as a transitional form from archaic hominins to H. sapiens on the basis of derived facial morphologies, mainly including a small size, flattened malar region, weak prognathism, a well-developed anterior nasal spine and a parabolic dental arcade with an anteriorly displaced incisive foramen10. Another lineage, characterized by an exceptionally large cranium, has been proposed as Homo juluensis, encompassing Xujiayao/Xuchang crania and the Denisovan-affiliated Xiahe and Penghu mandibles, identified through palaeoproteomic analyses1,11,12. Beyond the Denisovan hypothesis, competing phylogenetic hypotheses proposed these hominins as ancestral to either H. sapiens or another sister group, further obscuring the evolutionary scenarios in late Middle Pleistocene eastern Eurasia6,13,14.

The Xujiayao assemblage, dated to around 199–138 ka through combined uranium series and electron-spin-resonance methods15, comprises 20 hominin cranial, mandibular and dental fossils excavated between 1976 and 1979 from a well-defined stratigraphic context in the Nihewan Basin, northern China16,17. This assemblage, recovered alongside abundant artifacts (dominated by scrapers and spheroids) and faunal remains, represents one of the most extensive collections among East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo18,19 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Text 1). Initial analyses identified a thick cranial vault, large cerebral fossa relative to the cerebellar fossa, moderate occipital torus, an anterior nasal spine, reduced inion–endinion distance and Neanderthal-like dental morphology, leading to classification as Homo sapiens neanderthalensis, early H. sapiens or archaic H. sapiens3,16,17. To circumvent taxonomic disputes when sapiens lacks consensus, terms such as archaic Homo were temporarily adopted4. Recent analysis of Xujiayao 6, comprising parietal, temporal and occipital bones, revealed an exceptionally large endocranial capacity of 1,700 ml, designating Xujiayao as the holotype of H. juluensis20,21. Shared dental traits between Xujiayao and Altai Denisovans, including large crown size, complex occlusal surface and highly divergent molar roots, have been proposed to support their taxonomic affinity22. Altai Denisovans and Xujiayao were then grouped under H. juluensis, alongside the Penghu and Xiahe mandibles, Xuchang crania and the Tam Ngu Hao 2 tooth due to their close morphological affinity1,23. This lineage is thought to be distinct from H. longi (Harbin, Dali and Jinniushan), archaic Homo (Hualongdong) and a potential Neanderthal-like Maba lineage1. As an abundant fossil assemblage with a clear stratigraphic context and linked to nearly all classifications of East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo, a confirmed taxonomy of Xujiayao would be critical to resolving the evolutionary relationships of other Middle Pleistocene Homo in East Asia through palaeoproteomic analysis and morphological comparisons.

Here we implemented a palaeoproteomic analysis to explore Xujiayao’s taxonomic classification and assess its genetic population affinity. Subsequently, we conducted a systematic morphological comparison between Xujiayao and other members of East Asian late Middle Pleistocene within a broader evolutionary framework of genus Homo to investigate their morphological affinities to the Xujiayao population. The palaeoproteomic results revealed Xujiayao hominins as Denisovans, and their morphological similarities with other East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo (such as Jinniushan, Dali and Hualongdong) indicate that they all belong to the Denisovan lineage.

Molecular evidence was sought to determine the phylogenetic placement of the Xujiayao hominin samples, on the basis of the positive result of animal samples from the same site (Supplementary Text 2). Ancient proteins were successfully extracted from two bone fragments, including one right parietal bone (PA1490, XJY-6) and one mandible (right ramus, PA1497, XJY-14). To maximize protein yields, we extracted proteins from bone samples by sequential fractionation, resulting in one acid-soluble fraction (>3 kDa) and two acid-insoluble fractions for each sample (Methods and Supplementary Table 1). The preservation of peptides in both Xujiayao hominin samples was first confirmed through the identification of collagen peptide markers24,25,26 in matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (Supplementary Text 2), and the proteomic data were then further analysed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis.

To obtain protein sequences from the Xujiayao hominin samples, LC–MS/MS data of the samples were searched on PEAKS Online against the Harbin database8, which includes bone proteins of published archaic individuals and the proteome of present-day humans. The PEAKS search yielded 102,029 peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) and 15,194 unique peptides across the two samples (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). We also searched the data on MaxQuant for confirmation, and the search yielded 3,526 unique peptides across the 2 samples (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To differentiate endogenous ancient proteins from modern contamination, Gln and Asn deamination characteristics of ancient proteins were calculated for each protein. Proteins with neither Gln nor Asn residues were excluded from the analyses, as the deamination rates of those proteins cannot be assessed. For proteins that contain at least two peptides with Gln or Asn, we calculated deamidation rates for 41 proteins containing both or one of those Gln/Asn residues. Similar to ancient proteins obtained from previous studies12,27,28, the majority of collagens showed a high level of deamidation rates (PA1490: Asn, 25.0–94.7%; and Gln, 33.3–100.0% (Supplementary Fig. 3a); PA1497: Asn, 20.0–89.4%; and Gln, 22.2–87.5%, except for COL14A1 (Supplementary Fig. 3b); in PEAKS results, the same hereafter), and the most common collagens in ancient bones (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL2A1, COL5A1 and COL5A2) had much higher deamidation rates (PA1490: Asn, 61.4–94.7%, and Gln, 44.9–92.6%; PA1497: Asn, 62.6–89.4%, and Gln, 49.3–86.1%). By contrast, typical contaminants such as keratins and trypsin showed a low level of deamidation (PA1490: Asn, 0.0–5.3%; and Gln, 0.0–7.7% (Supplementary Fig. 3a); PA1497: Asn, 0.0–5.6%, and Gln, 0.0–10.0% (Supplementary Fig. 3b)). As a result, we obtained 31 proteins with elevated deamidation rates for PA1497, and 20 for PA1490. We further filtered out peptides that cannot be confidently assigned to a single gene (Supplementary Table 3), peptides identified in both the Xujiayao hominin samples and the extraction blanks, and proteins that could not be confirmed by MaxQuant, resulting in 18 endogenous ancient proteins for downstream analyses (13 for PA1490 and 15 for PA1497; Supplementary Table 4).

Preliminary MALDI-TOF spectral analysis and LC–MS/MS data searching against the Mammalia type I collagen database indicated that the Xujiayao hominin samples belong to the genus Homo (Supplementary Text 2); we therefore further used single amino acid polymorphism (SAP) assessment to establish a higher-resolution taxonomic classification. Of the SAPs that we obtained from the Xujiayao hominin samples using both software programs that were assigned to genera within Hominidae, the ancestral type was present at all non-Homo derived positions, confirming that the Xujiayao samples belong to Homo (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Among the SAPs within Homo, COL1A2-996K is reliably identified (Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Text 3). This variant is specific to all the Denisovan individuals with proteomic or genomic evidence, including the two Xiahe individuals12,28, the Penghu 1 individual11, the Harbin individual8 and Denisova3 (ref. 29), while Neanderthals and modern humans contain an arginine at this site. We further validated this SAP in the Xujiayao hominin samples using spectral data quality and deamidation rates. Abundant peptides with high-quality spectra (Fig. 3a,d and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6) containing this SAP were observed in both Xujiayao hominin samples (57 peptides, 207 PSMs for PA1490; and 61 peptides, 244 PSMs for PA1497), with very few peptides identified by low-quality spectra showing an arginine there (1 peptide, 1 PSM for PA1490; and 1 peptide, 5 PSMs for PA1497). Moreover, increased deamidation rates of residue Asn977 or Gln1002 in COL1A2-996K-containing peptides were observed (Asn977: 69.2% in PA1490, and 69.4% in PA1497; Gln1002: 100.0% in both), confirming the endogenous origin of these peptides. With no new SAP reliably identified, we therefore determine the Xujiayao hominin samples to be from a Denisovan population.

To confirm the phylogenetic placement for the Xujiayao hominin samples, we reconstructed a Bayesian phylogenetic tree from the concatenated consensus sequences of 2,074 amino acid positions overlapping between the PEAKS and MaxQuant results (with at least 2 PSMs coverage in each software) from 8 endogenous proteins, which is 16.0% of the total protein sequence alignment (Supplementary Data 1). The protein sequences of a set of selected representative individuals from the Harbin database were used to build the phylogenetic tree (Methods). The phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3c) exhibited topological congruence with those established by genomic29,30,31,32 and palaeoproteomic studies8,11,12. Although incongruence might occur when using different datasets due to the relatively modest amount of proteomic data and the sequence variation among modern human population (Supplementary Text 4), the Xujiayao hominin samples, Denisova3 and other Denisovan-related individuals always formed a monophyletic group with a high posterior probability (>99%). However, within the Denisovan clade, different individuals formed unstable topology with low posterior probabilities (<50%; Supplementary Fig. 7) as no more SAPs were shared besides COL1A2-996K (Supplementary Text 5); we can therefore only determine the Xujiayao hominins to belong to a late Middle Pleistocene East Asian Denisovan population, without further grouping.

With Xujiayao classified as Denisovan, the preserved cranial, mandibular and dental materials, incorporating the newly identified Harbin Denisovan8,9, enable a detailed morphological characterization of the Denisovan lineage (Supplementary Figs. 8–10 and Supplementary Text 6). Xujiayao hominins exhibit erectus-like features, including a low and wide neurocranium (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 11), a thick cranial vault (bregma, 7.7 mm; parietal tubercle, 12.0 mm) (Supplementary Fig. 11), a weak parasagittal depression (not in Harbin), a short (large in Harbin) and inwardly inflexed mastoid process, an asymmetrical mandibular notch (with the crest attached to the lateral third of the condyle), an open mandibular foramen, and M2, M3 and M3 complete main cusps (where the superscript and subscript values denote upper jaw and lower jaw molars, respectively)6,18,33,34. Denisovans also display a group of derived features shared with some later Homo species (African and European Homo heidelbergensis, Neanderthals and modern humans): a strongly arched temporal squama, a moderately developed suprameatal spine and a more symmetrical crown outline with a longer buccal cusp compared with the lingual cusp, reduced buccal contour protrusion and a simplified occlusal surface lacking the bifurcated buccal essential crest in P3 (upper third premolar)35,36,37.

Notably, the Xujiayao Denisovan displays several metric and non-metric features that are different compared with most H. erectus and modern humans (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 8–10). These include a low cranial profile with near-parallel or slightly convergent lateral walls, a nearly vertical occipital plane (with a larger occipital angle than in H. erectus; Supplementary Fig. 11) but a non-rounded occipital bone, a non-coronally oriented tympanic plate deflected to the petrosal (also present in late Neanderthals), a weak angular torus and a moderate linear occipital torus (commonly moderate in late Neanderthals but weak in Harbin)6,18. In these features, the Xujiayao Denisovan is generally comparable to African and European H. heidelbergensis and early Neanderthals35,36,37,38. Although retaining the moderate or pronounced labial convexity and pronounced shovelling as in H. erectus, the finger-like prolongations in the lingual side of the upper central incisor, elevation degree of the lingual central ridge and cuspule-like marginal ridge in the upper canines tend to be simplified in Denisovans. The Denisovan mandibles represented by those from Xujiayao, Xiahe and Penghu display a distinct depression within the mandibular planum triangulare, a feature that is uncommon in other members of the genus Homo12,34,39 (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 9).

After defining the Denisovan morphology using the Xujiayao and Harbin fossils, we investigated whether this morphological suite is consistent with that of other East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 8). As in Xujiayao and Harbin, the other members of East Asian late Middle Pleistocene also exhibit erectus-like features (for example, parietal sagittal keeling and a weak parasagittal depression), derived characteristics (such as pronouncedly or strongly arched temporal squama) and a group of features that differ from both H. erectus and modern humans (such as a low cranial profile with near-parallel or slightly convergent lateral walls, a weak angular torus and a moderate linear occipital torus)2,6,13,40 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Xujiayao’s mandibular features, including an asymmetrical mandibular notch, open mandibular foramen, superior medial pterygoid tubercle, planum triangulare depression and gonial eversion, are fully present in Penghu and partially so in Xiahe and Hualongdong12,13,34,39. This unique combination of morphological traits, distinct from H. erectus, modern humans and Neanderthal-lineage hominins, supports the assignment of most members of East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo as Denisovans, reinforced by shared cranial traits with Harbin and mandibular traits with Penghu, confirmed as Denisovan by ancient DNA analysis and/or palaeoproteomics8,9,11,12. This suite of morphology does not appear simultaneously in individual specimens but manifests in different forms or combinations across various specimens. The inclusion of additional members of East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo, alongside Xujiayao, expands the morphological variability of East Asian Denisovans.

Denisovan morphology has been characterized through comparisons with H. erectus sensu stricto, African and European H. heidelbergensis and modern humans. As sister groups sharing a common ancestor, Denisovans and Neanderthals warrant detailed comparison to elucidate both shared and distinct features between them and clarify their morphological divergence (Supplementary Figs. 8–10). Denisovans share some features with Neanderthals: a low placement of the posterior canal in temporal labyrinth configuration (present in Xujiayao and Xuchang, but not in early Neanderthals), a suprainiac fossa (present in Xuchang; in early Neanderthals it appears as a rough and porous area), a retromolar space (present in Xujiayao and Xiahe) and a superior medial pterygoid tubercle (present in Xujiayao, Penghu and Hualongdong)2,12,13,34,39,41,42,43,44,45,46,47.

However, East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo differs from Neanderthals in several aspects. The erectus-like features (parasagittal depression, parietal sagittal keeling) are generally absent in early and late Neanderthals. M2, M3 and M3 of Xujiayao and most other members display complete main cusps, different from a number of Neanderthal specimens that lack the last-formed cusp48. Other features that are typical in early and/or late Neanderthals, including an occipital bun, a rounded cranial profile in the posterior view, a mandibular notch crest attaching to the middle third of the condyle, a bridged mandibular foramen and an inverted gonial angle, are generally absent in East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo12,13,34,35,39,43,46. The typical Neanderthal-like dental morphologies (for example, extremely convex labial surface of incisor I1, cuspule-like tuberculum dentale of upper canine, transverse crest of M1 and middle trigonid crest of lower molars) are rarely seen in East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo48.

Molecular phylogeny and shared morphological traits classify Xujiayao, initially assigned to different lineages, as Denisovan. The abundant Xujiayao remains, comprising a large part of the skull, offer insights into the physical appearance of Denisovans through a suite of morphologies instead of less direct characterizations through molecular and fragmentary fossil evidence. Dated at around 199–138 ka, Xujiayao is among the earliest molecularly confirmed Denisovans and approximately coeval with the early confirmed Denisovans from northeastern China and southern Siberia8,29,49.

This consistent suite of morphologies has been identified among East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo, supporting their classification as Denisovans. Xujiayao and Xuchang were previously assigned to a single lineage (H. juluensis), distinct from the groups of Dali, Harbin, Jinniushan and Hualongdong1. However, the main difference in endocranial capacity between H. juluensis (1,700–1,800 ml) and other members of East Asian late Middle Pleistocene Homo (1,120–1,420 ml) falls within the documented variation range of modern humans (1,090–1,880 ml), and also is approximately compatible with the spectrum of Neanderthals (1,065–1,740 ml)6,20,50. This variation in endocranial capacity should therefore be considered an intrapopulation variation, especially considering these two groups shared a unique combination of plesiomorphic, derived and transitional features that distinguish them from other members of the genus Homo. If taking into consideration the facial bones of Harbin (confirmed as Denisovan by molecular analysis8,9), Jinniushan, Hualongdong and Dali, we will have a nearly complete picture of the cranial morphology of Denisovans. They exhibit a low and sloping forehead, massive supraorbital torus curved backward, pronounced but not continuous supratoral sulcus, square or obliquely rectangle-like orbit, flat or moderately elevated nasal saddle, broad inferior nasal aperture, weak anterior nasal spine and subnasal fossa, coronally flat zygomaxillary region lacking obvious midfacial pneumatization and weak prognathism (also matched with the genetically predicted phenotypes)6,10,13,40,51.

Defined initially by DNA from remains in Siberia29,52, Denisovans appear to have occupied an extensive geographical range across East Asia, represented by Xiahe and Harbin in the north and Penghu in the south. If both morphological and molecular evidence were considered, Denisovans could have lived in East Asia from at least 300 ka to 45 ka, parallelling the temporal range of Siberian Denisovans (250–55 ka)13,28,53,54. The inclusion of the isolated lower molar from Cobra Cave of northern Laos, assigned to Denisovan due to its morphological similarity to Xiahe23, further supports Denisovans as a broadly distributed Homo lineage that adapted to diverse ecological zones, including the high-altitude plateau, subtropical forest and insular environments, across Siberia, East Asia and Southeast Asia. Sporadic dispersal of modern humans into southern China (for example, Zhiren and Daoxian) might not have substantially interfered with the living niches of Denisovans until a major migration around 40 ka replaced them (with partial Denisovan introgression into modern humans), forming the basal population of regional living populations55,56,57.

The classification of Xujiayao and most East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo as Denisovan, which is a sister group to Neanderthals29,30,31,32, provides insights into the morphological divergence of these two lineages. Early and late Neanderthals form a single lineage based on a series of apomorphic traits35,45,48, but differ in such aspects as the cranial profile, mastoid process orientation, mandibular ramus breadth, suprainiac fossa form and the mandibular gonial angle. Molecular studies revealed that the late Neanderthal’s Y chromosome and mitochondrial DNA possibly were replaced with the introgression of the modern human population in the early Late Pleistocene58,59. Alternatively, late Neanderthals may have originated from a unilineal modern human expansion into Europe 400–250 ka, accompanied by substantial introgression of local archaic genes into the autosomes60. Except for the configuration of the temporal labyrinth and indented suprainiac fossa2,41, East Asian late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo aligns more closely with early Neanderthals, as well as African and European H. heidelbergensis. They share a low cranial profile with nearly parallel or slightly convergent lateral walls, wide mandibular rami, and more primitive features, such as short and inwardly inflexed mastoid process, parietal sagittal keeling, and parasagittal depression or flattening. Taken together, these findings suggest that modern humans or local archaic hominin introgression not only altered the genetic structure but also influenced the cranial morphology of later Neanderthals. This might explain the substantial morphological divergence between Denisovans and late Neanderthals.

Proteins were extracted from bone powder of Xujiayao hominin samples and animal samples from the same layer (Supplementary Tables 1 and 9) using previously published methods61,62. All sample preparations were performed using personal protective equipment in the dedicated clean room for ancient protein analysis in the Molecular Paleontology Laboratory, Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. For the hominin samples, a minimal-impact sampling was conducted, which did not adversely affect macroscopic morphology analysis. A small hole in each sample was drilled to collect the bone powder, while protecting surrounding areas with aluminium foil during the drilling procedures (Supplementary Fig. 12). The holes were kept as small as possible to avoid affecting important morphological features. The 3D scan was done before protein extraction to preserve the complete morphological structure.

Samples of drilled hominin bone powder were demineralized in 0.6 M HCl and then separated. After centrifugation, the supernatant was ultrafiltered through an Amicon Ultra-4 (3 kDa) centrifugal filter unit. After washing the filtration units with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution (ABC), the filtered proteins were dissolved in ABC and referred to as the acid-soluble fraction (3 kDa). The acid-insoluble pellet was washed with ultrapure water until neutral and then incubated with 50 mM ABC at 65 °C for 3 h. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected as acid-insoluble fraction 1 (pellet). The new acid-insoluble pellet was rinsed in the same way as the first pellet, and then incubated with 50 mM ABC at 65°C for 3 h. The supernatant with pellet was regarded as acid-insoluble fraction 2 (pellet 2). For the hominin samples, three fractions were collected for further digestion and mass spectrometry analysis, and, for the animal samples, only the conventional acid-insoluble fraction 1 (pellet) fraction was used.

All of these fractions were digested with 3 μl of 0.4 μg μl−1 porcine trypsin (Promega) overnight at 37 °C, then stopped by adding trifluoroacetic acid to a final concentration of 0.1%. Peptides were desalted and eluted using Pierce C18 Pipette Tips (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A small aliquot of the elution was used for MALDI-TOF analysis. The other elution of the hominin samples was dried for the LC–MS/MS analysis. For PA1490, neither the ‘pellet’ nor the ‘pellet 2’ elution showed an obvious collagen signal, so they were combined for further LC–MS/MS analysis. Extraction blanks following the identical procedure were used to estimate protein contamination during protein extraction and analysis. A bone sample from a modern camel (Camelus bactrianus) was also extracted concurrently with the samples as a positive control.

For preliminary collagen assessment, the peptides were screened on MALDI-TOF MS after protein digestion and peptide purification. All samples were analysed in triplicate on the Bruker autoflex maX MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer. Specifically, 1 μl of sample was spotted onto an MTP384 Bruker ground-steel MALDI target plate, and 1 μl of α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid matrix solution (1% in 50% ACN/0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (v/v/v)) was added to and mixed with the sample. Mass spectra were obtained over 700–3,500 m/z, and the raw files were then processed using mMass (v.5.5.0)63. Peak picking parameters in mMass were S/N threshold=3.0, Abs. intensity threshold=0.0, Rel. intensity threshold=0.5%, and picking height=90(%). Baseline, smoothing and deisotoping were applied and no shoulder peaks were removed.

The LC–MS/MS experiments were performed in two independent laboratories. For the LC–MS/MS analysis at Capital Medical University (labelled as SY), the eluted peptides from the bone powder samples were analysed under DDA mode on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Easy nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides were initially loaded onto a trap column (100 μm inner diameter × 2 cm, C18) and then separated on an analytical column (150 μm inner diameter × 15 cm). Both columns were packed in-house with reversed-phase silica of 3 μm (Reprosil-Pur C18 AQ, Dr. Maisch). The elution of peptides was carried out using a 120 min linear gradient, increasing from 2 to 100% solvent B (where solvent A is 0.1% formic acid in water, and solvent B is 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 500 nl min−1. MS1 data were acquired across 375–1,400 m/z with a resolution of 120,000, a 250% AGC target and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. MS2 scans were performed at a resolution of 15,000 with an AGC target of 100%, a 35% normalized collision energy and a maximum injection time of 22 ms. Two runs were conducted to obtain more peptide identification from three fractions from PA1497 and the acid-soluble fraction from PA1490. Blank extractions were processed concurrently to monitor the exogenous contaminants during the procedure.

The LC–MS/MS analyses were processed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer with a FAIMS Pro device (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to the Easy nLC 1200 system (Thermo Scientific) at Fudan University, Shanghai (labelled as FD). Then, 4.00 μl of sample solution was injected for each fraction, and the peptides in solution were separated on a home-packed analytical column (75 μm inner diameter × ~25 cm; 1.9 μm, ReproSil-Pur 120 C18-AQ, Dr. Maisch). The LC flow rate was 0.80 μl min−1 under a 500.00 Bar maximum pressure. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B consisted of 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. A 80 min gradient was used with the following profile: 5–8% B, 2 min, at a flow rate of 200 nl min−1; 8–44% B, 38 min, 200 nl min−1; 44–70% B, 8 min, 200 nl min−1; 70–100% B, 2 min, 200 nl min−1; 100% B, 10 min, 200 nl min−1; 100–5% B, 2 min, 200 nl min−1; 5% B, 2 min, 300 nl min−1; 5–100% B, 6 min, 300 nl min−1; 100% B, 10 min, 300 nl min−1. Full MS scans were acquired for the first 65 min, across the range of m/z 350–1,600 with a resolution of 60,000 and an isolation window of 1.6 m/z. Standard AGC target and auto maximum injection time mode were set. The MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 15,000 with standard AGC target, a maximum injection time of 30 ms and a normalized collision energy of 30%. The column was then washed and re-equilibrated without data acquisition for 15 min.

To verify the taxonomic identification (Homo), the MS/MS data files from each LC–MS/MS run were searched against a custom Mammalia type I collagen database, comprising type I collagens of mammals on GenBank and UniProt. Next, raw files were searched against our previously published database8, which included the modern human proteome and common bone and dentine proteins translated from archaic hominin genomes (hereafter, the Harbin database). This search was conducted to obtain accurate sequences for the bone proteome of the Xujiayao hominin samples. A common contaminant database was also included in each database search, which comprised the cRAP database (https://www.thegpm.org/crap/), the contaminant database from MaxQuant64 and a published contaminant database65.

All PEAKS Online (v.12)66 searches, including de novo, DB Search, PTM and SPIDER, were conducted using a precursor ion (MS1) mass tolerance of 10 ppm and a fragment ion (MS2) mass tolerance of 0.02 Da. Semispecific trypsin cleavage was applied, allowing a maximum of two missed cleavages in the searches. We included deamidation (NQ), oxidation (M), hydroxylation (P), Pyro-Glu from E and Pyro-Glu from Q as variable modifications, with no fixed modifications, allowing up to six modifications per peptide. The length range of peptides was 8–45 amino acids. A 1% false-discovery rate (FDR) at the peptide level was set, and proteins were filtered using the criteria of −10log10[P] ≥ 20. Other parameters were the default.

To validate our analysis result, we also conducted a search with MaxQuant (v.2.6.0.0). Digestion and modification parameters were the same as those used in PEAKS. Proteins were filtered with 10% FDR level to identify potential low-abundance proteins8,11,67,68,69, and the PSM FDR was 1%. Other parameters were set to the default. For monitoring the contaminant proteins from the experiment and comparing with other published individuals, we also searched our extraction blank data and the published Xiahe 1 and Xiahe 2 data12,28 using the same strategy.

To assess the endogeneity of the identified proteins, overall Asn and Gln deamidation rates for each sample were first calculated on the basis of peptide intensities70. Then, peptides associated with multiple genes were excluded, and the Asn and Gln deamidation rates were calculated for each identified protein on the basis of PSM counts. Proteins containing one or fewer Gln/Asn-containing peptides were excluded. Proteins with low deamidation rates were considered exogenous, while those with relatively high deamidation rates (Asn or Gln) in both PEAKS and MaxQuant results were classified as endogenous ancient proteins (Supplementary Table 4). Additional attempts to find other proxies to classify and confirm endogenous proteins were in Supplementary Text 7, Supplementary Tables 10–16 and Supplementary Figs. 13–19. We did not use those additional proxies here as they were not well examined with enough data.

The number of peptides and PSMs covering phylogenetically informative sites, along with their y or b ion conditions, was evaluated to assess their reliability. After removing peptides which could match bacteria or fungi, or could be attributed to other proteins from plants or animals possibly distributed around Xujiayao site by BLASTp (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/blast/blastp/), only the sites with at least two remaining peptides from both PEAKS and MaxQuant were retained. The y and b ions of each spectrum were then checked. The sites were considered reliable only if at least one related spectrum contained enough y and b ions. If the sites were located at the terminals of the peptides, at least 1 of the 2 possible y or b ions was required. If not, at least 2 of the 4 possible y or b ions was required. An additional test of Penghu-specific SAP COL5A2-E1211V was also conducted, but no reliable evidence supporting E or V was obtained (Supplementary Text 5 and Supplementary Fig. 20).

For each endogenous protein, peptides with abnormal or artificial post-translational modifications were excluded. Only alleles that met the strict criteria of PSM count ≥ 2, PSM ratio ≥ 25% and intensity ratio ≥ 10% were considered reliable and were retained, regardless of whether known or novel. For sites with both H/D (an oxidation product of His), P/E (di-oxidation product of Pro), N/D (deamidation product of Asn), or Q/E (deamidation product of Gln) residues, we changed them to single fixed His, Pro, Asn or Gln, respectively, as the alternative amino acids were probably derived from common taphonomic post-translational modifications. We then converted all leucine to isoleucine as they are isobaric. Consensus sequences were subsequently filtered by multisoftware confirmation, and only the amino acid positions that had overlapping coverage in both PEAKS and MaxQuant results were retained. This strategy resulted in 9 proteins with consensus sequences out of the 18 endogenous proteins (Supplementary Data 1). COL4A4 was excluded from further phylogenetic analysis as it was identified only in PA1490 with very low coverage (13 amino acids, 0.77% of the total sequence). We selected two Xujiayao hominin samples, Denisova3 (ref. 29), Altai (Neanderthal)32 and Vindija33.19 (Neanderthal)31 in the Harbin database, and 50 randomly selected present-day human sequences from the Human Genome Diversity Project (HGDP)71 to build the comparative dataset. For genome data retaining heterozygous amino acids, we randomly chose one protein sequence per individual. P. troglodytes sequences translated from a published high-coverage genome (Clint)72 were used as the outgroup. We used PartitionFinder2 (v.2.1.1)73 to find the optimal partitioning schemes and amino acid substitution models. Subsequently, we constructed a consensus Bayesian phylogenetic tree using the software MrBayes (v.3.2.6)74, running for 10 million iterations in 2 independent runs, sampling every 500 generations, with the first 2 million iterations discarded as a burn-in. Additional phylogenetic trees based on different datasets were also conducted using the same procedure (Supplementary Text 4 and Supplementary Figs. 7 and 21–23).

The Xujiayao hominin fossils were scanned using high-resolution computed tomography with the following parameters: 190 kV, 130 μA and a resolution of 37.56 μm. Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction and cranial measurements were conducted in VGSTUDIO MAX 2023.1 (Volume Graphics). A total of 26 linear distances, 3 cranial angles and 2 indices were obtained (Supplementary Table 17). The definitions and measurement follow standard procedures described in the published literature75,76.

Comparative fossils or models were digitized using a 3D surface Scanner (Artec 3D) at a point accuracy of 50 μm. Metric data for these specimens were collected and further supplemented as needed. The comparative samples include East and Southeast Asian H. erectus, African, European and South Asian H. heidelbergensis/early Neanderthals, East Asian late Middle/Late Pleistocene archaic Homo, Neanderthals and modern humans (Supplementary Table 18).

Principal component analysis was performed on key linear distances, angles and indices, including maximum cranial breadth (XCB), biporionic breadth (POB), porion-bregmatic height (PBH), parietal sagittal chord (PAC), parietal subtense (PAS), occipital sagittal chord (OCC), occipital subtense (OCS), parietal angle (PAA), occipital angle (OCA), po-b height-cranial breadth index (PBH/XCB) and parietal curvature index (PAC/PSA). A correlation coefficient matrix was applied for variable standardization in principal component analysis, which effectively reduced biases caused by inconsistent scales among different types of indicators. Box plots were used to visualize the parameters, showing significant variation across taxonomic groups (PAA, OCA, PBH/XCB and parietal bone thickness). One-way analysis of variance was used to detect differences among groups; with P values are displayed on the box plots. All statistical graphics were generated using R (v.4.1.1)77 using the packages ggplot2 (ref. 78), dplyr79 and ggpubr80.

#3498. Easy Ideas for World Ocean Day

World Ocean Day is celebrated around the world on June 8th to highlight the crucial role oceans play in supporting life on Earth. Did you know that oceans produce around 50% of the Earth’s oxygen and provide a main source of protein for billions of people? The health of our oceans is vitally linked to the overall health of our incredible planet and its ability to maintain a stable climate. We should all be fighting to protect them.

Aside from the practical benefits of healthy oceans, oceans are home to much of the Earth‘s biodiversity and a source of wonder and inspiration for people everywhere. Using less water helps prevent excess runoff from running into the oceans.

More greenhouse gases lead to global warming, which will have a devastating effect on our oceans. If the temperature of our oceans continues to rise at its current rate, there will be no coral reefs by 2050.

#3500. Neue Heimat, alte Wurzeln: Kurden in Deutschland

Kurdinnen und Kurden gehören zu den größten Zuwanderungsgruppen in Deutschland. Dass dennoch vergleichsweise wenig über sie bekannt ist, liegt auch daran, dass es keinen kurdischen Staat gibt.

Die Zahl der in Deutschland lebenden Kurdinnen und Kurden lässt sich nur schwer genau bestimmen. Der Grund ist einfach und zugleich traurig: Es gibt keinen international anerkannten kurdischen Staat. Die Menschen stammen aus verschiedenen Ländern, vor allem aus der Türkei und Syrien. Eine eigene kurdische Staatsangehörigkeit gibt es nicht. In ihren Herkunftsländern haben viele Repressalien erlebt. Viele sind mittlerweile auch in Deutschland geboren.

Dennoch versuchte der Mediendienst Integration, mehr Klarheit über die Zahl und die Lebenssituation der Kurdinnen und Kurden in Deutschland zu schaffen. In einer Videokonferenz des Mediendienstes berichtete die Psychotherapeutin und Sozialwissenschaftlerin Çinur Ghaderi über neuste Studien und Statistiken, aber auch über Probleme und Anfeindungen Kurdinnen und Kurden gegenüber in Deutschland.

Ghaderi ist selbst 1970 im Irak  geboren und lehrt an der Evangelischen Hochschule in Bochum. Im Januar dieses Jahres war sie Mitautorin einer Studie, für die mehrere hundert Kurdinnen und Kurden in München befragt wurden.

Das Ergebnis ihrer Untersuchungen: Mit rund 1,5 Millionen Menschen gehören Kurdinnen und Kurden zu den größten Einwanderungsgruppen in Deutschland. Allein seit 2010 sind mehr als 250.000 Menschen vor allem aus Syrien nach Deutschland gekommen. Hauptgrund der Flucht war die zunehmende Verfolgung während des syrischen Bürgerkrieges.

"2011 ging die Bundesregierung von etwa 800.000 Kurdinnen und Kurden in Deutschland aus", sagt die Bochumer Wissenschaftlerin. "Zwischen 2010 und Juli 2026 registrierte das Bundesamt für Migration und Flüchtlinge knapp 608.000 Asylanträge."

Vor allem für Menschen aus Syrien hat sich die Situation in den vergangenen Monaten teilweise drastisch geändert. "Nach dem Sturz des Assad-Regimes 2024 haben Rückkehrbewegungen eingesetzt. Zugleich bleiben die Verhältnisse besonders im Norden und Nordosten unsicher", so Ghaderi. Dennoch wolle die Bundesregierung nun vermehrt Menschen nach Syrien abschieben und tue dies bereits, was zu erheblicher Verunsicherung bei den Betroffenen führe.

Menschenrechtsgruppen, so Ghaderi weiter, berichten von willkürlichen Übergriffen: Der syrischen Übergangsregierung werde vorgeworfen, besonders in Aleppo gewaltsam gegen Kurdinnen und Kurden vorgegangen zu sein. Auch Schwierigkeiten, nach der Rückkehr wieder an das eigene Eigentum zu gelangen, werden geschildert. Zwar hätten sich die zuvor heftigen Kämpfe in Syrien beruhigt, von einem sicheren Land könne trotzdem nicht die Rede sein, so Ghaderi.

2025 war die Türkei Deutschlands wichtigstes Abschiebeland. Dabei würden die Antiterrorgesetze trotz der PKK-Entwaffnung weiterhin weit ausgelegt.

Die Türkei spielt auch in der Geschichte der kurdischen Zuwanderung nach Deutschland eine zentrale Rolle. Ab etwa 1920 kamen zunächst Angehörige der Elite, darunter Studierende, Diplomaten und Intellektuelle, nach Deutschland. Ab 1961 folgten Hunderttausende Kurdinnen und Kurden im Zuge der deutsch-türkischen Anwerbeabkommen als Arbeitskräfte. Eine dritte große Zuwanderungswelle setzte vor allem ab 2010 ein, als Krieg und Verfolgung viele Menschen zur Flucht zwangen. Außerdem mussten Kurdinnen und Kurden in Deutschland immer wieder unter dem pauschalen Verdacht leiden, Anhänger der kurdischen Arbeiterpartei PKK zu sein, für die schon seit 1993 in Deutschland ein Betätigungsverbot besteht.

Hinzu kommt: Gerade die Kurden sind Anfeindungen von anderen Zuwandergruppen in Deutschland ausgesetzt, neben den wieder zunehmenden Vorbehalten gegen Migranten allgemein. "Diese spezifischen Abwertungen gegen Kurden gehen insbesondere von Akteuren aus anderen Teilen der Migrationsgesellschaft aus, die in den Herkunftsstaaten eher der Mehrheit angehören, wie etwa ultra-nationalistische türkische Gruppen", sagt Ghaderi.

"Viele fühlen sich in Deutschland zuhause. Sie engagieren sich in Parteien, Kommunen und Vereinen", sagt die Wissenschaftlerin weiter. "Zugleich bleiben Krieg und Verfolgung in ihren Herkunftsländern sehr präsent".

Entsprechend aufmerksam und zugleich unsicher werden die Meldungen zur Entwaffnung der PKK aufgenommen. Im August verabschiedete das türkische Parlament ein Gesetz, das eine eingeschränkte und an Bedingungen geknüpfte Amnestie für PKK-Kämpfer vorsieht. Die PKK wiederum erklärte kürzlich, alle ihre Kämpfer vollständig aus der Türkei und dem Nordirak abgezogen zu haben.
