#1255. The Fall of “Scientific American”

When the beloved science publication got too political, its corporate owners stepped in. Now its future is uncertain.

In March of 2026, in a nondescript, 46th-floor office overlooking lower Manhattan, about two dozen members of Scientific American’s editorial staff had gathered in person and online to talk openly about their approach to political coverage.

David Ewalt, the editor in chief, wanted them all to understand something: The magazine was not “kowtowing” to the Trump administration. In fact, according to a recording of the meeting obtained by The Nation, he asked staff present to “raise your hand if you’ve ever heard me say not to cover a political story.”

For well over a year, rumors had been circulating among the staff about top-level editors’ rejecting pitches they saw as too political, controversial articles triggering angry phone calls from Scientific American’s corporate owner, and even, on at least one occasion, ownership forcing editors to blunt language critical of the Trump administration.

Then there was what happened to the opinion department. In the first months of the new administration, it had been publishing about 15 pieces a month, many of which criticized Trump’s attacks on scientific research. But after the department published a particularly provocative column in May 2025—“Science Tells Us the U.S. Is Heading toward a Dictatorship”—management, in what one staffer would later call an “unprecedented” move, had told the department to “pause” publishing new articles until the new editor in chief started.

The opinion department’s hiatus had worried some people more than others—there were legitimate journalistic reasons to want to emphasize breaking news, even if not everyone agreed with them. But by March 2026, nine months into Ewalt’s tenure, the opinion section seemed to be gone for good—and other departments were feeling political pressure, too. That, along with a round of layoffs, a restructuring of the newsroom, and veiled threats about the future of the magazine, had left the staff frustrated and looking for answers. Now that the new editor in chief was finally coming to the full editorial staff “to listen as well as to speak,” as one staffer put it, they began asking questions.

What about that rumor, someone asked him: Had editors really been told to not put the word “Trump” in headlines?

That was true, Ewalt said. Last year, a Breitbart article had criticized Springer Nature, Scientific American’s German owner, for attacking those “skeptical of the coronavirus vaccine” and spreading “gender insanity.” “No more taxpayer money for woke publishers!” declared Donald Trump Jr., retweeting the article. In June 2025, Axios reported that all federal subscriptions to Springer Nature publications had been canceled. Although Trump officials later walked back the bulk of the cuts, they had shocked the scientific community: Without access to Springer Nature’s thousands of journals, including the prestigious Nature publications, researchers at federal agencies—from the NIH to NASA—would struggle to stay abreast of scientific developments. The company itself, meanwhile, risked losing as much as $25 million in federal contracts.

So if Ewalt had asked people to keep Trump out of the headlines last summer, it was because corporate higher-ups were visiting the site all the time. “And if they saw the word ‘Trump,’” he said, according to the recording, “they were wetting their fucking lederhosen.” People had no idea how “lily-livered and weak-kneed and cowardly” Springer Nature was, he said. “They are so fucking afraid of the Trump administration.”

The opinion editors had long since been reassigned to other roles, but now, almost a year later, Ewalt was telling his staff that even news stories sometimes had too much of a slant to them. “I am not ever telling anybody here ‘Don’t go after the Trump administration,’” he told them. “I’m saying I don’t want to hear your opinion.” He just didn’t think it was their “strongest weapon.” If everyone stuck to objective, hard-hitting reporting, Ewalt believed that they would be able to stay out of trouble with Springer Nature.

Mentioning Trump in headlines was now allowed. “But let’s be realistic about the world we live in,” he told his staff. Last August, Ewalt warned, Springer Nature could have easily decided that the magazine’s “$4 million in losses” wasn’t worth risking millions more that the company earned from federally funded subscriptions to its scientific journals. He had been “trying to keep Springer Nature from freaking out and shutting us down,” he said.

As the meeting came to an end, Ewalt seemed optimistic. “This is useful,” he said. “We’ll do this more.”

But staffers still felt uncertain. Could people really trust their editor in chief to stand by their reporting, like he promised? Or would he retract articles if readers sent too many angry emails, or if Springer Nature’s billionaire CEO called? Those concerns, one staffer said, weren’t “quelled in that meeting at all.” Rather, it had transformed accumulated gossip and implication into something far more concrete—and “set everybody’s teeth on edge,” said another staffer, who, like a number of sources for this story, chose to speak anonymously because they feared retaliation from current or prospective employers. “He was trying to be honest with us,” the staffer said. But, also, it “alarmed the shit out of everybody.”

In April, the staff of Scientific American took the first formal step to form a union with the Writers Guild of America East, citing a number of concerns, including editorial independence. And on June 23—three days before the National Labor Relations Board was scheduled to count the unionization votes—Springer Nature announced that it was selling the magazine to a little-known company called LabX.

Scientific American is the oldest magazine in the United States and, among science publications, one of the most familiar and beloved. Like other publications of its caliber, it also has a tradition of editorial independence. In the 1980s, after a tumultuous split between its co-owners, it was absorbed by the German publishing empire Holtzbrinck, which currently controls both Springer Nature and the Big Five publisher Macmillan. A 1998 Vanity Fair feature called the company the “shark of choice” for struggling publishers: It promised to allow them to continue their work with minimal interference.

In recent years, however, Springer Nature’s hands-off reputation has begun to falter. Interviews conducted this summer and fall with a over a dozen current and former Scientific American employees depict a magazine that, especially over the past two years, has struggled to protect its editorial independence in the face of sometimes overwhelming pressure from its corporate ownership—a picture corroborated by audio recordings and documents obtained by The Nation.

Back in 2017, Springer Nature had made headlines when it admitted that it had censored hundreds of academic articles in mainland China at the behest of the Chinese government. But for most staffers, the first sign of a more hands-on approach came in 2021, when an opinion article about the medical situation in Gaza was pulled from Scientific American’s site after right-wing backlash.

At the time, the article’s authors received no explanation. But current and former Scientific American staffers familiar with the situation told The Nation that the reason was simple: Stefan von Holtzbrinck himself—known for his enthusiasm for Israel—had called then–editor in chief Laura Helmuth and ordered that it be taken down.

It wouldn’t be the last conflict between Helmuth and Springer Nature. Helmuth, who joined the magazine in 2020, had pushed it to cover more politics, arguing that science cannot be disentangled from its social context. Scientific American had issued presidential endorsements for the first time under her leadership. In 2024, after Helmuth called Trump supporters “bigots” and “fascists” on social media on election night, the magazine again became the target of right-wing backlash. This time, facing pressure from Springer Nature, she resigned.

The magazine had been struggling financially for years, but the pressure to be profitable only increased as Springer Nature repeatedly tried and failed to go public; in 2024, it finally succeeded. After Helmuth’s departure, “it felt like a pendulum swing back to ‘let’s stick to the science,’” said Kelso Harper, who was then the magazine’s senior multimedia editor. “Let’s not get involved in too much messy political stuff.”

In the first months of Trump’s second administration, though, messy politics was almost unavoidable. Trump brought disaster after disaster for science writ large—thousands of grants suspended or canceled, tens of thousands of workers fired or pressured to resign, billions of dollars of funding slashed or frozen. Scientific American’s opinion department critiqued Republican attacks on everything from trans health care and measles vaccines to fluoride and the National Weather Service.

Then, in April 2025, as the administration’s war on DEI in science continued to accelerate, psychiatrist and anthropologist Eric Reinhart posted a letter to X that the Trump-appointed US attorney for Washington, DC, Ed Martin, had originally sent to the Illinois-based medical journal CHEST. The letter, dated April 14, asked: Was CHEST protecting the public from misinformation? Was it disclosing when viewpoints were influenced by funders? Did it publish articles from competing viewpoints? “It has been brought to my attention that more and more journals like CHEST Journal are conceding that they are partisans in various scientific debates,” the letter read. “The public has certain expectations, and you have certain responsibilities.”

CHEST is not well-known outside its field, but the letter made national headlines as an example of the administration’s unprecedented efforts to undermine what the right called “woke” science. Reinhart submitted an op-ed to Scientific American senior editor Dan Vergano arguing that the letter represented “an authoritarian threat to science.” As the story moved toward publication, news broke of similar letters to other journals, including the prestigious New England Journal of Medicine and JAMA. Reinhart’s op-ed quickly progressed through an initial edit, a “top” edit, and a copy edit; according to e-mails and documents obtained by The Nation, a legal reviewer made only one, minor change.

But when the draft returned from another round of edits, this time from acting editor in chief Jeanna Bryner, the word “fascist” had disappeared, along with a reference to the genocide in Gaza. A paragraph in which Reinhart argued that fascists benefit from “self-interest, automatic obedience, and ethical cowardice” had been cut entirely. In a comment in the document, he protested: “Shying away from publicness and directness on these issues will only make them worse.”

The draft next went to Springer Nature’s US head of legal for review. He left a comment declaring, with no explanation, that the cuts were “necessary.” “Got it,” Bryner responded.

A tranche of recently released documents show that Springer Nature had good reason to fear retaliation: On April 14, 2025, Ed Martin had sent Scientific American a threatening letter nearly identical to that received by CHEST. He sent similar letters to Nature Medicine—also owned by Springer Nature—along with at least 10 other publications. (Martin is currently facing disciplinary proceedings from the DC bar for sending a different threatening letter to Georgetown University.)

At the time, Scientific American president Kim Lau responded to Martin privately, asserting that its content was “fully protected by the First Amendment.” Behind the scenes, however, Springer Nature leadership was insisting on substantial changes to Reinhart’s op-ed, against Lau’s objections.

Those objections were why they were “ultimately allowed to print it” at all, Lau told Jeanna Bryner and the two opinion editors in a May 2025 meeting, according to audio obtained by The Nation. The only reason things didn’t “blow up in a bigger way,” she said, was that Reinhart eventually agreed to the changes. But when the piece was eventually published, on May 1, it received little attention—probably, she said in the meeting, because they had “watered it down so much.”

And when Dan Vergano published his column “Science Tells Us the U.S. Is Heading toward a Dictatorship” two weeks later, it was the final straw. Shortly after, Lau called Bryner, Vergano, and the chief opinion editor, Megha Satyanarayana, into the May 2025 meeting.

“There’s no way to sugarcoat this,” Lau told them. Springer Nature felt that Scientific American’s criticism of Trump was threatening their broader business—selling subscriptions to their scientific journals. The federal government was a major client.

“I have reiterated repeatedly that we have a history of editorial independence, and that these things are crossing that line,” said Lau, referring to Springer Nature’s repeated editorial interference. “It’s very clear that they understand that. But given where our business is, and the threats to the larger business, they don’t care.”

The department’s work, she said, was being placed on “pause” until the new editor in chief, David Ewalt, arrived.

Lau was explicit about the pressure they were all under. “We’re upsetting the company on a daily basis,” she said. Germany was six hours ahead of New York, and she and Bryner would wake up to early-morning calls from the German board. “If I thought me quitting would help the situation, I would have done it,” Lau said. “They are terrified about the things that are happening in the US.”

Although Lau gave the editors reason to hope that Ewalt might manage to resurrect the opinion department—“I think he sees value in it,” she said—he would turn out to be on the same page as the Springer Nature executives in at least one regard: It was time to reduce opinion output significantly. During his interviews, Ewalt had pitched them his new vision of the magazine: less editorializing, more facts.

In May 2025, though, the opinion editors—like the rest of the magazine—still didn’t know much about their new boss. Lau assured them they could all talk things over with Ewalt and see how he wanted to approach the issue. “We just need to be clear that what we’re fighting for is worth it. And I’ll be honest with you,” she said, “I don’t think this is the thing I want to die on the hill for.”

“We are in a fight right now for the future of SciAm in general,” she said. “There is nothing that’s a given in our future. We are losing a lot of money, and we do not necessarily have a clear path to closing that gap.”

Over the course of the next year and a half, at least 53 of the approximately 84 people employed at the magazine would either be laid off, resign, or retire.

Ewalt took over the magazine on June 2 with a mandate to increase traffic and subscriptions. But there were other changes, too. Helmuth’s departure had triggered a new attention to employees’ political expression, and Ewalt’s arrival reinstated objectivity as the journalistic North Star. Things that had produced no objections in the past became contentious: People were discouraged from following only left-leaning social-media accounts, for example, to avoid the appearance of bias. Although he initially promised that his restructuring efforts would not include layoffs, in October 2025 10 employees lost their jobs, replaced by a slate of hires to new positions, including three breaking news reporters. In December, Kim Lau threatened further changes if revenue didn’t start picking up—more layoffs, workers guessed, or maybe a sale.

Though October’s layoffs galvanized the then-incipient union campaign, staffers had additional concerns. Vergano, who had been reassigned to report on Washington, remembers hoping that the union could serve as a counterweight to the “clear, corrosive corporate interference and influence” he’d witnessed at the magazine. That emphasis on editorial independence, said WGAE organizer Andy Greenberg, was one of the things that set the campaign apart from others he’d worked on. “We have to look out for us,” one staffer realized, “because Springer Nature is not.”

But it was becoming difficult to enjoy the moment. In the weeks after the vote, rumors began to circulate that Springer Nature executives were “basically measuring everybody’s desk to sell it,” Vergano said. “It was not subtle.” The bad news hit on June 23: sale to LabX, salary cuts, mass layoffs.

“The amount of panic, the amount of tears, the amount of ‘I don’t know the next time that I will see you, goodbye forever maybe’ conversations that were happening in the newsroom—I mean, traumatic puts it lightly,” said books and culture editor Brianne Kane. Even those who had received job offers from the new owners were told to clear their desks and that their laptops would be shut off by midnight.

The next week, a few dozen workers, freelancers, and allies gathered at the 1 New York Plaza office. Despite the previous week’s events, the mood on the picket line was jaunty. A small but energetic brass band accompanied chants of “New York is a union town,” and the signage was heavy on puns (“I Do Not Cosine These Layoffs”).

Behind the scenes, Springer Nature was still doing its best to nullify the election, including by trying to withdraw the petition for an election by pointing out that it had laid off everyone at the magazine. “This was a case where a corporate parent restructured and sold its subsidiary in a transparent attempt to defeat an election,” the WGAE wrote in a legal filing. “The Board should not reward such conduct.” When the NLRB counted the votes, the tally was 31 to 1 to unionize.

In the immediate aftermath of the sale, staffers were left scrambling to learn more about Scientific American’s new owner. Previously, LabX’s best-known publication had been Discover, a onetime dentist’s office stalwart that was hollowed out by private equity buyers in the aughts; according to multiple staffers, the ownership transition seemed rushed, chaotic, and disorganized. When one worker reached out to a former employee of one of LabX’s other publications, the stories they heard—including ones related to editorial independence—were part of why they turned down their job offer, they said. (LabX did not respond to multiple requests for comment.)

The NLRB case with Springer Nature remains open, but the union also filed for a new election. LabX hasn’t hired any outside union-busters, but it has held three meetings asking for more time to prove that a union isn’t needed. “It’s comical,” said one staffer, given that the company’s first action as the new owner was to lay off “many very talented, very successful staff members.” The bargaining unit is much smaller this time around—from 35 to less than 20, with a few positions still in dispute—but workers remain as committed to organizing as ever. The result, counted this week, was 12 to one.

When Dan Vergano reached out to the Society of Professional Journalists’s ethics committee for advice in May 2025—shortly after the department’s work was put on pause—he was told to start by working through official channels: Going public could come at a high personal cost. Besides, as a journalist, “you don’t want to be part of the story,” Vergano said. “It’s literally stomach-churning.”

He declined to speak to particular incidents, citing legal concerns. Still, he said, when a company is willing to interfere in critical journalism, how could reporters not end up pulling their punches? “We are trying to deliver you the raw stuff of democracy,” he said. “If a company allows their own greedy interests to shape that, then that’s a problem for democracy.”

It was clear that the “pall” Vergano described extended beyond the opinion section. “When you hear about people’s pieces getting cut to pieces, when you hear about pitches getting turned down,” said another editorial staffer, “these things ripple out to other departments.” That March meeting with Ewalt was the first time the newsroom had discussed editorial interference so openly, but by that point the problem was far from new: It was often unclear whether decisions were motivated by corporate interference or editorial judgment; stories of infamous incidents circulated for years.

“Reporters like to think that there should be a 60-foot brick wall between the business side of a news outlet and the editorial side,” Dan Axelrod, the chair of the Society for Professional Journalists’s ethics committee, told The Nation. “The reality is there isn’t, and there never has been.”

For most staffers, it’s still unclear exactly what motivated the sale—the money Scientific American was already losing, or the far vaster sums that Trump had threatened to take away, or both. Journalists at Nature News, also under the Springer Nature umbrella, were enduring “the same scrutiny” of their reporting on the Trump administration; Lau had told the opinion editors as much last year. But, Lau said at the time, Nature was “not a brand that’s losing money.” Scientific American, meanwhile, was a public-facing magazine owned by a company with little interest or expertise in public-facing journalism.

“The decision to divest reflected a strategic focus on our area of greatest expertise, academic research and scientific publishing,” Springer Nature’s North American president said in a statement to The Nation. David Ewalt had put it more clearly, perhaps, during the March meeting: “We are journalists working for a weird multinational corporation,” he told his staff. “We are the appendix of this organization. They don’t need us. They don’t even, frankly, want us anymore.” (Both Ewalt and Lau declined to comment on this story, and both have left Scientific American.)

Though it’s not ideal for democracy or free speech, Axelrod said, the journalism code of conduct does not bar a publication’s owners from steering it away from public service in search of profit. But “it’s sad and surprising to think that they would potentially do that on the fly, in a way that breaks readers’ trust.”

It was tragic, he added, “that the journalists who are supposed to be reporting on undue influence by the government on the creation and dissemination of scientific findings appear to be subject to that same undue influence.”

For one staffer, who started working in journalism after the recession, the field had been in bad shape for as long as they could remember—but Scientific American, they said, was always held up as “one of the icons, if not the icon, of meaningful, impressive journalism about science.” It was their dream job. On its own, they said, Scientific American wasn’t going to solve all the challenges at the intersection of science and society—attacks on public health, a lack of climate action, widespread disinformation. Still, they worried about “losing the spirit of it,” and that it would become just another website publishing less-meaningful “cool stories about science.”

In a letter to readers in Scientific American’s September issue, the new editor in chief—Jeanna Bryner, promoted once again—announced that the magazine was moving out of its Battery Park office. “I can’t promise that the next chapter will be free of mistakes or tumult,” Bryner wrote. But the change in ownership was “an opportunity for SciAm to be even better”—and, she added, “have some fun.” (Bryner did not respond to multiple requests for comment.)

At least one freelancer has already written publicly about their negative experience with the new Scientific American, and two more editorial staffers were laid off at the end of September, including Megha Satyanarayana, Vergano’s former colleague in the opinion department—who declined to comment for this piece—and a data reporter. Vergano remains worried that LabX seems more interested in “gee-whiz stuff” than critical, substantive reporting. From what he’s seen of their publications, he said, “they produce things that are schlockfests. And we were doing good work at Scientific American.”

In the May 2025 meeting that shut down the opinion department, Vergano and Satyanarayana had grappled with the unintended consequences of that work, work that leadership had once championed. “We thought that by being more provocative and by becoming more of a voice ourselves that we would hopefully make a difference,” Satyanarayana said to Lau. The experiment, she said, was “costing us more than I ever thought it would.”

Satyanarayana was talking about their columns, but she could as easily have been referring to a broader project: Scientific American’s efforts, dating back to Laura Helmuth’s tenure, to advocate for science at a time when science was under attack more than ever before.

“This is something that many outlets are struggling with right now,” Lau said. “That doesn’t excuse it, or make it better. Probably it makes it worse.”

#1257. Pope lung nodule: Could it be cancer?

The case of Pope Leo shows how small lung nodules are often found during routine health checks. Quick treatment is generally advised before nodules can grow to become larger life-threatening masses.

It often takes a chest X-ray or CT scan to spot small lung (or pulmonary) nodules, especially when a patient is asymptomatic.

At less than 3 centimeters (1.1 inches), lung nodules are less likely to press against your airway and cause any chest pain or shortness of breath or wheezing.

In the case of Pope Leo XIV, who told a gathering at the Vatican on October 11, 2026, doctors had found a nodule on his lung, it is unclear exactly how large that nodule is.

All we know (at time of writing on October 12, 2026) is that it was "detected at a very early stage," and that it was "well localized."

That suggests it has not spread or metastasized, although its full status is not publicly known. Doctors advised the pontiff to have his lung nodule removed.

Whether his was a chance finding — or whether the pope had presented with symptoms — we do not know.

But "incidental findings of lung nodule(s) in asymptomatic individuals" is considered an "increasingly common clinical dilemma," according to a 2019 paper published in the Annals of Thoracic Medicine.

This "dilemma" rests in part on the fact that biopsies — removing a small sample of tissue from the spot or nodule to determine whether it's malignant (cancerous) — are difficult to perform on small nodules.

The risks involved in a surgical diagnosis are considered "excessive compared to the relatively low prevalence of malignancy in the small nodules."

That's according to a 2017 paper published in the European Respiratory Review. And that may also be the dilemma that Pope Leo's doctors have faced.

Fortunately many — if not most — early-stage lung nodules are benign. It is also possible to have more than one nodule at a time, and they can all be benign.

It's said the pope will need a week to recover after his operation to remove the lung nodule. He is due to travel to Uruguay, Argentina and Peru between November 6-17.

Lung nodules are generally defined as a white spot or lesion on the lung. They have a rounded or irregular shape.

Radiologists refer to their "opacity" because, compared to the surrounding air-filled lung tissue, lung nodules appear as white or gray spots on a CT scan — that is, they have a certain density.

Lung nodules can be caused by old lung infections, foreign particles that are inhaled and get lodged in the lung, inflammation, scar tissue and indeed the onset of cancer.

At more than 3 centimeters, the nodule is defined as a mass. The bigger it gets, the greater the chance that it will obstruct your airway and the lung's ability to function.

Symptoms may start with chest pain, shortness of breath and wheezing. They may then progress to a chronic cough or coughing up of blood, fatigue, hoarseness, loss of appetite, unexplained weight loss and recurring respiratory infections like bronchitis or pneumonia.

It is hard to determine when lung nodules become life-threatening cancer. But one indicator of a lung nodule's malignancy is its size.

At 2 milimeters or smaller, the risk of cancer is very small and remains so up to a nodule size of about 6-7 milimeters.

The risk of cancer becomes 9%-10% at about 8 to 10 milimeters. And at larger than 1 centimeter, the risk of cancer is about 15%.

The density of a lung nodule is also a vital indicator of malignancy.

There are two main categories of lung nodule density: solid and subsolid. Subsolid nodules are further divided into pure ground-glass (nonsolid) and part-solid nodules.

According to a 2026 review article published in the New England Journal of Medicine, subsolid nodules are less prevalent than solid nodules, but they have "a higher likelihood of being malignant."

#1269. How to Stop Trump’s Coup

South Korea’s lessons for the US midterms.

How do you stop a would-be dictator? The first requirement is knowing that it’s possible. In 2024, the people of South Korea took to the streets and stopped a would-be coup in its tracks. Their example offers lessons for how Americans can counter Donald Trump and his allies if they disrupt or defy the results of November’s midterms.

After escalating conflicts with South Korea’s legislature and bribery accusations against his wife, President Yoon Suk Yeol abruptly declared martial law on December 3, 2024, at 10:23 pm. Like Trump, Yoon had already appointed loyalists to key military posts, and his state prosecutor used libel laws to attack political opponents and journalists. Now Yoon banned all political activity, protests, and strikes, and claimed the right to detain anyone he chose. He ordered the military to enter the National Assembly by force to remove the members, “including the use of firearms if necessary.”

But 16,000 protesters rushed to the building to protect the legislature. As Korea’s opposition leader and others headed there, they livestreamed calls for more people to join them. Citizens went on to nonviolently block armored vehicles. When military helicopters landed inside the assembly grounds, staffers constructed barricades from furniture and used fire extinguishers to slow soldiers breaking through the windows.

By 1 am, enough parliamentarians had reached the chamber to form a quorum, and they unanimously exercised their constitutional right to revoke Yoon’s martial law. For the next 10 days, citizens surrounded the parliament despite freezing temperatures—chanting, singing K-pop songs, waving light sticks, and calling for Yoon’s impeachment. The protests grew to over a million people by December 14, when the assembly impeached him. Yoon’s party lost the presidential election the following summer, and he is now serving a life sentence for insurrection.

Here are five key lessons for the US from South Korea.

1. Elections matter all the more under a would-be dictator.

Because South Korea’s opposition party had won major gains in the April 2024 election, it was already blocking some of Yoon’s initiatives before he declared martial law. Party leaders had defied previous attempts to silence them, and their leadership on the night of the attempted coup was critical. Because overwhelming vote margins are our best guarantee of securing our elections, we can’t let fear of Trump’s interference deter us from doing all we can to get people to vote.

2. Act quickly to confront attempted seizures of power.

As soon as Yoon declared martial law, activists and opposition party leaders called on people to protest. Had they waited, martial law would have become the status quo, making protest much harder.

For the US midterms, most points of contestation will be local: Citizens and local governments need to protect polling places and vote-counting centers, including having police protect them from disruption. Groups like the League of United Latin American Citizens are suing in advance to block ICE from polling places, state attorneys general and secretaries of state preparing emergency legal briefs, and the Brennan Center has published checklists refuting potential administration arguments. But massive, immediate nonviolent protest will also be essential.

3. Encourage security forces to stay loyal to the Constitution.

South Koreans who confronted the martial law forces said many soldiers showed open or subtle resistance to the coup. For example, soldiers didn’t actively repel the crowds in front of the parliament or remove the aides who were resisting inside. A major and a sergeant who were ordered to issue ammunition refused, saying that replenishing it would take too long. Officers commanding two military buses ordered their troops to stay aboard rather than fully deploy. A sergeant refused flight approval for a special forces transport helicopter. When one unit was ordered to seize computer servers to prevent a parliamentary vote, they delayed by stopping to eat ramen noodles at a nearby convenience store.

Ordinary citizens also urged soldiers to refuse unlawful orders. Former special forces members invoked their shared backgrounds with the soldiers tasked with putting down the protests. Protesters asked soldiers to remember the 1980 Gwangju massacre and not support a dictatorship.

In our own context, it mattered enormously that six US senators and House members with military and national security backgrounds created last November’s video on the duty to refuse illegal orders. We need to keep highlighting this duty to service members. We hope we’ll never reach a moment like South Korea’s, but if we do, we shouldn’t assume that the entire US military will do Trump’s bidding.

4. Prevent the coup’s initiators from consolidating power.

The day after the attempted coup in South Korea, civil society organizations held press conferences demanding the president’s arrest. Daily protests outside the National Assembly culminated in the demonstration by over a million people on December 14, pressuring legislators to impeach Yoon. Protests then shifted to the presidential residence and the Constitutional Court, demanding Yoon’s arrest. Labor unions demonstrated at the offices of legislators who refused to support impeachment and created a website to swamp those legislators’ phones with text messages. A widely circulated PowerPoint debunked Yoon’s arguments and offered counter-slogans.

The lesson for the United States is that power gets consolidated in stages, and every stage that’s blocked makes the next stage easier to prevent. Legal challenges are essential, but so are the actions of ordinary citizens. For example, Indivisible and No Kings have been conducting Hands Off the Vote trainings and recruiting poll monitors, and the long-standing 866-OUR-VOTE national hotline is expanding its presence. Veteran nonviolent organizers the Freedom Trainers have mapped out how people can respond to multiple scenarios, from preelection attempts to suppress the vote to attempts to block vote certification.

5. Create festive, diverse forms of resistance.

As South Korea’s demonstrations kept growing, K-pop light sticks replaced the traditional candles as the protest symbol. The sticks gave the rallies the feel of a concert, as protesters turned K-pop hits into chants. Musicians and artists showed up, adding to the festive atmosphere. Supporters prepaid for hundreds of cups of coffee, snacks, and meals at nearby cafés and food trucks. This atmosphere was crucial in drawing younger Koreans, the core K-pop fan base.

America’s major rallies, like No Kings, already combine festivity with defiance, a combination key to helping people keep going. Let’s embody that in our final election push!

Have pro-democracy organizers across the US engaged enough people to prevent Trump from overturning election results he doesn’t like? We won’t know until Election Day and the days immediately after. Meanwhile, South Korea’s example can inspire and instruct us as Americans defend our own beleaguered democracy.

It is a big commitment to make, and all in the name, essentially, of vanity. Only time will tell if it’s worth it.

#1279. The Accidental Activists of Data Center Alley

These would-be rebels in Northern Virginia joined the fight against data centers mostly to stop the encroachment on their homes and exclusive private communities.

I am hiking on a nature park that cuts through 45 miles of Northern Virginia, crisscrossing grassy terrain, wetlands, wooden bridges, and goldenrod, and then a dozen promontories offering the most unnatural of sights—windowless, hulking block-long data centers and their attendant transmission lines and substations. I heard “the hum” of the centers before I saw them; their sound resembled a horde of cicadas on meth. I felt nervous, and somewhat overwhelmed, as I counted roughly 20 of them in just the two miles I walked on the Washington and Old Dominion Railroad Trail in Loudoun County.

Here is the country’s thickest concentration of data centers—233 centers in all, over 3 percent of the county’s overall land mass. Data Center Alley, as it’s called, is a harbinger of what is to come in this country if tech hyperscaling continues unchecked. But Loudoun County is not only an example of tech infrastructure running amuck. It’s also an example of a semi-successful battle to press pause.

The week I visited in September, local activists had just secured a significant victory when the county’s Board of Supervisors voted 7–1 to impose a 12-month pause on board action for data centers and electrical substations. It was one win for the broader opposition, who were cross-partisan, unlikely dissidents in their 50s and 60s, who joined the fight mostly to protect their own homes and exclusive private communities. Some of them even refused to say a bad word about AI, claiming the fear of data centers in popular imagination was “overblown,” even though they themselves had been organizing against them as they encroached on their homes.

One of these accidental activists is Jessica Medeiros, 55. I met her in front of her home, in the Oak Trail Square development in Sterling. She works with juvenile offenders by day, as a pretrial officer in nearby Prince William County. In her off hours, she is just another empty nester of two grown children, hanging with her boyfriend, enjoying frequent outdoor runs in the area and five-times-a-day walks with her dog. Well, that was before the gargantuan blue Vantage VA2 data center at the end of her street, four city blocks long and wide, rose up.

That Vantage VA2 center happens to be one of the worst ones out there. For one thing, it isn’t connected to the electrical grid, meaning its energy comes from on-site or nearby power plants. These plants are primarily powered by natural gas; the VA2 center uses gas turbines and backup emergency diesel generators. This reliance means that such hubs risk being louder than normal, filthier than normal, and ultimately worse for the environment than normal. As a case in point, on the morning we met, Medeiros pointed out where the soot from the day usually falls.

More and more developers have sought power plant equipment like turbines and generators to elide regional power grids, which are now stressed under the data center’s electricity requirements.

To me, these data centers are a physical embodiment of both the decline of the rule of law and the rise of the tech oligarchs. They are, of course, part the national conversation around AI and have become a center of the activism against it. In the first half of 2026, the Data Center Watch project by AI security company 10a Labs found that at least 75 data center projects worth $130 billion were disrupted by local groups in the first quarter and at least 45 projects worth $68 billion in the second. The Data Center Opposition Report found that 640,000 people are in anti-data center Facebook groups in the United States. Gen Zers have joined groups like New Jersey’s Climate Revolution Action Network, or CRAN.

“To me, this center…is just indicative of what is happening in our country, because the money and the power are taking over the lives of citizens,” says Medeiros, a petite blonde who describes herself as having the energy of a puppy, with the swift gait of a runner, despite a recent foot injury. She was rapidly walking around the perimeter of a building that looks like a post-lobotomy IKEA. It has got the same cheerful cobalt exterior but no furniture, customers, or meatballs inside, only racks and heat. “Last week, Trump eliminated the, what was it? Oh yeah, the emissions level requirements for these power plant. For people like us, that basically screws us. We don’t have those protections anymore.” (The Trump EPA had recently exempted data centers from publicly disclosing their emissions.)

Medeiros and her neighbor Greg Pirio, 75, a government contractor who specializes in foreign aid, are two of a number of neighbors who have thrown themselves into protesting the Vantage plant, attending community meetings and town halls, doing press, creating systems for monitoring noise and air quality, and petitioning their local representatives.

But the center has also taken its toll, they say, on their bodies. Both have gone to doctors and Pirio twice to the ER, with extremely dry eyes that they believe were caused by the plant. (After an hour, my own eyes were throbbing, and I was blinking for tears to relieve the irritation.) For more vulnerable people in the area, the effects have been even worse. According to Medeiros and other neighbors, one family’s four children have all developed respiratory problems as a result. Medeiros’ voice is a rasp, now, as well, which she attributes to the centers.

They aren’t just imagining things. A recent report, “Health-Informed Computing: Estimating and Addressing the Public Health Impact of Data Centers,” modeled emissions from data center electricity and on-site generator usage, projecting health risks and costs particularly in host communities. One of the key culprits is the fine particulate matter these centers create.

But that’s not their only threat. Their constant whining noises should be understood as noise pollution, which the World Health Organization has found to cause high blood pressure, heart disease, and sleep disturbances. (Two of the people I spoke to mentioned sleep disturbances since the centers opened near their homes.) Hearing damage is a possibility if the roar of the center goes above 85 decibels—and the constant sound, all day, near schools has also been connected to lower educational outcomes, according to a forthcoming study from researchers at Brown University. How would the football team I watched, playing at a high school field hemmed in by an Amazon data center complex, be affected?

As Medeiros says, “It’s not an escape to get outside anymore.”

On the upside, Loudoun County has become a symbol for how these centers have given rise to new assemblies of people fighting back. These would-be rebels included two middle-aged men who worked in tech, who were clad in polo shirts, one whom I met overlooking the giant pool at his housing development, and a couple employed in real estate and construction that own two vintage Jaguar cars.

Another of these unlikely mavericks is Ben Keethler. Wearing one of those polo shirts, his hair close-cropped, the 54-year-old father of three co-owns an IT business that often serves federal agencies. The night I met him, after he had returned from ferrying his 12-year-old to golf practice, he was quick to say he was “skeptical of sensationalism,” which is why he casts some allegations of data center health consequences as insufficiently substantiated. Nevertheless, he did add that he was no fan of how closely two data center campuses abut his own housing development in Ashburn, Virginia, looming over the $1.5 and $2 million five-bedroom homes and the association’s man-made pond. “This should not happen,” he says, gesturing at the data center across the water. “What was once a beautiful view of a lake is now a beautiful view of Microsoft.”

It’s “invaded us,” Keethler says, before quickly shifting gears, noting the tax revenue that has been raised and the quality athletic complexes that now exist thanks to Big Data. We get closer to the data center in the moonlight: I can hear it buzzing like hundreds of air conditioners, its klieg lights blaring at 8:30 on a weeknight. Keethler’s vibe shifts. “I’ve told them not to leave the lights on like this!” he says. We walk right by the house in his neighborhood that’s closest to the data center, a mere 140 feet away, behind a wood fence and fir trees.

There are now over at least 3,000 operational facilities like this across the country, that account for 5 percent of the United States electric usage, and by 2030 they could amount to up to 12 percent, the amount of yearly electricity consumed by New York City—times 12.

To me, the growth and ubiquity of the data centers embody the theorist Mark Fisher’s idea of “science fiction capital.” The centers are avatars of tech monopolies, which calculate probabilities and promise surplus value in the future but also threaten humans’ jobs and sense of meaning in the present. According to an essay by Maha Hosain Aziz, a professor of international relations at NYU, an “AI Precariat” is now being created, whose jobs have been made contingent or volatile by the large language models.

Data centers and the AI they enable are sci-fi enough to have turned public opinion decisively against them. For instance, the House just approved a federal bill to keep AI data-center power costs from being put back on citizens’ electricity bills. The rare nearly unanimous congressional vote reflects the immense negative outcry over hyperscaling of physical computing infrastructure, although even this watered-down victory is sure to be eventually voted down by Trump.

The function of these centers is to know everything about us, analyze, and sell our information, but what do we know about them? As an early Internet hub near the nation’s capital, Loudoun County first saw data centers in the late 1990s. #1280. How green is Europe's urban public transport? A data-driven reality check

Greener buses and cleaner fleets are reshaping public transport across Europe. But are cities moving fast enough to meet their zero-emission goals?

An electric plug icon on their windows makes them easy to spot in cities like Brussels and Madrid. Quietly navigating the streets, electric buses have become an increasingly common sight on Europe's streets in recent years.

Michela Le Pira, assistant professor of transportation at Italy's University of Catania, says removing tailpipe emissions — the gases and particles generated when a combustion vehicle is running — might be "the easiest strategy to improve public transport."

Most Europeans live in urban settings, and road transport harms air quality. In 2023, almost 300,000 deaths across the EU were caused by air pollution levels exceeding the World Health Organization's (WHO) recommended thresholds.

Of all fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations in European urban areas, about 14% come from road transport, including public, private and freight transport. Additionally, towns and cities account for nearly a quarter of the European Union's transport greenhouse gas emissions (GHG).

DW and the European Data Journalism Network evaluated more than a decade's worth of data from more than 100 reports and sources on public transit and the emissions it causes, and identified 148 sustainability commitments by local authorities and transport operators in 33 cities across Europe. The analysis focuses on motorized mass transit, leaving out bikes and pedestrians as well as taxis, shared vehicles and e-scooters.

The EU wants to become the first climate-neutral continent by 2050. A key part of that plan is cutting transport-related GHG emissions by 90% from 1990 levels.

As DW's analysis shows, Copenhagen is the only EU capital that has almost electrified its entire bus fleet — its vehicles do not have combustion engines and therefore do not produce tailpipe emissions. In cities like Rome, Lisbon and Nicosia, more than 90% of buses still run on diesel, and metros and trams account for less than 10% of their public transport fleet.

Although cars still dominate European streets, "electric buses are one way to reduce local air pollution," said Martin Baierl, data analyst with Transport & Environment, an advocacy non-profit for clean transport.

EU funds enabled many cities to increase their electric and alternative fuel bus fleets from 2021 on.

Electric buses comprise 20% of the total fleet in EU capitals and selected cities. Their number increased by 55% between 2021 and 2025 — equivalent to 1,234 more such vehicles on the road. The number of diesel-only buses has been declining since then, although they still outnumber those running on alternative fuel.

In addition to decarbonizing the bus fleet and helping to reduce air pollution, embracing electric models can help to lower noise and public transport operating costs. All the more so if the city produces its own electricity, explains Baierl. However, the outlay for e-buses is higher than for diesel models. In 20 out of the 31 cities with data to analyze, over 50% of the bus fleet is made up of traditional vehicles.

Brussels, for example, was an early adopter of moving away from traditional diesel-powered buses, switching to hybrids instead. Now the Belgian capital is in the process of electrifying its fleet, but at a slower pace. "It's not easy to know what the right technology will be in the end," Baierl said.

Another example is hydrogen-powered buses, which also produce zero emissions but are more costly to operate than their electric counterparts.

Transportation is the only major sector where greenhouse gas emissions remain above 1990 levels, despite the EU's ambition of a 90% reduction by 2050.

On average, public transport — including buses, trams and metros — is used for more than a quarter of trips in the 10 largest EU capitals, according to recent data from the non-profit group CDP (formerly the Carbon Disclosure Project). In Stockholm, the share is 49%, while in the smaller Slovenian capital of Ljubljana, it's 11%. DW analysis of transport sustainability pledges reveals that only four cities have explicitly committed to achieving zero emissions in buses or all public transport.

Yannis Papadimitriou lives 85 kilometers (about 53 miles) away from Athens, the EU capital with the highest air pollution mortality risk. He travels to work by commuter rail, a means of transport he'd like to see promoted more.

"For me it is a very conscious choice to use electric public transport, especially trains, which are the safest, most environmentally friendly and quickest way of transport."

Though trains, trams and metros run on electricity, there are more buses within city boundaries, and in many urban areas the majority still run on diesel.

In Northern Europe, 15 kilometers away from Copenhagen, Poul Kattler also used to commute to work. He is now retired and takes the bus more frequently with his grandchildren. Despite Copenhagen having an almost completely fossil-fuel-free public transport system (only one bus runs on biomethane), the metro expansion, which is responding to the city's growth, comes with its own set of concerns.

"The whole construction phase [of the new metro line] is extremely environmentally demanding," Kattler said. If the construction emissions are considered, "the CO2 balance would be very poor for the metro now."

But in the long run, the benefits of a new metro line in reducing CO2 emissions outweigh its construction-related emissions, Le Pira said.

Combustion or exhaust emissions are not the only way transport pollutes the air and the environment. Indirect emissions, like those coming from electricity generation or fuel production, add to transport's CO2 footprint. Brakes and wheels release non-exhaust particle matter when wagons or vehicles slow down.

Since 1992, buses and any vehicle running on fossil fuels in the EU have been subject to stricter emissions standards. Fine particle emissions from buses and heavy-duty vehicles had dropped by 92% in 2022 according to the European Environment Agency. Yet overall road transport non-exhaust emissions from tire wear have increased 58% in two decades, as cars have become heavier.

With a few exceptions, EU member states are not close to cutting greenhouse gas transport emissions by 90%, as envisioned by the EU's 2050 target.

Zero-emission public transport is one step toward decarbonizing transport in cities and meeting GHG reduction goals. The next challenge is to get people out of their cars.

"If we talk about sustainable mobility in general and not only clean public transport, the main problem is the modal shift from private cars to public transport," Le Pira said.

On average, private automobiles account for more than a third of transport in the largest EU capitals, according to CDP data.

Cheaper ticket prices for groups, like students, and disincentives for car use, such as low-emission zones, are two complementary ways of attracting more passengers while reducing private transport.

In her research work, Le Pira investigates how public transport can be more efficient and appealing for city dwellers, for example, by combining transport modes where people step off their bikes and onto a train. This kind of solution could be particularly relevant for small cities "where cars are still the main transport mode."

According to Baierl, places with good public transport draw people in. "Cities where not everybody has to use a car are just more livable," he said.

Relay, an AI-powered workflow automation tool built as an alternative to Zapier, shut down entirely in August. More recently, chatbot platform Character.ai filed for bankruptcy just five years after launching.

Aside from Character.ai and Relay, plenty of AI bets never even survive inside the companies that build them. According to S&P Global Market Intelligence, about 42% of AI initiatives are ultimately abandoned by their corporate parents. The reasons vary: insufficient funding, technical challenges, competition, difficulty scaling, or just weak user demand.

The result is a growing AI graveyard. Below is a look at some of the most notable AI products, startups, launches, and bets that have shut down, pivoted, or significantly missed expectations. Think of it less as a scorecard of flops than as a record of what these attempts can (hopefully) teach the rest of the industry.

Even the biggest players aren’t immune. OpenAI, one of the most valuable private companies in the world, has had its own share of casualties and missteps. One of the more recent examples was its attempt to turn ChatGPT into a broader “super app.” On July 9, OpenAI updated the app to combine several experiences, including separate “Chat,” “Codex,” and “Work” modes, while renaming the traditional, familiar version “ChatGPT Classic.”

The redesign quickly drew criticism from users who found the new interface confusing and cluttered, and OpenAI rolled back the change soon after, bringing the familiar ChatGPT interface back.

That wasn’t OpenAI’s only attempt to pull everything under one roof. The company has also shut down several stand-alone apps and folded their features directly into ChatGPT — a strategy that’s worked out better than the redesign did. ChatGPT Atlas, a stand-alone AI-powered web browser, lasted less than a year before being discontinued on August 9, with its most useful features absorbed into ChatGPT.

The company did the same thing with Operator, an AI agent that could browse the web and complete tasks on a user’s behalf, and OpenAI has increasingly built image generation directly into ChatGPT, shrinking the role of DALL-E as its own separate destination for creating images.

We also can’t forget its video-sharing platform, Sora, which struggled with high operating costs and user-retention challenges and shut down in March 2026.

When Apple unveiled Apple Intelligence in 2024, an improved Siri was one of the highlighted features. The pitch promised a Siri that could understand context, know what was happening across apps, and get tasks done effectively.

However, Apple repeatedly pushed back the release of the new Siri, citing various reasons like engineering issues and bugs. This delay eventually contributed to a $250 million settlement over claims regarding how Apple marketed the AI capabilities of the iPhone 16.

Siri AI finally appeared in the iOS 27 beta this past July, with the new AI-powered version rolling out to English-language users first this month; support for other languages is expected to follow.

Announced at its 2024 Build developer conference, Microsoft’s Recall feature was pitched as an AI-powered “photographic memory” for Windows PCs, periodically taking screenshots of what a user was doing so they could later search back through their own digital history.

Predictably, the announcement triggered an immediate backlash over privacy and security. Critics argued that Recall could create a searchable archive of highly sensitive information, including passwords, private messages, financial details, and other activity. Microsoft delayed the feature for nearly a year, giving the company time to rethink its security and privacy protections.

Even after a redesign, the controversy hasn’t gone away. A cybersecurity researcher recently built a tool that could extract and display data that Recall had captured, reviving questions about whether Microsoft has actually fixed the underlying problem.

Notion Mail launched in April 2025 as an AI-focused email product designed to help users organize and automate their inboxes. However, the company noticed its users were using separate AI agents to handle their email inboxes instead. As a result, Notion Mail is scheduled to shut down on September 22.

The company filed for bankruptcy in early October, citing legal, regulatory, and operational headwinds.
