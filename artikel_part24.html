HAMOUD, Jordan, Oct 25 - When gunmen opened fire on a church in Jordan, Sahar al-Halsa hid behind a pillar as she heard the shooting and people screaming in panic during a funeral service for her cousin's daughter.

When she rushed outside in the chaos, she said she spotted the assailants and another cousin lying on the ground wounded.

"I went to him and said: 'Assem, Assem, my darling, answer me'," she said.

She said he opened his eyes to look at her, but then "he was gone".

Friday's attack on the Greek Orthodox place of worship in Karak governorate, about 120 km (75 miles) southwest of the capital Amman, prompted King Abdullah, a US partner in the fight against Islamist militant groups, to order security forces to respond with an "iron fist" to what authorities described as extremist terrorism.

Nine people died in the shooting that witnesses said lasted for several minutes. The funeral services were held on Sunday.

Eight of the victims’ coffins were wrapped in Jordanian flags before they were buried in a graveyard in the village of Hamoud, where the attack took place. Mourners and priests gathered at the scene.

Forty people were wounded in the rare attack on minority Christians.

Speaking from her home, from where the red dome of St George's church is visible, al-Halsa concluded that God must have saved her.

"My life starts now, because I was going to die," she said, speaking beside her husband Ziad al-Halsa. A statue of Jesus on a cross was displayed on a shelf.

Authorities have said they are still searching for the attackers, adding it was too early to determine whether they had links to known groups inside or outside Jordan.

There has been no immediate claim of responsibility.

Ziad said he ran the short distance to the church after he heard the gunfire, describing the overwhelming sense of relief when he realised Sahar, who he described as "the love of his life", was safe.

"To my cousins, may God have mercy on them," he said.

Such attacks are highly unusual in Jordan, a relatively stable state in the Middle East, though authorities there say they have foiled several Islamist plots in recent years.

Speaking ahead of the funerals, priest Yuhanna Hamati said:

"Our message from this church, from this place where this horrific crime was committed, is a message of love, peace, and reassurance. A message that we send to the whole world." REUTERS

#1157. Trump's diesel deal with Russia is unlikely to significantly lower global fuel prices despite expectations of rapid relief

As the world's largest diesel exporter, the US benefits from high prices. At home, however, consumers are feeling the strain of rising living costs. Why Donald Trump's deal with Russia is unlikely to change that.

With or without a deal with Russia, diesel prices on global markets are unlikely to fall significantly anytime soon.

That is the assessment of experts interviewed by DW regarding the latest fuel-supply agreement between US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin.

"The main reason for high diesel prices is the war in Iran and the simultaneous disruption of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandeb Strait," said Samina Sultan, a foreign trade expert at the German Economic Institute (IW) in Cologne.

The situation has led to production outages and made fuel shipments "more expensive, less secure and more difficult," she said.

Trump's deal with Russia also has an additional geopolitical dimension, Sultan said: "It causes the wars in Ukraine and Iran to become completely intertwined."

Things are looking up, though. Roughly 13.5 million barrels of crude oil are once again flowing through the Strait of Hormuz a day, according to shipping and commodities analytics firm Kpler.

But for refined products such as diesel, volumes remain far lower at around 677,000 barrels per day, compared with 3.6 million barrels before the war.

Carsten Fritsch, a commodities analyst at Commerzbank, also attributes the price increases to the wars.

"If nothing changes in Ukraine and the Middle East, I see very few ways to resolve these supply bottlenecks," he said.

In a social media post on October 9, Trump announced that he would temporarily suspend some sanctions on Russia and begin importing Russian diesel.

He wrote "diesel prices for Americans and, indeed, the world, will be COMING DOWN, IN RECORD NUMBERS, AND FAST!" in a mix of lowercase and all caps.

Russia confirmed the arrangement. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told Russian state news agency TASS on October 13 that supplying diesel fuel was "in the interests of Russia, the United States and global energy markets."

Diesel prices in the US and many EU countries have risen sharply over the past 12 months.

According to the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), prices in the US have climbed by about 60% and now stand at $6.30 per gallon, equivalent to $1.66 (or €1.49) per liter of diesel.

But even if Russia were able to flood global markets with cheap diesel, prices would not fall nearly as much as they recently rose, something all of the experts contacted by DW agree on.

Many refineries around the Gulf region have suffered disruptions or damage, while transporting fuel via shadow fleets and securing insurance remains costly, cumbersome and risky.

Another key shift is that Russia is no longer the world's largest diesel exporter. In fact, it is facing its own fuel shortages at home.

The US is now the world's biggest diesel exporter. In 2025, it exported an average of 1.26 million barrels of diesel per day, accounting for roughly 15.4% of global diesel exports.

At the same time, Russia fell to second place with a 9.5% share, and that was before major Ukrainian drone attacks on its refineries.

Besides the new US-Russia diesel agreement, Trump also recently floated the idea of US diesel export restrictions. But the idea was abandoned because it could lead to even higher diesel prices in the country, according to Samina Sultan.

"There simply is not enough diesel storage capacity in the United States," Sultan said. "Production would have to be reduced, and then prices would rise again."

If the US were to impose export restrictions on diesel, buyers in Latin America and the European Union would be hit particularly hard. Since Russia invaded Ukraine, EU countries have increasingly imported diesel from the US instead of Russia because of sanctions.

"An export ban merely redistributes the shortage; it does not solve it," said ING chief economist Carsten Brzeski.

"Global prices would continue to rise, while refineries would scale back production as margins shrink," he added.

Export restrictions aside, few people consider that the US-Russia diesel deal will have any big impact.

According to calculations by the platform The World Data, the promised shipment of 2.2 million barrels of diesel from Russia would not even cover a single day's US diesel demand of roughly 4 million barrels.

Even if all of the diesel volumes announced by Trump were combined, totaling 35.8 million barrels, they would only cover about nine days of US domestic demand.

"I CANNOT OVERSTATE HOW MUCH OF A NOTHING BURGER THIS IS," Canadian oil market analyst Rory Johnston wrote in an all-caps post on X shortly after the announcement.

Russia would normally export almost 10 times that volume if its refinery fleet was not being targeted by Ukrainian drone attacks, he added.

Canada also supplies diesel to the United States. Roughly 80% of all US diesel imports come from its northern neighbor.

Given Trump's verbal attacks on Canada and the ongoing trade dispute, analysts view the prospect of Canada supplying significantly more diesel to the US as highly unlikely.

For months, Palestinian testimonies from the West Bank and Gaza, reprinted in major global newspapers, had telegraphed horrific scenes: shrapnel slicing through the torsos of bystanders; students taken out while exiting their school. The operation that day in June was, however, a tipping point. Twenty-seven IDF pilots penned a letter refusing to play a part in operations they deemed “illegal and immoral.” Thousands more rallied in support. It was the first coordinated protest among Israeli pilots against such policies in the occupied Palestinian territories. It was also the last.

NAZA, a new documentary by Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor, the Israeli half of the team behind the Oscar-winning No Other Land, helps elucidate why, offering a portrait of the technological war machine that developed in the years since. Composed of interviews with anonymous reservists and officers from Israeli units responsible for planning and coordinating air strikes, the film digs into how Israel’s military carried out one of the most destructive air campaigns in modern history in the weeks following October 7, 2023. The documentary’s name is not a combination of “Nazi” and “Gaza” as some reviews have insinuated, but rather taken from the Hebrew acronym for collateral damage. Previous reporting on the film has focused on the dystopian array of machine-learning technologies used to kill Palestinians—one officer calls Israel’s air war the “perfect use of AI.” Less remarked upon, however, though no less consequential, is how NAZA showcases this arsenal’s political effects on the country’s liberal camps: how Israel’s embrace of automated warfare has made policies that once seemed exceptionally cruel, like intentionally killing dozens of innocents as they sleep in their beds, feel routine to the intelligence officers who helped coordinate Israeli air strikes in Gaza. And with them, how ideologies once relegated to the margins of political discourse have become mainstream.

For those who have been paying attention to reporting on Israeli military operations in Gaza for the last three years, NAZA’s revelations are not exactly new. Palestinians in Gaza have been documenting the astronomical toll of these air strikes since October 2023. Abraham and others have also reported on the operational details, like how the army hacks children’s phones with spyware to kill a Hamas operative and his family and military lawyers sign off on these operations. What is new with NAZA, though, is the way the film articulates these points: through testimonies from secular, ostensibly liberal young Israelis who served in elite military units. The film’s subjects sit on Tel Aviv rooftops, describing how they helped destroy Gaza from a distance, with the click of a button. “Fun” is how one officer describes the work of coordinating air strikes that kill families sleeping in their homes. “I understand how a man can blow someone up and go home to his degree in clinical psychology,” another officer admits. “It was like a video game,” still another tells Abraham. “Everything was done remotely.”

It would be amiss to view these technological developments in a vacuum; they are part of Israel’s shift to the far right over the last few decades. The IDF began to adapt these systems en masse in the 2000s. The land, sea, and air blockade that began after Hamas was voted into power in 2006 spurred a dramatic change in the form of Israel’s military rule over the Gaza Strip. Israel’s army poured resources into expanding the use of drones, cell phone tracking systems, and wiretaps prototyped and refined during the Second Intifada. The idea was to leverage new military technologies to maintain Israeli control without deploying boots on the ground. Intelligence units—whose conscripts were often plucked from Israel’s affluent urban centers—tracked down members of Hamas and Islamic Jihad by listening to their cell phones, hacking into their e-mails, or poring over aerial images of Palestinian cities, compiling them into long lists of military targets for drone or fighter jet pilots to bomb.

The effects on the ground were gruesome and bloody, but the technology at the disposal of airforce and intelligence units meant that many involved—unlike their compatriots patrolling the West Bank—were increasingly shielded from its effects. The names of targets were displayed in spreadsheets, the coordinates of their mobile phones as blinking dots on a virtual map, and they were killed by munitions launched from armed drones, hovering hundreds of feet in the air. “It reminds me of a Play Station game,” one intelligence operative told a Haaretz reporter after Operation Cast Lead back in 2009, a 22-day assault on the Gaza strip that killed 1,400 Palestinians, an unprecedented number. “You hear cheers in the war room after you see on the screens that the missile hit a target.”

Though Operation Cast Lead was roundly condemned by institutions like the United Nations and International Criminal Court, following the event the Israeli military only set about making its surveillance and targeting arsenal more potent. Israeli conscripts developed predictive algorithms to synthesize the reams of data taken from the Palestinian territories, where Israel’s control of the telecommunications infrastructure and airspace give soldiers panoptic powers. NAZA describes one named Lavender, which culls through automatically translated and transcribed calls, text messages, social-media content, and location history stored on phones to assign Palestinians in Gaza scores, which, in turn, determine whether someone is a military “target” or a member of Hamas or Islamic Jihad’s armed wings.

Technological innovations often go hand in hand with political ones. With time, long lists of algorithmically generated military targets helped make wars on Gaza more frequent and gruesome: By 2021 they had become an annual event. Israel’s growing right-wing camps were galvanized by these spectacular displays of military force. Politicians like Benjamin Netanuyahu and Itamar Ben Gvir boasted to their supporters of expanding lists of Palestinians killed, who had taken to shouting “death to Arabs” at rallies. Meanwhile, the daily violence necessary to maintain Israel’s occupation in the West Bank, with settler vigilantes killing Palestinians outside their homes and burning their agricultural lands, armed soldiers searching high school students’ phones at makeshift checkpoints or raiding homes in the dead of night, allowed a militant strain of Jewish supremacy to creep from the margins of Israeli political discourse into the mainstream.

Among Israel’s shrinking left-wing camp, the same arsenal helped the possibility of unending military rule disappear from view. Soldiers from Israel’s more liberal constituencies circulated from elite technological units into high-paying jobs at multinational technology firms setting up shop in central Israel. They might have gotten their start using spyware to hack into Palestinians’ phones or tracking a reaper drone as it zeroed in on a house in Gaza city, but on LinkedIn the work was reframed as “user experience research” or “development operations.” Though violence gestated a few miles away, as settlers torched Palestinian agricultural lands in the West Bank and air strikes pummeled Gaza, an influx of foreign capital transformed cities like Tel Aviv into hermetically sealed environments.

That seemed to change on October 7, 2023. The atrocities Hamas inflicted were an unprecedented national trauma, one that Israelis from across its ever-narrowing political spectrum desired to avenge. The technological arsenal detailed in NAZA allowed the army to act with unrestrained force on that day. One officer in the film describes how, though Lavender had a 15 percent error rate, soldiers were instructed to use it to mark even the lowest-ranking Hamas operatives out for assassination. Another describes using a program called “Where’s Daddy?” that alerted him when targets returned to their family homes by automatically flagging messages from their children. A third explains how this arsenal allowed the army to systematically kill hundreds of Hamas operatives as they slept alongside their wives and children at night.

On camera, the officers Abraham interviews sketch out the user-friendly interfaces they used to coordinate destruction. Some had no qualms about the unprecedented bloodshed, while others explain how specific military policies helped rationalize skyrocketing civilian casualties. As various investigations have shown, directives from the upper echelons of Israel’s government to wage war without restraint meant that military lawyers authorized strikes that could kill anywhere from tens to hundreds at a time, all in accordance with the army’s flexible interpretations of laws of war.

Reporting by Palestinians in Gaza has long evinced the cost these weapons, and the policies that come with them, exact on their victims. If that was not enough, NAZA now makes the consequences for their perpetrators impossible to ignore. When extraordinarily violent technologies—like spyware, predictive targeting algorithms, and 2,000 pound warheads—become ordinary instruments of military rule and occupation, practices that once seemed abhorrent, like dropping a bomb on a house full of children, become routine. The result is the Israel featured in the documentary: a place where 18-year-old soldiers dispatch drones to blow up houses with families inside of them in between espresso breaks at a shopping mall.

NAZA should be a warning to other countries—if it is not already too late. The United States military forged its AI arsenal through two decades of drone warfare in Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Syria, and beyond, where the killing of unarmed civilians by pilots sitting on airbases in Nevada was routine. It was released in full force in Iran, as large language models offered up long lists of targets for drones and fighter pilots to strike in operations that left hundreds of civilians dead. Today, many Americans rally behind politicians who champion unrestrained force and expansionist policies, parroting Israel’s own brand of hawkish nationalism. Others simply do not have to think about the gruesome outcomes of their country’s military adventurism, even those who engineer the algorithms now being used to determine where the Pentagon drops bombs.

The protests that rocked Israel back in 2002 were summarily condemned by IDF officials in the months to follow. Speaking to reporters later on that year, Dan Halutz, then the commander of the Israeli Air Force, famously told his soldiers they could “sleep well at night” whilst carrying out aerial strikes in Gaza or the West Bank, even when the mission might entail bombing civilians. They should trust the chain of command, from intelligence analysts who hunted down purported militants to commanders who reviewed the data and dispatched a pilot to kill. “When I release a bomb,” Halutz said, “I feel a light bump to the plane as a result of the bomb’s release. A second later, it’s gone. That’s all. That is what I feel.” NAZA showcases the technical arsenal that helped generations of soldiers heed his advice.

#1172. The full truth about JFK Jr and Carolyn Bessette's plane wreck is finally revealed: The heartbreaking details redacted from the report… and how close officials came to calling off the search

By CAPTAIN RUSS WEBSTER FOR THE DAILY MAIL

Published: 20:39 GMT, 25 October 2026 | Updated: 23:09 GMT, 25 October 2026

As darkness fell on the night of July 16, 1999, John F Kennedy Jr climbed into the cockpit of his Piper Saratoga II.

The avid flyer was taking his wife of nearly three years, Carolyn Bessette, to his cousin's wedding in the Massachusetts village of Hyannis Port, dropping off Carolyn's sister Lauren in Martha's Vineyard on the way.

About an hour after taking off from the Essex Country Airport in New Jersey, tragedy struck, with Kennedy, 38, becoming disoriented, losing control of the plane and plummeting downwards in what experts call a 'graveyard spiral.'

The crash, just seven and a quarter miles off the southwest tip of Martha's Vineyard, killed Kennedy, Carolyn, 33, and Lauren, 34, on impact, the wreckage settling 116 feet beneath the surface on the ocean floor.

Captain W Russell 'Russ' Webster was one of the first on the scene after getting word of the Kennedy heir's missing plane. Because the crash happened in his area of responsibility as Coast Guard Commander of Group Woods Hole on the western edge of Cape Cod, Webster served as Incident Commander for Operations of the grueling five-day mission – the largest search-and-rescue operation for private citizens in a small-aircraft crash in US history.

Twenty-seven years later, his full account of those days is detailed through The Last Flight: The Untold Story of the Search for JFK Jr and the Race Against the Atlantic, written with his historian wife Theresa Mitchell Webster.

In this exclusive excerpt, he describes how the Coast Guard, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), and the Navy worked together to find the plane wreck site. Then began the seemingly impossible search for its passengers.

Amid talks of calling off the operation, they were finally found, with rescuers undertaking the heartbreaking and extremely delicate mission to recover the three victims, and respectfully bury them at sea.

JFK Jr and his wife Carolyn were killed instantly in the crash

Wreckage of the single-engine Saratoga settled 116 feet beneath the surface on the ocean floor

Senator Ted Kennedy, in a red-and-white-striped short-sleeved polo shirt and a life jacket, watches intently as the divers descend, his face inscrutable.

His adamant request is that Navy divers raise John, Carolyn, and Lauren at the same time – that way his nephew isn't perceived as receiving special treatment, even in death.

Once at the wreck, the divers are on a strict timetable. Walking the Atlantic floor stirs up silt, further compromising low visibility. But sunrays filtering through the 116-foot water column provide a bit of natural light as they descend into the Atlantic.

JFK Jr is on his side on the ocean floor near his wrecked Saratoga.

The divers, with care and reverence, lift his remains and bring him to where the body bags are stowed on the Navy's salvage basket.

It is then that they discover that Kennedy's legs are missing – or that's what they report up the chain, according to retired Navy diver Josh Benjamins.

Whatever the circumstance, the divers still must do their job.

There is little light 116 feet below the surface, and divers can hardly see. But skilled as they are, they undo the tape affixing the rolled-up body bags to the basket. Next, they unroll one of the bags, laying it flat on the seafloor. The zipper extends the full length of the bag and is carefully opened.

Finally, once the top of the bag is opened, Kennedy is lowered into it. The bag is lifted and secured at four points.

It's time to recover the Bessette sisters.

Together, the divers walk through rising sandy silt, again with limited light, toward the fuselage to retrieve Carolyn and Lauren.

Both women's bodies are intact but badly damaged and horribly so. (Later, a witness at the autopsies, Frank Ciaccio of the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB), would only say that Carolyn and Lauren 'were not facially recognizable.')

The impact of the crash forced the backs of their seats to fold in forward over them.

The divers pry the seats back, slowly, then cut the seat belts. They can't tell which sister is which except by hair color. Lauren is a brunette, Carolyn an unmistakable blonde.

In a high-impact collision, this is not an unexpected outcome. Out of respect for the Kennedy and Bessette families, we leave further descriptions unsaid.

The first sister is lifted from her seat and, using a fireman's shoulder carry, her diver exits the fuselage, slowly and with great care, and walks toward the salvage basket.

He returns to the basket and, together with another diver, retrieves the second sister and places her into a body bag.

It's zipped, slowly, methodically. Both sisters are finally secured, together at last.

But before the basket reaches the surface, the Navy's Jeff Pauls informs Senator Kennedy about the suspected condition of his nephew's remains.

The uncertainty unsettles all. Will the other half of his body – if the impact had indeed cut JFK Jr in half – rise on its own?

Then the dive team and the basket holding the deceased ascend into daylight. All three victims are together in the salvage basket, just as Senator Kennedy had insisted. For the first time in days, JFK Jr, Carolyn, and Lauren are above the waterline.

It will be a few hours before a second dive team descends to attempt to raise the fuselage and engine.

In Pocasset, north of Woods Hole, the afternoon slides into early evening.

Three men – and only those three – are in a small, cream-tiled room at the aging Barnstable County Hospital, site of the medical examiner's office.

There, a pathologist, a Massachusetts State Police photographer, and Frank Ciaccio of the NTSB are present for the autopsies.

'There is no other extenuating conversation going on in the room, compared to other cases that I have worked on,' remembers Ciaccio. 'In all my years of doing autopsies and everything, this was probably one of the most solemn I was ever a part of.'

It is determined that John, Carolyn, and Lauren were killed instantly from numerous traumatic injuries when the Saratoga slammed into the Atlantic.

Toxicology tests on JFK Jr reveal no alcohol or drug use.

'You never want to make these things personal,' adds Ciaccio. 'But I think what made this very difficult for a lot of us is what we knew of the person. The question of the condition of Kennedy's body has been answered yet remains controversial.

The story I was told at the time of the tragedy was that JFK Jr's legs were folded up into his chest cavity, and this was revealed during the medical examiner's autopsy.

Others concur. 'The report I received was that he had been jammed up, so that he was basically compressed from the impact. But everything was there. It was just mashed together,' says the USS Grasp's commanding officer, Eric Anderson. [Grasp was the Navy rescue and salvage ship that led recovery operations.]

The NTSB's forensic anthropologist, Frank Ciaccio, as noted before, witnessed the autopsies: 'I will tell you 100 percent that [the report that Kennedy's legs were missing] is false. His legs were not severed.

'His legs were in fact fragile and hanging on by tendons and muscles. But they were not amputated at all.'

This account differs from that of Navy diver Josh Benjamins.

Benjamins is one of two divers on the second plunge a few hours after the three deceased were respectfully recovered.

According to Benjamins, he and his diving partner are briefed by the master diver during the safety check process. The two are tasked with retrieving the plane wreckage and the engine. Unofficially, however, they are to undertake 'a quick search for the legs.'

Benjamins and his partner descend. They first search for human limbs. Finding none, the divers set to work hoisting the fuselage and, minutes later, the engine.

Benjamins insists, even after all these years, that what he had heard that day was that '[Kennedy's] torso up was all they had' before he was placed in the body bag.

'They both [the divers below] had actual physical hands on him, and they both had done multiple body jobs before. They went on record saying, "We know for sure that only his upper half was recovered,"' Benjamins will recall.

'So, in all our wisdom mindset, [we thought] what was the difference? They did a ceremony, they put them at sea, let them go to sea.'

The last physical part of Carolyn remaining on Earth had its own burial at sea the same day her ashes were conveyed to the nearby depths.

#1179. Do non-German nationals commit more crimes than Germans?

People without German passports appear more frequently in German police crime statistics than German nationals. A new study explains why.

Every year, when the Federal Criminal Police Office publishes the police crime statistics, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) — a party that is, in part, right-wing extremist — pays particularly close attention. They are looking for crimes committed by refugees, migrants and foreigners living in Germany.

But studies by media scholars show that 95% of the AfD's press releases focus on non-German suspects, while crimes involving German suspects are almost completely ignored.

However, the common narrative of the AfD — that non-Germans are inherently much more violent — is simply not true.

In a new research paper, Susann Prätor, a sociologist, criminologist and professor at the Lower Saxony Police Academy, has taken a very close look at crime data in Germany, together with her colleagues from the University of Zurich and the Lower Saxony Criminological Research Institute.

She looked not only at officially reported crime — that is, all offenses that appeared in police crime statistics — but also at unreported crime: These are all offenses that are not reported or detected, and therefore do not appear in any official statistics but are identified through representative surveys.

"According to police crime statistics, people who do not have German citizenship commit crimes more frequently than German citizens. However, studies into unreported cases show that non-German nationals are twice as likely to be reported. Differences between the two groups are also evident in crimes that go unreported, but they are significantly less pronounced," Prätor told DW.

Crimes committed by foreign nationals are therefore twice as likely to appear in police crime statistics as crimes committed by Germans.

These statistics record all reported cases and do not specify whether the allegations prove to be true.

When the statistics are adjusted for the bias in the reporting of crimes, foreigners are no longer 2.6 times more likely to commit crimes than Germans, but only 1.7 times more likely. This figure is also consistent with the results of studies on unreported juvenile violent crime. According to these studies, foreign youths are about 1.6 times as likely to commit violent crimes as German youths.

Other factors affecting crime include age and gender. Young men make up a larger proportion of the foreign-born population, and young men are the group most prone to criminal behavior across all societies.

According to Prätor, "categories such as being young, male, poorly educated and having a history of violence are factors that increase the likelihood of violent behavior. A certain concept of masculinity also plays a role, wherever that may come from. But we do not see culture as a factor, contrary to the prejudice that masculinity and violence play a greater role in other cultures."

Prätor points to a survey on attitudes toward violence. According to the survey, 5% of German youths view violence positively. Among those without a German passport, the figure is 10%.

The criminologist has identified a crucial factor: "Family upbringing plays an important role in shaping attitudes toward violence, because these attitudes are formed in the home. If I experience violence in my own home, I will have a different relationship to violence than if I hadn't experienced it," said Prätor.

Germany is, however, on the right track, says Prätor, even if it doesn't always feel that way. "Fifty or sixty years ago, parental violence was much more common in child-rearing than it is today. Acts of violence committed by young people are also far less frequent than they were in the 1990s or the early 2000s."

Prätor emphasizes that it is important to bring objectivity to the debate. A good illustration of this is a look at police records of serious violent crimes — such as aggravated assault, manslaughter, or murder. "Of every 100 German citizens, 0.2% commit violent crimes; among non-German nationals, the figure is 0.6%."

However, Prätor points out that "more than 99% of the people living here in Germany — whether of German or non-German origin — the overwhelming majority in this country do not commit any serious violent crimes at all."

#1180. The Decades-Long Battle Within the Democratic Party Establishment Between the Heirs of Dean Acheson and Franklin Roosevelt

The calamitous history of America’s wars of choice.

Carden shows in The Great Betrayal: How the Democrats Became the Party of War, Truman and his hawkish secretary of state, Dean Acheson, steered the party in a direction not intended by Truman’s predecessor, Franklin D. Roosevelt. Truman and Acheson’s embrace of the burgeoning national security state and their adoption of the paranoiac anti-communism that heretofore had been characteristic of the American right led them headlong into an unnecessary war of choice in Korea. Korea set the precedent for Vietnam, for Iraq, and for Iran—it was the first of many wars waged in the absence of constitutional authorization on the flimsiest of pretexts.

The invasion of South Korea by North Korea’s Kim Il Sung provided Washington’s Cold War planners with just the story they needed at just the right time. North Korea launched its attack on June 24, and even then the Joint Chiefs of Staff warned that it would be “militarily unsound” for Washington to commit large forces “in an area of slight strategic importance.”

“If this was allowed to go unchallenged,” he continued, “it would mean a third world war, just as similar incidents had brought on a second world war.” In his seminal study on the “Origins of the Cold War,” the realist scholar Hans Morgenthau noted, “The misinterpretation of the North Korean aggression as part of a grand design at world conquest originating in and controlled by Moscow resulted in a drastic militarization of the cold war.” Indeed, Truman and Acheson viewed Asia solely through the prism of the Cold War. Dean Rusk, an Acheson protégé then serving as assistant secretary of state for the Far East, blithely dismissed China as a puppet of the Soviets. China, in Rusk’s view, was merely a “colonial Russian government, a Slavic Manchukuo on a large-scale.” Acheson also thought he saw Moscow’s hand behind Ho Chi Minh’s insurgency in Vietnam. By the time the new century dawned, Truman’s decision to drop the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki; his headlong rush into a Cold War with the Soviet Union; and his unconstitutional war of choice in Korea were all treated as acts of statesmanship by both popular historians and the Democratic elite. #1190. Tom Hayden’s Legacy and the New American Left

Standing in front of protesters on a misty day in Seattle, a 59-year-old Tom Hayden spoke into a bullhorn, declaring his hope. “I never thought the time would come that a new generation of activists would part the waters, the waters in which your idealism is supposed to be drowned.” The venerable activist, cofounder of Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), had spent almost two decades in the California State Legislature. But like other progressive legislators, he, too, was stymied by the conservative politics of the late 20th century. Earlier that day, tens of thousands of people—a historic coalition of union members, environmental activists, and anarchists—overwhelmed city officials and shut down the World Trade Organization (WTO) Ministerial Conference on November 30, 1999. It was the first major blow against the Clinton-era free-trade consensus.

The crowd repeated Hayden’s words through the “people’s mic” method and mirrored the then–state Senator’s visible giddiness toward the end of his remarks. “You have slowed the machinery of destruction down. But it can’t be about slowing the rate of destruction. It has to be about speeding the rate of creation of a new world, a better place.” Decades after he organized the landmark anti-war protests at the 1968 Democratic National Convention, Hayden was inspired by what he witnessed in Seattle. He could once again imagine a left that could win.

Tom Hayden passed away 10 years ago, on October 23, 2016, just weeks before Trump’s unexpected victory. As a result, his worst fears of an authoritarian right’s limiting the gains of the 1960s came true. Still, his life has become an even more relevant case study for those trying to figure out the most effective way to resist the current administration’s assault on democracy. As a lifelong insider-outsider, a Freedom Rider who later advised presidential candidates, Hayden was a passionate proponent of both radical direct action and electoral campaigns. Whether in the streets of American cities fighting back against ICE or in the offices of activist politicians like Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez or Mayor Zohran Mamdani, today’s progressives are finding creative ways to build on the work of Hayden’s generation. As memory of the Vietnam War era fades, a reexamination of ecumenical organizers like him will strengthen our understanding of the links between the victories and defeats of the past and today’s attempts to build a better democracy.

Born in 1939, Thomas Emmet Hayden was a product of middle-class America. Coming of age as the American left was decimated during the Red Scare, he did not consider himself political until he learned about the sit-in movement in 1960. He was first arrested as a Freedom Rider in Albany, Georgia, on December 10, 1961, the moment he began work on the first draft of SDS’s manifesto, the Port Huron Statement. Completed by Hayden and other founders of the organization the following June, the document promoted participatory democracy, offered a powerful rebuke of the era’s Cold War consensus, and soon became required reading for organizers for decades to come. What began as a modest network of student leaders would grow dramatically following the expansion of the war in Vietnam, with approximately 100,000 members at its peak in the late 1960s.

Hayden became one of the leading figures of the New Left, a figure whom many reporters treated as an entry point to a new age of radicalism. Together with other SDS leaders, he helped establish community organizing projects in several cities, traveled to Vietnam, where he once facilitated the release of three American POWs, and was put on trial for conspiracy to riot as part of the Chicago Eight. Over time, the times increasingly radicalized Hayden, who vacillated between calling for “Two, Three, Many Columbias” and clinging to his battered but lingering hope for mainstream politicians like Robert F. Kennedy during his presidential campaign.

However, Hayden’s work stretched far beyond the 1960s. Like other New Left leaders, he is often tied to a narrative of decline about the New Left’s rise and fall, centered on the well-documented implosion of SDS in 1969. The truth is that Hayden’s life tells a far more complicated and representative story of the decade’s activism, its ongoing influence on today’s progressives, and the porous relationship between radicals and reformers in America.

Unlike some high-profile leaders of his era, Hayden remained dedicated to movement work. Instead of burning out, he gradually shifted toward more conventional tactics in the early 1970s. Together with his then-wife Jane Fonda, he led the Indochina Peace Campaign, which helped sustain the anti-war movement’s political power and cut off funding for the Vietnam War. Following an insurgent Senate campaign, he and Fonda established the Campaign for Economic Democracy in California in 1977. By the early 1980s, CED had become a statewide organization that helped pass rent control initiatives and elect dozens of local and statewide officials. Hayden won a State Assembly seat in Santa Monica in 1982, serving a decade as a state representative, then another eight years as a state senator. Despite Republican governors’ limiting him for most of his time in Sacramento, Hayden still championed environmental reforms, including the Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986, and pushed for peace among street gangs in Los Angeles.

After he was termed out of the State Legislature, Hayden taught classes on social movements, joined The Nation as an editorial board member, and once again became a fixture at anti-war rallies and other meetings in progressive spaces. During his brief stint as a fellow at Harvard’s Institute of Politics, he led a series of workshops, “Activism Now!: Students, Sweatshops and Globalization,” that culminated in a trip where four students were arrested while documenting planned protests against the Free Trade Area of the Americas. After Occupy Wall Street, Hayden was in regular contact with new movement figures such as Debt Collective cofounder Astra Taylor after the two appeared on a panel together in 2014. “Good luck to Strike Debt. As your generation’s prospects decline and a new Dark Age rises, I do think you have a window of glory ahead though,” he wrote to Taylor. It was one of many interactions where Hayden cheered on a new generation shaped by the WTO protests, the post-9/11 wars, and the 2008 financial collapse.

In his final years, Hayden tried to situate the Obama presidency within a longer history of protest that contributed to his 2008 victory. He encouraged progressives to celebrate the best elements of the 44th president’s foreign policy (Cuba, Iran) while remaining critical of the war in Afghanistan and the administration’s tepid domestic agenda. He drew links between the Black Lives Matter movement and his work in Newark and lauded the lobbying efforts of the Dreamers. In 2016, Hayden infuriated many progressives when he backed Hillary Clinton over Bernie Sanders just days before the New York primary. He saw it as a pragmatic choice, rooted in fears of a Trump administration and the future of the Supreme Court. Those fears were legitimate and have proven prescient, but his endorsement underestimated the Sanders campaign’s potential impact.

If he were alive, he would celebrate that 2016 was a real turning point for the American left, when countless young organizers began to seriously engage in electoral politics. While he never would have predicted that the Democratic Socialists of America would become the post-2016 left’s primary beneficiary (even though he spoke at their annual convention in 2013), he would praise its electoral work, especially in cities like New York City, Chicago, and Los Angeles.

Much as he felt witnessing the Battle of Seattle in 1999, Hayden would have been ecstatic to see how a broad cross section of ordinary people has stood up to masked ICE agents occupying American cities over the past two years. The Minneapolis protests and general strike were, in many ways, the model many others from the 1960s generation had dreamed of for decades. The former community organizer would have been moved by how Minnesotans courageously faced down the president and won.

It is, in fact, a new dark age, but it has also benefited from those who built on the work of figures like Tom Hayden. He recognized the long battle but was also often driven by his desire to take urgent action to confront injustice. His story reflects that of many from his era who spent decades searching for different channels for progressives to win political power. Hayden had a searching mind, open to experimentation and adapting to history. Throughout it all, he thought that radical grassroots movements could merge with conventional politics to create a new democracy.

#1196. California must respond to DOJ's emergency request over ballots mailed to alleged noncitizens, court rules

The 9th Circuit ordered California to respond to an emergency Justice Department request seeking to set aside ballots tied to roughly 20,000 suspected noncitizens ahead of the midterm elections.

A federal appeals court ordered California to respond by noon Saturday to the Trump administration's emergency request involving roughly 20,000 mail-in ballots sent to people the Justice Department suspects are noncitizens ahead of the midterm elections.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit issued the order late Friday after the DOJ filed an emergency request earlier in the day seeking a ruling by Saturday.

The Justice Department sought emergency relief from the appeals court after U.S. District Judge David Carter did not rule by a deadline previously requested by the department.

The DOJ is asking California election officials to set aside ballots associated with individuals on its list while their eligibility is reviewed, rather than allowing the ballots to be processed alongside other votes.

In its emergency filing, the DOJ emphasized the urgency of its request.

"This is an emergency," the filing, submitted by Attorney General Todd Blanche and First Assistant U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli, stated.

"Every minute of processing that occurs without segregating the ballots of suspected non-eligible voters causes untraceable disenfranchisement and dilution of the votes of United States citizens," the filing continued.

Despite the Ninth Circuit's order, a hearing before Judge Carter remains scheduled for Tuesday, according to Fox News' Matt Finn. It remains unclear whether the appeals court will rule before that hearing.

#1199. Brazil’s Workers’ Party Is Not Stopping

With just a few days remaining before the runoff election, supporters of Lula’s party are doing everything possible to rally support behind the president.

Supporters of Brazil’s president and presidential candidate Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva of the Workers’ Party attend a campaign event in São Paulo, Brazil, on October 11, 2026, ahead of the presidential runoff election.

Rio de Janeiro—On Tuesday, October 6, at roughly 5 pm, people wearing red shirts, hats, and stickers started filling the Rio de Janeiro Bankers’ Union office in the center of the city. Still shaken by the results of the first round of Brazil’s elections that previous Sunday, Workers’ Party voters had been called to a meeting by newly elected congressman Marcelo Freixo to strategize the next steps of a national campaign to turn the elections around.

That Sunday, October 4, millions of Brazilians voted to elect seats in the chamber of deputies and the Senate, as well as state deputies, governors, and the president. Democratic socialist President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, a member of the Workers’ Party, was facing far-right candidate Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of Jair Bolsonaro, who is currently in jail for an attempted coup d’état in 2022. Contrary to the polls leading up to election day, which gave Lula a lead over the far-right candidate, Bolsonaro captured 1.87 percent of valid votes more than Lula, with 47 percent of total votes against Lula’s 45 percent. Since neither candidate reached over 50 percent of the vote, the race heads to a runoff on October 25. But even if Lula were to win this coming Sunday, Bolsonaro’s party, the Partido Liberal, swept the Senate, winning 28 of 54 available seats, giving the far right a majority in the legislative chamber on top of its control of the lower house.

A Bolsonaro win in the runoff would give unprecedented power to the far right in two of the three top branches of Brazil’s government. And in the 48 hours following Bolsonaro’s advantage in the first round, he announced plans to modify the country’s 1988 Constitution to “redemocratize the country,” proposing to abolish reelections and reform the Supreme Court, the last independent branch of government. The Supreme Court has been key in maintaining Brazilian democracy. But experts warn that a far-right majority in the Senate could be used to impeach and humiliate ministers who voted to convict Jair Bolsonaro. “It’s the end of the Lula era and we will be in power for decades,” Flávio said in a campaign event following the race.

The Lula era began in 2003, when he became president on a democratic socialist platform. He was elected again in 2006, with an approval rating of 52 percent. He won his third election in 2022, ousting far-right incumbent president Jair Bolsonaro. Lula’s governments were marked by a decline in poverty through welfare policies, expansion of investment in higher education, increased access to water and basic sanitation, the delivery of hundreds of thousands of public housing units, and the expansion of workers’ rights.

After the first round of voting, Lula and the Workers’ Party accused Meta of meddling with the 2026 elections through disinformation campaigns and the suppression of left-wing content across Meta’s social-media platforms. Hundreds protested outside of Meta’s office in São Paulo on Monday, and the company announced on Tuesday that is complying with an order from the electoral court, triggered by the Lula campaign’s accusation, “to give equal treatment in its platforms” to both candidates ahead of the runoff, reported the Associated Press.

As Lula campaign supporters race to activate voters ahead of Sunday’s runoff, legal experts remain concerned about Flávio’s intentions to change the Constitution. For Wallace Corbo, professor of constitutional law at the State University of Rio de Janeiro and the Getúlio Vargas Foundation, this proposal should be understood as a dog whistle confirming the likelihood of a return to far-right authoritarianism. “Substituting the 1988 Constitution in a context of the rise of authoritarian governments, with very low popular participation, is very problematic in terms of legitimacy, democracy, and rights for citizens and workers,” Corbo told The Nation, referencing the record number of people—33.4 million people, or 21 percent of the electorate—who did not vote in the October 4 elections. This low participation contrasts sharply with the support for the 1988 Constitution, which involved an overwhelming number of participants from the different historically marginalized groups, including Indigenous and Black people and women, becoming “the most democratic and participatory Constitution in our history,” after 21 years of a far-right military dictatorship supported by the United States, Corbo said, adding, “There’s a danger of its being effectively abolished or significantly disfigured, with the rollback of certain rights for the population.”

Additionally, a reform of the judiciary, as Bolsonaro has promised, might risk rolling back the limited abortion rights in the country. Abortion is largely banned in Brazil but allowed in the cases of rape, life endangerment, or when the fetus has been diagnosed with anencephaly—exceptions that have been protected by Supreme Court rulings. In 2025, for example, Justice Alexandre de Moraes blocked a ban on fetal asystole, a technique used to stop the heartbeat of a fetus before it’s removed from the uterus, which would have ended all abortions beyond 20 weeks in Brazil. “The judiciary is still one of the institutions that most firmly upholds established rights and resists certain setbacks—this could be lost, and it could completely change this paradigm if, for example, there were an expansion of seats on the Supreme Court,” said Angela Boldrini, a São Paulo–based journalist specializing in coverage of reproductive rights.

The next president will be able to name at least four new Supreme Court justices. If Bolsonaro wins, this would give him unprecedented power over the three branches of government. In the Senate, the risk is a conservative majority passing even more draconian anti-abortion legislation. “If we have a chamber of deputies, a Senate, and a president that align with Bolsonarism, we lose all the checks and balances that could, for example, lead to a veto or the overturning of a presidential veto,” added Boldrini.

In the Rio de Janeiro Bankers’ Union office earlier this month, around 2,000 people showed up to strategize ahead of the election runoffs. The sense of urgency was palpable in the room, as was the resolve to do everything possible to bolster Lula’s campaign. Lula is an original founder of the Workers’ Party and has dedicated his life to defending its principles, including the redistribution of wealth through welfare policies and taxing the rich.

“Each leftist militant here has to convince one person who didn’t vote to go vote,” said Alberes Lima, the Rio de Janeiro state president of the Workers’ Party. “We need to instill in people what a Bolsonaro government means. It means the end of workers’ rights, the end of a raise in the minimum salary; it means the end of many social programs that were built throughout many of your lives. Many things were built by people before us and we cannot make a mistake now.” With Brazil’s previous military dictatorship, which suppressed speech and violently persecuted leftists from 1964 to 1985, hanging in the air, Lima concluded, “We need to make a difference and we need to build something new so this bleak reality doesn’t fall upon Brazil again. Nineteen sixty-four, never again.”

While many on Brazil’s left are sounding the alarm on a possible dictatorship that is similar to that established by the US-backed coup d’état of 1964, Corbo says this is a new, more subtle version of a shift to an authoritarian government. “[The Bolsonaro family and their allies] didn’t succeed in installing a violent coup d’etat [in January 2023], so they are trying to install it under a semblance of normality through the control of the three powers of Brazilian democracy,” he said. “With a president controlling all of these powers [the judicial, the legislative, and the presidency], there will be creations of new mechanisms to weaken the opposition, weaken regulatory channels, and establish an authoritarian regime through normal institutional channels.”

But much like 1964, this authoritarian regime would function in service of the interest of the United States, and consequently, could have catastrophic consequences for the whole world.

Flávio and Trump met months before the election, and Flávio’s proposals contain 16 of the 21 demands Trump made of Brazil in October of last year, when the US imposed tariffs on the country. The demands include less regulation of American tech companies; no expansion of Brazil’s digital and free payment system, Pix; a “strategic and long-term partnership” with regards to Brazil’s rare minerals; and an alignment on deforestation of the Amazon, 60 percent of which is located in Brazil.

A Flávio presidential term presents an existential threat not only to Brazil but to a world currently dealing with the accelerating effects of climate change. With the far right running the Senate and Bolsonarista governors across Brazil acting in support of deforestation, the addition of a Flávio presidency would be devastating.

“Flávio Bolsonaro has shown that he will be just as willing as his father to dismantle protections for climate and nature and allow illegal miners, loggers and land grabbers to invade Indigenous territories,” Jonathan Watts wrote for The Guardian on October 7. “As a lawmaker, he backed a 2019 bill to eliminate requirements for landowners in the Amazon to conserve a proportion of native vegetation. In his manifesto, he completely ignored the climate crisis. And he has courted support from some of the most predatory landowners in the rainforest.”

On the ground, left-wing organizers—both inside and outside of the Workers’ Party—are doing everything possible to mobilize support for Lula and Brazilian democracy, hosting rallies and making their presence known. “We need discipline in a moment like this,” congressman Freixo said at the October 6 meeting. “We cannot make mistakes.”

Given that a significant number of voters did not make their way to the polls in the first round—the highest number of abstentions since 1998—the Workers’ Party is focused on finding and captivating these eligible voters to overtake Bolsonaro. They have just three days left to turn out those voters, in what Freixo called “the most important election of our lives.”
