#2653. Q&A: Why a pivotal IPCC meeting in Ethiopia ended in ‘profound disappointment’

Countries have failed – for the sixth time running – to agree on the timeline for publishing the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s (IPCC) next set of influential reports.

The IPCC’s 65th session (IPCC-65) was seen by many to be the final opportunity for countries to agree on a schedule where its seventh assessment report (AR7) would be published in time for the UN’s second “global stocktake” of climate action.

Before last week’s meeting in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, IPCC chair Prof Sir Jim Skea had warned that releasing the reports in time for the stocktake in 2028 would depend on a timeline being agreed by the end of 2026.

But the week-long meeting descended into fraught disagreements between governments, resulting in Skea “suspending” the talks on Friday evening with no decision made.

Countries were also unable to agree on the IPCC’s budget for the rest of the AR7 cycle, leaving the body with a “serious funding crisis”.

The IPCC has pledged to reconvene the meeting at the “earliest opportunity”; however, it has not yet given an indication of when this will be.

In his concluding remarks, Skea described the Addis Ababa summit as a “profound disappointment” and “among the most difficult IPCC meetings”, according to reporting from the Earth Negotiations Bulletin (ENB).

Some countries have reacted with dismay to the breakdown of negotiations, warning that a failure to have the IPCC’s latest assessments on climate adaptation and mitigation ready for the UN stocktaking exercise would undermine global climate action.

A climate envoy from a Pacific island state said it was a “travesty that a handful of countries continue to delay the IPCC’s reports – the science we need back home to help us save lives and livelihoods”.Below, Carbon Brief explores the key developments, debates and disagreements at the IPCC meeting.

Top of the agenda during the Addis Ababa meeting was a decision on the timeline of the publication of AR7’s three working group reports – also known as the “work plan”. The three reports cover climate science (WG1), impacts and adaptation (WG2) and mitigation (WG3).

Countries failed to reach a resolution on the scheduling of the influential reports at five consecutive IPCC meetings since 2024 – despite drafting being well underway.

(For more, see Carbon Brief’s write-up of discussions in Istanbul, Sofia, Hangzhou, Lima and Bangkok.)

Reporting from the ENB from inside these meetings has highlighted that countries are split, broadly, into two groups – those that want all three reports to be published in time for the UN’s second “global stocktake” in 2028, and those that one, or more, to be published afterwards.

A key pillar of the Paris Agreement, the global stocktake is a formal assessment of the world’s collective progress on tackling climate change. The exercise is designed to guide future global climate action, including decisions on national emissions targets and climate finance.

The second global stocktake is due to culminate in late 2028 at the COP33 climate summit.

The Addis Ababa meeting was the final IPCC plenary where countries would be able to agree on a stocktake-aligned timeline. In April, Skea told Carbon Brief that publishing reports by the end of 2028 would only be possible if a decision was taken before the end of 2026.

India, Kenya, Russia and Saudi Arabia have emerged as among the loudest voices calling for a timeline where the IPCC’s latest assessment of climate impacts and mitigation are published after the stocktake. They have argued that developing countries need more time to prepare, review and approve working group reports.

A coalition of developed nations and small-island developing states, meanwhile, have argued in favour of all three reports being published in time for the stocktake, so that the latest climate science can inform future climate action.

(For more on the standoff around the reports and why it matters for climate action, read Carbon Brief’s explainer.)

Over several days of fraught talks in Addis Ababa, the number of timeline options in contention ballooned to seven – before being reduced down to four.

On Monday, Skea established a “contact group”, chaired by Brazil and Ireland, to focus on the timeline issue.

By Friday morning, the group reported back to the plenary that they were still a “long way from consensus”, according to the ENB.

By that afternoon, four options still remained – the three set out by the IPCC ahead of the meeting and a fourth, proposed by Haiti, which would see WG1 approval in April 2028, WG2 in July 2028 and WG3 in October 2028.

The IPCC’s secretariat subsequently presented a “draft decision” for the timeline issue to plenary, informed by the contact group’s work. The text did not establish a formal decision on the timeline, but presented a number of options for how the IPCC could move forward on the issue, as well as proposals for addressing inclusivity concerns.

Two out of three options suggested that IPCC co-chairs continue work on reports in 2027 “without prejudging the panel’s agreement on the timing of the respective approval sessions” and for timeline discussions to be continued at a future session.

Another suggestion was that the IPCC fund a second delegate for developing countries and “economies in transition” for future approval sessions.

After one delegation said they were uncertain how to engage with the draft text, Skea clarified the text had three elements – a preamble, timeline and a section on inclusivity and avenues for providing input.

He said that “it was clear the panel would not be able to take a decision on the timeline at IPCC-65” and explained the idea was to include the four timeline options that emerged from the contact group in an annex “in order to capture progress made during IPCC-65”.

After countries were not able to agree on the draft decision, Skea suspended the meeting at 8:29pm on Friday.

He noted that it would no longer be inclusive for the meeting to continue, given that a number of delegations from developing countries had left.

Earlier in the plenary, Antigua and Barbuda’s delegation had emphasised that 6pm was its deadline and noted that representatives of other small-island developing states had departed. Nepal’s negotiating team had also stated it was leaving.

The ENB’s report details how the five-day meet was beset by disagreements between country delegations on the timeline issue.

As proceedings kicked off on Monday, a significant number of countries set out their position on whether publication of the reports should occur before, or after, the global stocktake.

Both camps cited inclusivity concerns. Those in favour of a stocktake-aligned timeline said that “inclusivity is a priority and should be addressed, but said delaying delivery of the report would further disadvantage the countries most vulnerable to climate change”, according to the ENB.

In discussions on Monday, the Pacific island nation of Vanuatu underscored that “every year of delay is a year in which communities on the frontlines bear losses that cannot be measured or claimed”, the ENB says. The delegation pointed to the International Court of Justice’s 2025 advisory opinion that countries have binding obligations to protect the climate.

The Netherlands delegation said it was “inconceivable” that two years before the conclusion of second global stocktake, the “body created to produce scientific advice accepts it will not deliver working group reports on mitigation and adaption, while the [IPCC co-chairs] say it is possible”, according to the ENB.

But other countries emphasised that the global stocktake could draw from other sources of science if the reports were not ready in time, the ENB notes.

China’s delegation opposed the IPCC holding more than three plenaries each year and said IPCC reports did not need to be linked to the global stocktake. Kenya said that publishing reports after the stocktake “did not mean science would not inform policy”.

Also among those to argue for a longer timeline were Iran and Iraq, with the latter underscoring the importance of participating in IPCC processes on an “equal footing” with other governments, the ENB says.

There were also disagreements over the specific mandate of the contact group set up by Skea on Monday to advance the timeline question. This debate endured well into the end of the week and days after the contact group had started its work.

Countries pushed back on each others’ characterisation of the different timeline options. Proponents for a longer timeline described plans where reports were published in 2028 as “rushed”, whereas those who wanted all reports published in time for the stocktake referred to the longer timeline as “delayed” or “significantly extended”, according to the ENB.

Countries also failed to reach consensus on the draft timeline decision presented to plenary on the last day of the meeting.

While some countries – including Antigua and Barbuda, Costa Rica, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Kiribati, Marshall Islands, Mexico, the UK and Uruguay – detailed their preference for particular passages of the IPCC secretariat’s text, others rejected it outright.

For example, Algeria, India, Iraq and Saudi Arabia stated the draft text could not serve as a basis for work or decisions, according to ENB. India, meanwhile, argued that it could not engage in the decision as the contact group reached “no clear conclusion”.

In an analysis of developments at IPCC-65, the ENB states that discussions in Ethiopia had revealed “room to compromise” between countries.

It continues that “most countries” in attendance had stressed the importance of not deviating from a tradition where the IPCC follows the advice of working group co-chairs when it comes to the schedule for production of assessment reports.

In this case, that would mean agreeing to publish all three working group reports in 2028 “on a timeline that the co-chairs consider feasible and meets all the criteria for IPCC reports” (options one and two in table above).

In a statement shared with Carbon Brief after the meeting, Marshall Islands climate envoy Tina Stege said that it was a “travesty that a handful of countries continue to delay the IPCC’s reports – the science we need back home to help us save lives and livelihoods”. She continued:

“We were here to work, but found every proposal dismissed – on inclusivity, on the relevance of the science for our national planning, on the necessity of evidence to inform the next global stocktake. Progress was blocked, down to the end – a shameful play for time when there is no time to waste.”

Hamid Abakar Souleymane, a negotiator from Chad, tells Carbon Brief the situation was “not helping” countries such as his, which are on the “frontlines” of climate change.

The African nation faces a host of climate impacts, including drought, heatwaves, severe rainfall, desertification and biodiversity loss, he says. As a result, it wants AR7 reports to be ready by the global stocktake so that “[climate] mitigation action can be accelerated”. He continues:

“It is only about two or three countries that are opposing every single element of the agenda – and this is really disappointing. We are in a very uncomfortable situation now because the global stocktake is coming. We don’t have a timeline [for reports] – and we haven’t yet adopted a budget.”

Abakar Souleyame also notes that Chad was at a disadvantage during negotiations because it was only able to send one representative to the meeting:

“A lot of meetings take place at the same time…The participation of a single delegate puts us in a very uncomfortable situation where we cannot contribute adequately to the ongoing process.”

Inextricably linked with the AR7 timeline is the IPCC’s budget, which – among other things – allocates money for its author meetings and approval sessions, including support for experts from developing countries.

Throughout the meeting in Addis Ababa, governments and the IPCC leadership repeatedly emphasised that the IPCC is “facing a serious funding crisis”, the ENB says.

At the start of the meeting, the IPCC’s secretariat presented a proposed budget for 2026-29, which would cover the core work of AR7. This was submitted “against the backdrop of growing annual deficits and a progressively declining cash balance, raising significant concerns about the financial sustainability of the IPCC trust fund”, the document says.

The trust fund is made up of ongoing contributions from its parent organisations, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and UN Environment Programme (UNEP), in addition to voluntary contributions from its member governments and the UNFCCC.

The budget proposal notes that an “insufficient level of voluntary contributions” now “risks the successful completion of the AR7 cycle”.

Contributions in 2025 were 5.3m Swiss francs (£4.8m) lower than 2023, while costs have risen, the secretariat notes. As of the start of July, just 16 countries and organisations had contributed in 2026, totalling 2.8m Swiss francs (£2.6m).

These donors include the EU, UK, WMO, UNFCCC and UNEP, as well as Australia, Canada, Hungary, Luxembourg, New Zealand, Norway, Peru, South Africa, Sweden, Switzerland and Trinidad and Tobago.

Despite being its largest donor historically, the US has not contributed financially to the IPCC since 2024 – before the second term of US president Donald Trump.

According to its proposed budget, the secretariat says it needs approximately 33m Swiss francs (£30m) to complete AR7 – equivalent to just over 8m Swiss francs (£7.2m) per year. At the level of contributions received in 2025, the IPCC trust fund would be “depleted by the end of 2028, preventing the IPCC from delivering the AR7 reports”, the document warns.

As a minimum, the IPCC needs 5.8m Swiss francs (£5.3m) per year in contributions to “enable the successful completion of the cycle”, the secretariat adds, although this would leave “no financial buffer for unforeseen circumstances”.

At the meeting, “many countries expressed concern” about the IPCC’s financial situation and “called for existing donors to increase their contributions”, says the ENB.

Iceland and Ireland said they “anticipated” making contributions soon, the ENB says, while others – including Azerbaijan, China, Finland and the EU – “assured” the IPCC of their continued support.

However, according to the ENB summary, the meeting struggled to agree on a number of points, including whether budget discussions should be paused until a timeline for AR7 had been agreed, and the work of the “Financial Task Team” (FiTT).

An FiTT is assembled for each assessment cycle for “reviewing income and expenditures, assisting in preparing the budget proposals and developing other recommendations related to finance for consideration by the panel”, according to the IPCC.

The IPCC established the FiTT for AR7 at its 60th session in Istanbul in January 2024. It is co-chaired by Kenya and Australia, with core team members from the Bahamas, Norway, Saudi Arabia and the US. Other government representatives can also participate.

Midway through the meeting, FiTT co-chair Patricia Nying’uro from Kenya reported to the plenary that they had been “unable to advance” on reviewing the budgets beyond 2026, with disagreements on whether “budget lines should remain or be deleted”, reports the ENB.

The attempts to remove whole budget lines drew expressions of “deep concern and disappointment” from other countries, the ENB says, with many noting that the work of the FiTT is “purely technical and has never been controversial”.

Belize, France, Germany, Panama and the Netherlands specified that “two delegations have threatened to have no budget if their conditions are not met”, the ENB says, with even budget lines for activities already agreed being “taken hostage”.

The ENB notes that both Saudi Arabia and India expressed concern that certain lines in the 2027 budget “favoured one option” for the AR7 timeline. Both countries opposed using the proposed budget as a basis for discussion in the FiTT, the ENB says.

The ENB quotes Prof Skea, who noted that most of the budget lines apply to all options for the timeline and was puzzled that members seemed to “disagree about things they don’t disagree about”.

A way forward was found for the FiTT to continue its discussions – despite Saudi Arabia’s accusing the secretariat of “blatant bias”, says the ENB. However, further disagreements arose over budget lines for approval meetings in 2027 to extend financial support for developing countries to bring two representatives rather than one.

Skea noted that despite “almost universal” support for increased participation of developing countries in IPCC sessions, the plenary discussions had continued for two and a half hours without agreement. He asked the timeline contact group to take up the issue.

Discussions on the rest of the budget continued in the FiTT, but “there were no further reports to plenary” before the meeting was suspended, the ENB says.

In the evening of the meeting’s final day, with no agreement on the timeline, Skea asked delegates to consider approving the proposed budgets for 2026 and 2027 to allow work on AR7 to continue. According to the ENB, Saudi Arabia, India and Russia objected.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo warned that the delay to agreeing the budgets was an “immediate danger to the viability” of the panel.

According to the ENB, Skea said in his closing address that if there were stronger words than “profound disappointment,” he would use them.

He noted that recent months had been “some of the most turbulent for the panel” and characterised IPCC-65 as “among the most difficult IPCC meetings”, it says.

It remains to be seen when discussions on the AR7 timeline and budget will restart.

A statement from Skea released after the meeting detailed plans to reconvene the plenary “at the earliest opportunity so the panel can effectively conclude its business”.

The IPCC declined Carbon Brief’s request for an interview with Skea, with a spokesperson noting it could not “discuss a plenary that had not yet finished its work”.

On Monday after the meeting, nearly 120 scientists currently drafting the AR7 reports had signed an open letter where they pledged to “keep working” on the reports. By Wednesday, more than 200 scientists had added their names.

The report authors said they remained committed to delivering the assessments “in a manner that is rigorous, timely and inclusive”. The letter also stresses the “world should not have to face” the second global stocktake without the IPCC’s latest reports.

In a statement, Dr Bill Hare, CEO of Climate Analytics, called for discussions to be resumed this year. He said:

“Urgent agreement is now needed to give planning security to the voluntary [report] authors, more than half of which are from developing countries and to safeguard a timeline that sees all WG reports published in 2028, in time to inform the second global stocktake.

“A resumed IPCC-65 session still this year, whichever way possible, is essential to at the very least agree to the IPCC’s 2027 budget to allow work to continue.”

Prof Fabrice Lambert, a contributing author of WG1 and Chilean government delegate, wrote on LinkedIn that there was now a “clear need for professional negotiators and experienced facilitators” from UN bodies to help organise and run panel sessions. He continued:

“Discussions have moved too far beyond the scientific questions that should be the IPCC’s mainstay. Bringing in that expertise could facilitate breakthroughs that have eluded us for so many sessions during AR7.”

The IPCC has also not yet confirmed the date and location of its next plenary session, IPCC-66.

After every IPCC meeting, the organisation writes a report summarising the discussion.  However, the IPCC has failed to sign off on the reports for its past four sessions.

The first item on the agenda for last week’s IPCC meeting was the approval of these four draft reports. To open the discussion on Monday, Skea proposed developing guidelines for producing these meeting reports, the ENB says.

Belgium, France and the Netherlands suggested publishing full transcripts of the meetings in addition to the meeting reports. Panama supported the idea, “emphasising the need for transparency”, the ENB says. However, it adds that Saudi Arabia opposed the suggestion.

Saudi Arabia said it objected to the “highly selective manner” in which the draft meeting reports outlined certain topics in more detail than others, arguing that this “distorted [their] balance”.

Meanwhile, Belgium and Germany opposed the use of the passive voice in the meeting reports, arguing this gave an impression that all opinions are “equally supported”. They said that this resulted in a “skewed way of presenting discussions”, the ENB says.

IPCC vice-chair Prof Diana Ürge-Vorsatz led a huddle to continue the discussion. She reported back at the end of the week that the group had reached a “preliminary agreement” for the “principles for producing meeting reports”, the ENB notes:

“She said the discussion focused on how to record diverging views, as some panel members felt separating out two or three countries invited unwelcome attention or misinterpretation.

“Ürge-Vorsatz said the huddle agreed to group arguments, first identifying countries representing one perspective and elaborating on their argument, then identifying countries representing the other side of arguments. The secretariat will decide on the granularity of reporting.”

The panel requested that the IPCC secretariat prepare revised draft meeting notes in accordance with the new guidance. It decided to “submit the revised draft reports for consideration by the panel at its next working session”.

The IPCC also agreed to defer consideration of a review of its principles and procedures to a future meeting, after India, Iran, Russia and Saudi Arabia argued that there was not enough time to discuss the issue thoroughly, the ENB reports.

These documents are typically reviewed every five years. However, the last review was delayed due to the Covid pandemic.

The co-chairs of each AR7 working group updated governments on how the reports themselves were progressing.

All co-chairs highlighted their working group’s contribution to the special report on climate change and cities, scheduled to be published in 2027.

WG2 co-chairs Prof Winston Chow and Prof Bart van den Hurk explained to the conference floor that the second order draft of the special report was circulated for review earlier this year and received more than 25,000 comments. They added that the fourth lead author meeting was held in India in August and a final draft version is currently being prepared.

In his closing address on Friday, Skea said the progress reports presented by working-group chairs were evidence of the “ongoing achievements” of the IPCC, according to the ENB.

Noting that the IPCC’s scientific work was progressing, Skea reportedly stated that, despite challenges on the timeline issue, “the engine room of the IPCC is working – and it’s healthy”.

The sentiment was echoed by WG1 co-chair Dr Robert Vautard, who wrote on LinkedIn after the meeting that disagreements across country delegations on the AR7 timeline were “so disconnected from the actual collaborative spirit across authors in the report making”.

Nevertheless, the ENB’s meeting summary cautions that the ongoing inability of governments to reach agreement on “critical issues” was now “spilling over to authors”, with “serious potential consequences both for the IPCC and for the science the world depends on”.

It continues that, “while the work of scientists and governments is separate, the friction that has characterised AR7 plenary sessions is now directly affecting the ability of the scientists to carry out their work”.

On Tuesday afternoon, Mxolisi Shongwe, programme officer at the IPCC secretariat, told the plenary that a workshop on IPCC “methods of assessment” was held in February 2026, which featured discussions on artificial intelligence (AI).

He added that the IPCC bureau – made up managerial roles including the IPCC chair, vice-chair and co-chairs of working groups – decided to establish an “informal group” on AI that would provide advice on the “responsible use of AI and emerging digital technologies in IPCC processes”, reports the ENB.

India and Russia “expressed concerns about lack of transparency in [the] use of AI in scientific research”, according to the ENB. Burundi and Iran highlighted the need for disclosure and human oversight in AI-produced material, while Congo asked how AI has so far been used by the IPCC.

After discussions about the terms of reference for the informal group on AI, the panel agreed that any guidance or recommendations that it produced will be “subject to subsequent confirmation by the panel”, reports the ENB.

Separately, vice-chair Ürge-Vorsatz reported on the progress of the IPCC’s gender action team, explaining that the group had finalised the report of the IPCC expert meeting on gender, diversity, equity and inclusivity.

The gender action team “aims to enhance gender equality in IPCC processes through monitoring, accounting for gender in decisions, including diversity in nominations and appointment”, according to the IPCC website.

Dozens of countries commented on the update. Luxembourg, supported by nine other countries, invited the panel to consider expanding the mandate of the gender action team to include “gender diversity, equity and inclusion”, reports the ENB.

However, Saudi Arabia opposed expanding the mandate of the gender action team, arguing it risked “diluting” the team’s work.

To close the discussion on Tuesday, Skea suggested the gender action team consider expanding its mandate and bring suggestions to IPCC-66.

#2654. World Cup Football Science: Kick Off Summer with These Awesome STEM Challenges

The 2026 Football World Cup might be coming to an end, but football is ever popular in my house, so here are Four Football World Cup STEM Challenges for you to try this summer.

All the activities use very basic materials, and I’ve included free printable templates for the reaction time test and goalkeeper game. You’ll also find an online version of the goalie game and a calculator to work out how fast you can kick a football!

Grab some balls, a pen and paper and explore the world of football like never before.

Find a few different balls to test, for example, a tennis ball, a football, and a table tennis ball. Take the balls outside and draw a goal line on the ground using chalk (or use a goal if you have one), and a start line about 2 metres from the goal line.

Count how many breaths it takes to push each ball across the goal line by blowing down a straw.

Put all the balls back at the start line and try to gently kick each one into the goal, using the same amount of force.

Which ball is easiest to move using just your breath?

Which ball moves the furthest when you kick it?

Kids should notice that the heavier balls take more force to move than the lighter balls.

Reaction time is important for football. The split second it takes for a player’s brain to process what’s happening and then move can be the difference between winning and losing a match.

You can test your reaction time with a ruler or my handy template.

The best shooters can kick a football at speeds up to 80mph! At this speed, a ball travels 10m in just 0.28 seconds! 0.28 seconds is about the same time it takes to blink!

Work out how fast you can kick with this easy activity.

Measure a start and finish point 10m apart.

Ask a friend to hold the phone with the start and finish line visible.

Kick the ball while the phone is recording, use slo-mo mode if you can.

Use the video to determine the time it takes for the ball to move between the two lines.

Use the calculator below to work out the speed of your kick.

Experiment with a stationary kick, a run up kick and using your dominant and non-dominant leg.

Goalkeepers don't always have time to see which way a ball is going, so they have to make a decision which way to jump in a split second. This activity shows just how tricky making that choice can be.

Assign the role of striker and goalkeeper to each player.

Cut out the striker and goalkeeper squares and the goal.

The goalkeeper puts their square on one side of the goal while the striker flips the coin.

If the goalie is on the correct side of the goal, it's a save!

I've created a paper and an online version of the game, so choose whichever suits you best!

#2657. What Is Driving the Global Resurgence of Mangrove Forests?

Mangroves on the island of Mauritius in the Indian Ocean. Adam Burton

After decades of losses, mangrove trees are gaining ground on coastlines around the world, often in places they have never grown before. Paradoxically, scientists say, warming temperatures and the destruction of inland forests are helping to power the revival.

From the bayous of Louisiana to the backwaters of the Irrawaddy in Myanmar, from the lagoons of West Africa to the billabongs of northern Australia, mangroves are making a dramatic and unexpected comeback.

What a difference a couple of decades can make.

In 2007, ecologists from six countries asked in a letter published in the journal Science whether we would soon be living in “a world without mangroves.” One of the world’s most distinctive, biodiverse, and carbon-rich ecosystems was in rapid retreat in the face of encroaching agriculture, construction of sea walls, ports, and marinas, and rising sea levels. The mangroves seemed doomed.

But in recent months two separate studies have painted a very different picture. A high-resolution analysis of satellite imagery published in Science in June found that the stunted, salt-tolerant trees are now undergoing an “unexpected expansion,” healing after decades of destruction.

At the same time, a report on the State of the World’s Mangroves, published in September by a group of NGOs under the umbrella of the Global Mangrove Alliance, found that “mangroves are now increasing in extent, year on year,” said coauthor Mark Spalding of The Nature Conservancy.

It’s not that the destruction of mangroves has ended. But the Science study finds that new mangrove growth between 2010 and 2023 covered approximately 3,200 square miles, outpacing losses of around 2,400 square miles. Worldwide, mangrove cover is now back to the levels last seen in the 1980s.

The new findings are the surprising outcome of the most detailed analysis yet attempted of satellite imagery of mangroves over the past four decades, says Pete Bunting, a remote sensing analyst at Aberystwyth University in Wales, who worked on both studies. It shows that previous assessments had provided “a substantial underestimation of mangrove gains” over the past 15 years, says the Science study’s lead author Zhen Zhang of Tulane University in Louisiana.

“For the first time, these unique ecosystems are expanding at a faster rate than they are declining,” says Catherine Lovelock, a coastal ecologist at the University of Queensland, who was not involved in either study.

However, not all ecologists are celebrating.

For one thing, mangroves are recovering in some places thanks to environmental degradation elsewhere. For another, says Norman Duke, an ecologist at James Cook University in Queensland, Australia, and lead author of the 2007 letter, mangrove quantity should not be confused with quality. Lost old-growth mangrove forests, which harbor the most wildlife, are being replaced by less biodiverse and resilient new stands, he says. Contrary to the new studies, “the world’s mangroves are not bouncing back.”

A coral reef around the roots of mangrove trees in Raja Ampat, Indonesia. Wendy Mitchell / Mangrove Photography Awards 2026

Mangroves are unique trees, evolved with specialized roots that anchor in shallow, intertidal waters with slow currents, no frost, and plenty of sediment. Mangrove forests are critical biodiversity hotspots, supporting everything from barnacles to tigers, and are vital fish nurseries for the oceans. For coastal communities, they yield rich harvests of crabs and shellfish and form a protective barrier, absorbing winds and tidal surges during storms, and even moderating the energy of tsunamis. They also store large amounts of carbon — typically four times more than nearby tropical rainforests.

Zhang has recorded the biggest revivals in the river estuaries of northern Australia and river deltas in India. In each case, the new growth extends across more than 450 square miles. But the most surprising turnaround has been in Southeast Asian countries, which were once the scene of rapid mangrove destruction. Since 2010, they have accounted for more than 40 percent of global gains.

Myanmar is “still the most severely deforested country among major mangrove-holding nations,” says Zhang. But since 2010, its mangroves have grown in area by 10 percent. Indonesia tells a similar story. After major losses in past decades, due primarily to a boom in shrimp and fish aquaculture, mangroves have been stable since 2005, when the boom began to abate. The country remains home to around a quarter of the world’s mangroves.

So, what is going on? Why the dramatic upturn for a global ecosystem once considered all but doomed by human invasion and rising seas? The new remote-sensing analysis, despite its greater resolution, allows only tentative conclusions. “We don’t fully understand what is driving these gains,” says Bunting. His mapping cannot distinguish planted trees from natural recovery, for instance. But there is growing evidence that natural regeneration is powering the resurgence.

Sometimes, mangroves are recolonizing areas previously taken by humans but since abandoned. During the late 20th century, coastal communities across the tropics ripped out millions of acres of mangroves in East and Southeast Asia alone to make way for ponds where they raised fish and shrimp for lucrative international markets. But many of these ponds soon became unproductive through disease, pollution, or poor management, and were abandoned.

But such recolonization is only part of the story. “Some 65 percent of mangrove gains are in new places where mangroves have not grown before,” the State of the World’s Mangroves report finds. Sometimes climate change is helping extend the range of mangroves polewards. On the Mississippi Delta, they are overtaking salt marshes as warmer winters eliminate mangrove-killing frosts, restricting habitat for birds and foraging mammals that need open ground.

False-color satellite images show the growth of mangroves at the mouth of the Matamuhuri River in Bangladesh between 1990 and 2023. Source: Landsat imagery. Courtesy of Pete Bunting

The majority of the gains, however, “seem to occur in river mouths and deltas and are being driven by sediment coming off the land,” says Bunting. On reaching the ocean, the sediment settles, creating mudbanks that mangroves swiftly colonize. The unsettling paradox is that, in many cases, mangroves along coastlines are returning thanks to the destruction of their forest counterparts inland. The loss of trees and their roots destabilizes soils, causing erosion and the flow of sediment into rivers.

That is what has happened along the Yangon estuary in southern Myanmar. Part of the giant Irrawaddy Delta, the estuary stretches some 25 miles from the ocean to the country’s biggest city, Yangon. The estuary lost more than half its mangroves between 1988 and 2011 as rice farmers moved onto its shores and replaced mangroves with rice fields, erecting dikes to protect them from the salty water. But the mangroves have since made a dramatic comeback. By 2023, their coverage had increased almost tenfold to more than 13,000 acres, according to Phyu Phway Thant of the Myanmar Maritime University in Thanlyin, who has mapped the growth from satellite imagery.

The rice fields have not gone. Instead, almost all the growth was mangroves colonizing new mudbanks created from sediment dislodged by upstream sand mining and deforestation, which was driven by illegal logging and the expansion of agriculture along tributaries of the Yangon River.

This is not unusual. The growth of mudbanks in many estuaries has coincided with an increase in sediment brought down by rivers to coastal waters over the last 20 years, says the State of the World’s Mangroves report. Other examples include parts of the giant Amazon Delta in Brazil, the shores of peninsular Malaysia, and the North Island of New Zealand, which is at the southernmost limit of potential mangrove habitat.

A more troubling picture of sea level rise is coming into view. Read more.

“Rising sea levels should in theory restrict mangrove growth, as seas invade the intertidal areas where they grow,” says Lovelock, but at the mouths of many rivers, sediment deposition is creating land faster than the seas are engulfing it, creating new habitats that mangroves swiftly colonize. And once established, the dense networks of mangrove roots trap more sediment — enough, the State of the World’s Mangroves report concludes, to keep pace with current rising tides in many places.

Of course, this positive feedback loop can be confounded by upstream dams, which drastically reduce the amount of sediment being carried toward the ocean, stopping the growth of intertidal mud flats, and causing deltas to be drowned.

Shrimp farms in Prachuap Khiri Khan province in Thailand have displaced mangrove forests. Ingram Publishing

For this reason, there are serious concerns among ecologists about the fate of East Africa’s largest expanse of mangroves, on the delta of the River Rufiji in Tanzania. In recent years, new mangrove stands have grown on mudflats in the outer delta, says Bunting. But that growth could now abruptly halt. In August, the Tanzanian government completed the Julius Nyerere hydroelectric dam some 100 miles upstream. The environmental group WWF has estimated that the dam will trap most of the 16.6 million tons of sediment flowing down the river each year — initiating a cycle of delta retreat and mangrove loss.

The growth of new mangrove stands has been accompanied by a growing awareness among some governments and coastal communities of their value. This is especially true in Indonesia and other countries bordering the Indian Ocean that were hit by a tsunami in 2004 that killed more than 200,000 people. Villages that had kept their mangroves were much better protected than those that had converted them to fishponds.

A resulting fashion for planting new mangroves has had mixed results, however. Tens of millions of dollars have been spent on planting mangroves across the tropics in recent years, according to the NGO Wetlands International. But “the majority of these restoration projects have failed,” it said in a recent report. More than 80 percent of seedlings have died or been washed away by the tides.

In response, some conservation groups have begun helping communities manage coastal landscapes to encourage natural regeneration. On the north shore of Java, villagers have erected some 10 miles of near-shore brushwood barriers to create calm water where seedlings cast off by mangroves have become established.

And in the West African state of Guinea-Bissau, Wetlands International is recruiting locals to encourage the natural return of mangroves to abandoned rice fields. “We open up dikes and clean the old natural canals” to allow water carrying mangrove seedlings from nearby stands to return, says Abdoulaye Ndiaye, who runs the program. Nature does the rest. More than 6,000 acres have been restored since 2015.

Meanwhile, the greener landscapes seen in satellite images do not necessarily indicate that biodiversity has recovered, says Uday Pimple of King Mongkut’s University of Technology in Bangkok, who has been doing extensive field research in the mangrove swamps of Thailand.

In Phang Nga province in western Thailand — an area where tin mining, charcoal burning, and shrimp farming previously decimated coastal mangroves — he found that 90 percent of mangrove recovery since the 1980s has been through the natural regeneration of a range of mangrove species, creating biodiverse forest. But restored mangrove forests in Trat province in eastern Thailand, which appear identical on satellite images, were largely planted with a single species of mangrove, resulting in much less biodiversity.

A man plants mangrove seedlings in Banda Aceh, Indonesia. Hand-planted mangroves rarely reach maturity. Chaideer Mahyuddin / AFP via Getty Images

Clearly, the prognosis for mangroves remains contested. Bunting says, “It is quite possible that the future trend will be similar to the last 15 years,” with gains continuing to exceed losses. And in a study of the likely fate of mangroves in a world 2 degrees Celsius warmer, Daniel Friess, a colleague of Zhang at Tulane University, found reasons to believe the trees could thrive. Because, at least in some places, they seem well able to keep pace with rising sea levels. Because increased rainfall will often increase soil erosion inland and sediment deposition along shorelines, giving mangroves even more room to expand. And because warmer temperatures should push mangroves further outside the tropics, replacing many more salt marshes in Australia and the southern U.S., for instance, for better or for worse.

But Duke argues that the new mangrove stands colonizing the tropics may not be as resilient as their predecessors to the more severe weather triggered by climate change. Or as useful in capturing and holding onto carbon. “They are immature stands, not fully developed in either biomass or biodiversity,” he says. That will leave them ever more vulnerable “as damaging [weather] events become more severe and more frequent.” Recent research appears to support his pessimistic presumption.

Field studies in China’s Hainan province found that mature mangrove forests recovered much more quickly from tropical cyclones, with their high winds, strong rainfall, and storm surges, compared with younger forests. The researchers stressed the importance of protecting old-growth mangroves rather than relying on new stands to replace them. Without such a strategy, humanity may yet face what Duke feared: “a world without mangroves.”

#2661. Dental implants can hijack your immune system, scientists discover

For tens of millions of people, dental implants have provided a way to replace missing teeth with a full set of fixed, functional teeth rather than removable dentures. However, an estimated 10% to 20% of implant patients eventually develop peri-implantitis, a serious infection that damages the surrounding jawbone.

Antibiotics frequently fail to control this condition, and scientists have struggled to understand why. Now, researchers believe they have uncovered the mechanism responsible.

A new study published in PNAS Nexus by scientists at the Rutgers School of Dental Medicine reveals that bacteria can gradually corrode dental implants, releasing microscopic titanium particles into nearby tissues. These particles interfere with the immune cells responsible for fighting infection, trapping them in a state of excessive inflammation that progressively destroys jawbone.

The researchers examined human tissue samples, studied human immune cells in the laboratory, and conducted experiments using genetically engineered mice. Their investigation identified a calcium channel in macrophages, the immune cells that engulf bacteria, that becomes activated by titanium particles. When this channel was disabled in mice, the disease was prevented.

This discovery provides the first credible drug target for a condition that affects as many as one in five dental implant recipients and costs healthcare systems worldwide more than $1 billion annually.

"For the first time, we show why all the antibiotic treatments that work around teeth do not work around implants," said Georgios Kotsakis, senior author of the study and assistant dean for clinical research at Rutgers School of Dental Medicine. "Now that we know the cause, we can start developing therapeutics."

Why Dental Implant Infections Resist Antibiotics

One of the biggest mysteries surrounding peri-implantitis has been its similarity to periodontitis, the gum disease that develops around natural teeth. Both conditions begin with the same types of bacteria found in the mouth, yet they respond very differently to treatment.

For natural teeth, antibiotics combined with routine professional cleaning can resolve the infection. Around dental implants, however, the same medications are successful in fewer than half of cases. Even when treatment is attempted, the underlying jawbone may continue to deteriorate.

For the past two decades, scientists have largely concentrated on the bacteria responsible for these infections. Researchers in Kotsakis' laboratory decided to investigate another possible contributor: the implant material itself.

Their work showed that bacteria accumulating on implant surfaces create acidic biofilms that slowly damage the titanium. Over time, this corrosion can release billions of microscopic metal particles, each smaller than a red blood cell.

Titanium particles can also be dislodged during routine dental cleaning. This is particularly concerning when instruments designed for natural teeth are used on implants, because they can scrape and damage the metal surface.

How Titanium Particles Hijack the Immune System

Once released into the gums, the tiny titanium fragments attract a bacterial toxin known as lipopolysaccharide, which coats their surfaces. This combination creates a deceptive target for the immune system, making the particles resemble unusually large bacteria that cannot be broken down.

Macrophages, the white blood cells responsible for engulfing and destroying harmful microorganisms, attempt to consume these particles. But because the cells cannot digest titanium, they become stuck in a prolonged state of excessive inflammation.

As a result, the macrophages produce large quantities of inflammatory signaling molecules, including interleukin-1 beta. This protein is also associated with inflammatory processes involved in rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's disease.

The resulting immune response gradually breaks down the bone supporting the dental implant. At the same time, the macrophages become less effective at fighting the bacteria that triggered the infection in the first place.

Laboratory experiments revealed just how severely their defenses were disrupted. Macrophages exposed to titanium particles engulfed fewer than half as many bacteria as immune cells that had not encountered the particles.

"These particles are little magnets that attract the bacterial toxin, and they hijack the immune system, preventing it from clearing bacteria," said Kotsakis. "You have a perfect storm that defies antibiotics."

Scientists Identify a Potential New Treatment Target

After uncovering how titanium particles disrupt immune defenses, the researchers investigated the molecular pathway responsible for the damaging reaction.

They traced the process to TRPC1, a calcium channel (a specialized, pore-forming protein structure within cell membranes) found in macrophages. This channel plays a central role in the inflammatory response triggered by titanium particles and bacterial toxins.

To test its importance, the team used genetically engineered mice that lacked TRPC1. When these animals were exposed to the same combination of titanium particles and bacteria, their immune systems responded much more effectively.

The mice developed dramatically smaller abscesses, produced fewer inflammatory signaling molecules, and regained the ability to clear bacteria. Disabling the channel prevented the destructive disease process observed when TRPC1 was present.

The results point toward a potentially new approach to treating peri-implantitis by targeting the immune system response rather than relying exclusively on antibiotics.

With funding from the National Institutes of Health, Kotsakis and his colleagues are now evaluating potential drugs that act on the same molecular pathway in human cells.

Why Proper Dental Implant Cleaning Matters

Although the research opens the door to future therapies, it also reinforces an important preventive measure for people who already have dental implants.

Regular professional cleaning remains the strongest known protection against peri-implantitis. However, the instruments and techniques used during those appointments can make a significant difference.

Until approximately a decade ago, many dentists routinely cleaned implants with the same metal scalers used to remove deposits from natural teeth. Research by the Rutgers team and other scientists has since demonstrated that these tools can damage and corrode titanium surfaces, potentially releasing additional particles and accelerating the disease.

Today, nonabrasive cleaning methods have become standard practice, helping protect implant surfaces while reducing the risk of complications.

By revealing how corroded titanium can interfere with immune defenses, the findings offer a new explanation for why some dental implant infections are so difficult to treat and identify a promising direction for developing more effective therapies.

#2662. COP17: Which countries have released new pledges to tackle nature loss?

Some 58% of countries have met a UN call to submit new pledges on addressing nature loss – two years after the original deadline – according to Carbon Brief analysis.

Countries were asked to submit new pledges, known as national biodiversity strategies and action plans (NBSAPs), by the start of the COP16 biodiversity summit in October 2024.

After only 15% of nations met the original deadline, countries agreed at the summit to a new text that “urges” countries to release their NBSAPs “as soon as possible”.

Carbon Brief analysis shows that 113 countries have now come forward with new NBSAPs – up from 55 nations a year ago.

Countries will gather in Yerevan, Armenia, next week for COP17.

COP17 marks the halfway point of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF), a landmark agreement made in 2022 to reverse nature decline by 2030.

The summit will provide the stage for the first global review of countries’ progress towards meeting the targets of the GBF.

In Montreal, Canada, in 2022, nearly every country in the world agreed to the GBF. The overall “mission” of the framework is to “halt and reverse” biodiversity loss by 2030.

The GBF includes a list of 23 targets for 2030. They cover an expansive range of topics, including restoring ecosystems, addressing climate change and providing developing nations with finance to help cover the costs of protecting nature.

As part of the GBF and its underlying documents, countries agreed to a schedule for monitoring their progress towards achieving the 2030 targets.

This included the preparation of a “global report” of progress coordinated by the UN, which will inform a “global review” undertaken by countries at COP17.

The global report draws on countries’ NBSAPs and national targets. It also draws on countries’ seventh national reports, which were due to be submitted to the UN in February of this year.

Carbon Brief analysis shows that 113 out of 196 parties (58%) have now met the call to produce a new NBSAP – up from 55 nations just a year ago.

Since last year, many African nations have now published new NBSAPs.

COP17 host nation Armenia published its new NBSAP in April.

According to the GBF and its underlying documents, countries that were “not in a position” to meet the deadline to submit NBSAPs ahead of COP16 were requested to instead submit national targets.

Unlike NBSAPs, national targets simply list biodiversity goals that countries will aim for, without an accompanying plan for how they will be achieved.

Four of the world’s megadiverse nations – countries that account for the majority of Earth’s species – have not yet published new NBSAPs. These countries are Papua New Guinea, Peru, South Africa and the US.

The US is the only UN member country that is not a signatory to the UN Convention on Biological Diversity.

The country with the most land on Earth, Russia, has also not produced a new NBSAP.

By the end of COP16, some 119 parties had produced at least one national target. Two years later, this figure has risen to 175, or 89% of countries.

In addition, around half of countries met the deadline to produce their seventh national reports by February of this year. This figure has now risen to 73% of countries.

Over the past two years, many developing countries have expressed concerns that a lack of available funding has prevented them from publishing their NBSAPs.

A spokesperson for the Global Environment Facility (GEF), the multilateral fund that provides funding to help with the preparation of NBSAPs, told Carbon Brief in 2025 that 120 out of 139 countries that had requested financial support since COP16 had been able to access it.

The spokesperson added that the UN Environment Programme was “working to resolve outstanding issues” to allow the remaining 19 countries to access financial assistance.

#2666. World on Track for 2.8 Degrees Celsius of Warming

An oil refinery in Houston, Texas, that was shut down last year. Brandon Bell / Getty Images

Current climate policies put the world on a course for 2.8 degrees C of warming this century, well beyond international climate targets, according to a new U.N. report.

Under the Paris Agreement, nations set a goal of limiting warming to 1.5 degrees C. But according to the latest U.N. “Emissions Gap Report,” the world is rapidly blowing past that threshold.

Average global temperatures over the past three years were more than 1.5 degrees C warmer than the preindustrial era, and there is evidence that warming is accelerating. While the planet has not officially surpassed the Paris goal, which will be judged by the average temperature over 20 years, overshoot is imminent. Beyond 1.5 degrees C, scientists see a growing risk that the Earth will cross planetary tipping points, from the dieback of the Amazon rainforest to the thawing of Arctic permafrost, that would further intensify warming.

In the best-case scenario, countries could reach peak warming of around 1.8 degrees C in the middle of this century, before drawing down vast volumes of carbon dioxide to bring warming back down to 1.5 degrees C, according to the new report.

“Overshoot is not surrender,” said U.N. Secretary-General António Guterres. “We can still keep it as small and as short as possible — and bring temperatures back below 1.5 degrees by the end of the century.”

But achieving this feat would demand a herculean effort, with countries slashing emissions by 18 percent by 2030 and 69 percent by 2040. Nations would also have to draw down 320 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. However, as the report notes, it is uncertain whether carbon removal technologies could even be scaled up to meet that goal.

One bright spot in the report: Renewable energy is growing more quickly than predicted when the Paris Agreement was adopted in 2015. Solar, in particular, has grown seven times faster than was anticipated.

“The emissions gap is now a leadership gap,” Guterres said. “And the world is running out of time and excuses to close it.”

#2670. Cambridge scientists create a $10 chip that could put a medical lab on your wrist

A new optical sensor costing just $10 could bring laboratory-level precision to wearable health monitors, potentially allowing people to track blood glucose without needles. The technology could also transform food testing, industrial quality control, and environmental monitoring.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge and GlitterinTech (a start-up founded by the same research group) have developed a new type of optical spectrometer that combines exceptional accuracy with a remarkably small size and low cost. Measuring just centimeters across, the device could be incorporated into portable electronics and wearable health technology. Its potential applications range from monitoring important health markers in real time to identifying materials and detecting changes in chemical composition.

Optical spectrometers work by analyzing light to reveal information about the substances it interacts with. They are essential tools in medicine, manufacturing, chemical testing, and environmental research. However, making these instruments smaller has traditionally meant compromising their performance. Compact spectrometers often have limited measurement ranges, reduced resolution, or lower accuracy, making them better suited to identifying substances than performing precise measurements.

The researchers believe their new design could overcome these limitations. Known as a convolutional spectrometer, it uses a mathematical principle called the convolution theorem to analyze light in a fundamentally different way. According to the team, this approach delivers performance that surpasses existing types of spectrometers in several key areas, including designs based on light dispersion, Fourier transforms, and computational reconstruction.

A New Way to Analyze Light With Extraordinary Precision

Traditional spectrometers generally separate incoming light into its component wavelengths or use complex calculations to reconstruct its spectrum. The new device takes another approach by performing a mathematical operation called convolution directly on the incoming light.

To accomplish this, the researchers arranged optical components with repeating spectral responses in a sequence. These components can include unbalanced Mach-Zehnder interferometers or micro-ring resonators. By adjusting them in proportion to one another, the system can shift its response to different wavelengths in a controlled, linear fashion. Fast Fourier transforms are then used to accurately recover the light spectrum.

"The key insight was to go back to the mathematics and ask whether there was a fundamentally cleaner way to retrieve spectra," said Dr. Chunhui Yao, a lead author of the study. "By using the convolution theorem directly in the optical domain, we avoid many of the limitations that have held miniaturized spectrometers back. This gives us high precision, strong noise tolerance, and very low computational overhead, all in a compact and low-cost system."

The researchers built the spectrometer using a silicon nitride photonic platform and integrated it with onboard electronics. It operates across a broad range of near-infrared wavelengths (1200-1700 nm) and can collect and process measurements in less than a second.

Another important advantage is its ability to expand its measurement capabilities without major hardware changes. Because the system uses repeating optical patterns, its spectral bandwidth can theoretically be extended almost without limit. Adding more optical components in sequence can also increase its resolution exponentially.

A $10 Sensor That Could Transform Food Testing and Healthcare

To evaluate the device outside purely theoretical measurements, the researchers tested it on a variety of materials and substances. The results demonstrated its potential for applications that normally depend on larger, more expensive laboratory instruments.

When used to identify plastics, pharmaceuticals, coffee, flour, and tea, the spectrometer achieved a 100% classification success rate in the reported tests. It also measured concentrations in water-based and organic solutions with accuracies of approximately 0.01%, exceeding the performance of commercial benchtop spectrometers in those experiments.

The healthcare demonstrations were particularly promising. Under conditions designed to reflect real physiological environments, the device accurately measured several human biomarkers, including skin moisture, blood alcohol, blood lactate, and blood glucose.

The researchers also demonstrated noninvasive glucose tracking over an extended period in one participant. Although further testing would be needed before the technology could become a practical medical product, the finding suggests a possible route toward monitoring blood sugar without repeatedly piercing the skin.

"These biomedical demonstrations are particularly exciting," said Prof. Qixiang Cheng, who led the project. "What makes this work stand out is not just performance in the lab, but technical readiness. Dr. Chunhui Yao's contribution was crucial in translating a mathematical concept into a fully packaged, robust system that operates reliably across temperature extremes and real-world conditions. That combination is what opens the door to practical deployment."

The sensor also demonstrated impressive resistance to temperature changes. It continued operating reliably between -20°C and 80°C, an unusually wide temperature range for a miniature spectrometer. This durability could be especially valuable for wearable devices, factory equipment, and sensors operating outdoors.

Why a Simpler Design Could Be a Major Breakthrough

The new spectrometer's advantages extend beyond its accuracy, size, and price. Its internal design also reduces the amount of computing power needed to analyze incoming light.

Many existing miniature spectrometers depend on complicated calibration procedures and demanding algorithms to reconstruct spectral information. These methods can become unreliable when measurements contain noise, and they often require substantial processing resources.

The Cambridge team's convolution-based approach avoids much of this complexity. Because the measurements follow a linear mathematical relationship, the system can recover spectra rapidly and consistently while using relatively little computational power.

"This is a beautiful example of how elegant engineering can unlock real impact," said Prof. Richard Penty, who contributed to the photonic system design and integration. "The architecture is simple, scalable, and manufacturable, yet it delivers precision that rivals or exceeds much larger instruments. Dr. Chunhui Yao played a central role in bringing together the photonic design, system integration, and experimental validation that made this possible."

Its relatively simple construction could also make the technology easier to manufacture on a large scale, potentially bringing sophisticated optical measurements to devices where conventional spectrometers would be too bulky or expensive.

Bringing Laboratory-Grade Measurements to Everyday Devices

The researchers envision a future in which accurate optical sensing becomes a routine feature of everyday technology rather than something largely confined to specialized laboratories.

In industrial settings, inexpensive spectrometers could be installed throughout manufacturing facilities to continuously check material composition and product quality. Similar sensors could monitor food freshness, identify substances, and analyze environmental conditions on a much larger scale than is currently practical.

Healthcare could see equally significant changes. Compact sensors might eventually enable wearables to measure hydration levels, detect signs of alcohol intoxication, track biomarkers related to physical fitness, or continuously monitor glucose levels for people managing diabetes.

"Our vision is to make spectrometry as ubiquitous as temperature or motion sensing," added Prof. Qixiang Cheng. "This work shows that high-quality spectral information doesn't have to be confined to the laboratory -- it can be embedded directly into the technologies people use every day."

As photonic technology becomes smaller and more advanced, the convolutional spectrometer provides a promising example of how mathematical insights and practical engineering can make sophisticated scientific instruments simpler, cheaper, and more accessible. By combining high precision with a device that costs around $10, the researchers have opened a potential path toward a new generation of smart sensors capable of measuring the hidden chemistry of our bodies and surroundings.

#2675. Sixteen Years After Deepwater Horizon, Deep Sea Life Shows Few Signs of Recovery

An oil slick in the Gulf of Mexico after the Deepwater Horizon oil spill in 2010. Kris Krüg via Wikipedia

Sixteen years after an explosion on the Deepwater Horizon oil rig unleashed more than 4 million barrels of oil into the Gulf of Mexico, life in the deep sea has yet to recover.

A new study finds that in the first few years after the 2010 disaster, populations of octopus, squid, krill, shrimp, and lanternfish declined by at least 60 percent. The research, based on acoustic surveys and thousands of netted samples across a broad swath of the Gulf of Mexico, found little evidence that life in the deep sea has bounced back.

The study, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, described the losses as “the fastest and most severe” decline of animal life in the ocean layer that extends from about 600 feet deep to more than two miles down. While researchers stopped short of concluding that the oil spill had caused the declines, they said it was “the most parsimonious explanation.”

Since the disaster, scientists have found oil-related compounds in the flesh of squid and octopus and in fish eggs deep underwater, contamination that they say can impair reproduction and development or lead to an early death. Lingering pollution may make it harder for deep-sea fish that are guided by their sense of smell to find mates.

Beyond the drop in population numbers, the new study also found some declines in genetic diversity. The risk, authors say, is that populations may not harbor the genetic traits needed to survive in a rapidly changing world.

#2676. Does drinking hot drinks cool you down?

When you’re feeling warm and sticky, a hot drink probably isn’t top of your list, but it can actually cool you down faster than a cold one under the right conditions.

If you’re already feeling warm and your body detects additional heat from a hot drink, it starts trying to cool you down by sweating! Drinking the hot drink makes you sweat more than usual to cool you down.

Sweating works by a process called evaporative cooling.

For a hot drink to actually cool you down, the heat lost through sweat evaporation must be greater than the heat you gained from drinking the hot drink.

This only works in a dry climate. In a humid environment, the sweat won’t evaporate; it’ll just stay on your skin, making you pretty uncomfortable!

#2679. COP17: Developed countries ‘fall far short’ of UN targets for nature finance

The amount of finance for nature delivered to date “falls far short” of what is needed to meet the UN’s global goals, according to a report released ahead of the COP17 biodiversity summit.

The global stocktaking report, prepared by the UN Convention on Biological Diversity (UN CBD) ahead of this month’s summit in Armenia, finds that developed nations are failing to close the $200bn “biodiversity finance gap”.

Initiatives designed to support this goal are also stumbling, with a special “framework” fund for biodiversity receiving less than $140m from developed countries in the past two years and the “landmark” Cali Fund receiving only $6,000 from private companies.

The availability of finance – or lack thereof – is likely to be a key point of contention in negotiations at COP17 in Yerevan, Armenia, which begin on 19 October.

At COP15 in 2022, 196 countries agreed to an ambitious global deal to reverse biodiversity loss by 2030, dubbed the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF).

Goal D of the agreement, shown below, states that countries must “close” a $700bn “biodiversity finance gap” by 2030.

This overall $700bn is made up of two parts: changes to nature-harming subsidies; and raising financial resources to support biodiversity.

Target 18 within the text calls for countries to “identify by 2025, and eliminate, phase out or reform incentives, including subsidies, harmful for biodiversity…progressively reducing them by at least $500bn per year by 2030”.

The remaining $200bn should be met by “mobilising” resources, according to target 19 of the GBF. It states that countries should “substantially and progressively increase the level of financial resources from all sources” to “at least” $200bn per year by 2030.

The overarching $200bn can be from national budgets, multilateral development banks or money “mobilised” from the private sector.

However, the target also specifically states that “at least $20bn per year by 2025 and…at least $30bn per year by 2030” should be public biodiversity funding provided to developing countries by developed countries – or others that “voluntarily assume” their obligations.

According to the global stocktaking report prepared by the CBD, between 2020 and 2023, countries mobilised a cumulative total of just $186.4bn. This is equivalent to an average of less than $50bn per year.

While the report – published on 10 September 2026 –  cautions that it is “still too early to draw any conclusions”, it adds that this total “falls far short” of what is needed to close the $200bn-per-year biodiversity finance gap.

It continues to specify that the milestone of raising “at least $20bn per year by 2025” in public finance was also “likely not achieved”.

The report states that reporting countries raised a cumulative total of just $17.7bn in international public funding for biodiversity, across four years from 2020-23, as shown in the figure below. This amounts to an average of just $4.4bn per year.

These figures are supported by a report from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which suggests that global biodiversity finance reached $74.9-92.4bn per year between 2023 and 2025.

It adds that, of this, international public finance contributed around $16.1bn per year on average – nearly $4bn below the 2025 goal.

Moreover, the OECD report says that only $7.9bn in 2023 to $8.5bn in 2024 was “biodiversity-specific” funding, with another $5.7bn and $6.5bn, respectively, coming in the form of funds for other priorities, in which support for biodiversity was a secondary aim.

While the GBF sets targets for nature finance in 2025 and 2030, developed countries should be making similar contributions every year, according to thinktank Third World Network.

There are two main channels of international public finance that developed countries can use to meet their biodiversity finance commitments under the GBF. These are official development finance (ODF) and the Global Biodiversity Framework Fund (GBFF).

Separate figures for these channels also show financing falling behind the subtargets set out in the GBF.

This includes the Global Biodiversity Framework Fund (GBFF), which was established at COP15 in 2022.

It is currently housed under the Global Environment Facility (GEF) and reports directly to the biodiversity COP.

Despite an initial flurry of pledges for the initiative, developed nations had contributed less than $400m to the fund, as of July this year, according to data shared with Carbon Brief by the GEF.

Between August 2023 and 30 June 2026, the GBFF has received only $387.2m in signed commitments from 11 countries and one subnational government (Quebec in Canada).

The figure below shows that, after the initial pledges ahead of COP16 in 2024, there have only been a handful of new contributions to the fund.

Elsewhere, the Cali fund was created at the COP16 nature negotiations in Colombia in 2024.

It is the first-ever global fund for companies profiting from genetic data to contribute to conservation goals.

According to a working paper from the Grantham Research Institute at the London School of Economics, if all 21,690 eligible firms shared their profits according to the fund’s rules, they could raise $3.6bn for nature annually. The paper adds that, under a realistic uptake from companies, this could generate $929m annually for nature.

To date, however, the fund has received only two contributions, totalling $6,000.

Ahead of COP17 in Armenia, experts tell Carbon Brief that finance will be the most closely watched element of the stocktake of progress.

For developing countries that “hold most of the world’s biodiversity and bear the greater burden of implementation” of nature targets, the need for this financing is “very real”, says Lim Li Ching of the Third World Network speaking to Carbon Brief. She adds:

“These amounts fall far short of the GBF’s target 19. Developed countries have regrettably not met their legal obligations…[T]his failure should not be used as an excuse to pivot to the private sector or market-based mechanisms, which struggle to raise finance at scale and often deepen social and ecological harm.”

Options for private and “innovative” finance include debt-for-nature swaps, biodiversity offsets and carbon credits, but these are harder to monitor and have often faced criticism.

The Cali Fund is one avenue for companies to contribute directly to the CBD’s goals, with dedicated, transparent allocations for conservation projects and Indigenous peoples.

Dr Siva Thambisetty, associate professor of law at the London School of Economics, tells Carbon Brief that the fund is still a voluntary measure and it is up to countries “to create a constructive, ethical obligation” for companies to contribute. She adds:

“The private sector is doing what it always does: minimising outgoings and liability and maximising freedom to operate. I don’t believe the shift we need can be generated internally by businesses alone without outside pressure. States with jurisdiction over business need to take action.”

A non-profit initiative called the Cali Watch finds that “not a single government” has passed a law requiring companies to contribute to the fund. It estimates that eight companies alone should contribute more than $2bn, as part of what it calls their “Cali debt”.

Dr June Rubis, the Indigenous peoples and local communities lead on the Cali Fund’s steering committee, tells Carbon Brief that she is “worried about the pace” at which biodiversity finance is moving. Rubis continues:

“Each part of the finance architecture needs to start sending credible signals at the same time: developed countries on the $20bn commitment; donors on predictable finance for the GBFF; companies on Cali Fund contributions; and the Cali Fund itself on allocation and access. If one part moves while the others are repeatedly deferred, confidence in the wider biodiversity bargain becomes harder to sustain.”

She points out that for Indigenous peoples and local communities, “success also depends on whether finance can actually be accessed” to support communities’ priorities. She concludes:

“[H]eadline commitments matter, but so do the pathways through which money reaches people.”

#2680. Breakthrough PTSD treatment achieves 68% remission rate in clinical trial

A new approach to treating post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) has received FDA clearance, offering a personalized alternative for people who have not found sufficient relief through existing treatments. Developed by California-based Wave Neuroscience in collaboration with researchers at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology, the technology uses brain activity measurements to guide targeted magnetic stimulation.

Early clinical trial findings have been encouraging. According to Wave Neuroscience, patients experienced a 52% reduction in PTSD symptoms, while 68% achieved remission. The full findings are still being prepared for public release, but researchers believe the technology could significantly expand treatment options for millions of people.

The need for more effective PTSD care is substantial. In the United States, approximately 17.5 veterans die by suicide each day, and about 7% of veterans receive a PTSD diagnosis annually.

PTSD can develop after traumatic experiences such as military combat, serious accidents, natural disasters, or violence. An estimated 6% of Americans will experience PTSD during their lifetime, with higher rates among women and veterans.

Why Current PTSD Treatments Are Not Enough

Since PTSD was formally recognized as a diagnosis in 1980, treatment has largely centered on psychotherapy and medications known as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), including sertraline and fluoxetine.

Although these approaches can help, many patients continue to experience symptoms despite receiving treatment.

A 2020 review of PTSD medications found that patients reported improvements ranging from 6.8 to 10.1 points on symptom assessments. However, remission rates reached only 10.9% at most.

Wave Neuroscience reported substantially stronger preliminary outcomes in its clinical trial. Erik Won, DO, the company's chief medical officer, described the results as a major advance over previously reported medication outcomes.

“In comparison to that study, our FDA trial reported a 52% reduction in symptoms, so more than a four-fold increase in efficacy,” Won said. “And more importantly, we saw 68% remission in our clinical trial. So, it’s really a quantum change, an inflection point in what we can offer to patients. From a clinical care perspective, that’s really meaningful.”

These findings are promising, although the results come from different studies and should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of treatment effectiveness.

The newly cleared system uses Magnetic e-Resonance Therapy (MeRT), a technology that combines measurements of brain activity with targeted magnetic stimulation.

First, an electroencephalogram (EEG) records the brain's electrical signals. This information helps guide a personalized treatment plan using transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS.

During TMS, a coil positioned against the scalp delivers magnetic pulses that stimulate brain activity. The technique has already been used for other psychiatric conditions, including treatment-resistant depression.

What distinguishes MeRT is its use of an AI-assisted algorithm to customize stimulation protocols based on each patient's brain activity rather than relying on a standardized approach.

“Transcranial magnetic stimulation, or TMS, was previously FDA cleared for treatment-resistant depression,” Won said. “By adding an augmented AI derived multivariate algorithm, we were able to personalize protocols that received FDA clearance for post-traumatic stress disorder, which should open up market access to about 13 million people who may be struggling with PTSD.”

Dr. Kenneth S. Ramos, a physician-scientist at Texas A&M Health with extensive experience in precision medicine, helped guide the research.

His work focuses on adapting medical care to the characteristics of individual patients instead of assuming that everyone will respond to the same treatment.

“Most clinical interventions for the treatment of PTSD do not work very well,” Ramos said. “So, this is a huge area where identifying interventions that could hopefully add quality of life to the people impacted by this disorder would be tremendously impactful.”

Wave Neuroscience CEO Fred Walke said Ramos was a natural fit for the project because of his expertise in personalized medicine.

Conventional TMS generally follows treatment protocols that are less individualized. MeRT builds on the same underlying technology but adjusts treatment according to the patient's specific brain activity.

Ramos emphasized that even small biological differences between people can have major implications for medical care. Humans are approximately 99.9% genetically alike, yet the remaining 0.1% contributes to differences that can influence how individuals respond to treatment.

By accounting for these differences and tailoring stimulation to each person's brain, researchers observed substantial reductions in PTSD symptom severity.

Ramos illustrated the distinction with an analogy involving a leaking roof.

He likened conventional TMS to covering a leak with a tarp to manage its effects. MeRT, in contrast, aims to provide a more targeted solution, comparable to identifying and replacing the damaged shingles responsible for the problem.

The broader goal is to move beyond treating patients with standardized protocols and toward interventions guided by measurable biological information.

From Breakthrough Designation to FDA Clearance

The regulatory process reached an important milestone in 2024, when MeRT received an FDA Breakthrough Device designation.

Dr. Rick Silva, executive director of clinical trials at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology, explained that the designation helps promising technologies move through regulatory review more efficiently.

It does not eliminate scientific or medical requirements. Instead, it can streamline communication and reduce administrative delays while preserving the standards needed for FDA clearance.

Silva said the progress was particularly meaningful because the project began during the COVID-19 pandemic and advanced through the regulatory process unusually quickly.

According to Silva, medical device development and clinical evaluation can often take around 10 years before clearance is obtained. This project completed the relevant process in just over two years.

He credited the experience and dedication of the Wave Neuroscience team with helping make that accelerated progress possible.

“I’ve been involved in probably close to 1,000 projects in academic medical centers over the years at varying degrees of depth and detail,” Silva said. “Patients are why I do what I do. It’s hard, and the failure rate is high, and it’s a lot of work. But when you get to a point where something is going to benefit patients, it’s very professionally gratifying and personally gratifying in many ways as well.”

Dr. Israel Liberzon, distinguished professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at the Texas A&M Naresh K. Vashisht College of Medicine, served as the study's lead psychiatrist and PTSD specialist.

He believes the clearance represents an important expansion of the available treatments for PTSD.

According to Liberzon, MeRT will become the only FDA-cleared nonmedication treatment specifically for PTSD once it reaches patients.

“Right now, the FDA has approved only two medications for PTSD,” Liberzon said. “It doesn’t mean that only two of them are used, but both are SSRIs. This is the first time that in addition to the medication, we have a non-medication option. There is psychotherapy that people use for PTSD, but psychotherapies require a lot of effort. People must participate actively, and many drop out because it’s too anxiety provoking. So now we have a unique alternative.”

In the clinical trial, participants received five treatment sessions each week over a period of five to six weeks.

Researchers reported that the improvements appeared to persist after the treatment sessions ended, suggesting the benefits may extend beyond the active therapy period.

For patients who struggle with medication side effects or find psychotherapy difficult to complete, a treatment based on personalized brain stimulation could provide another option.

“People living with PTSD have been left with trial-and-error treatment, medications and their side effects, and limited outcomes—even when treated by the most dedicated and experienced physicians,” Won said. “Wave’s FDA clearance signals a fundamentally different future: one in which PTSD can be objectively evaluated, precisely treated and meaningfully healed.”

Won also believes the technology could eventually be adapted to other psychiatric conditions, including depression and anxiety.

Could Personalized Brain Therapy Transform Mental Healthcare?

Dr. Leslie S. Prichep, chief scientific officer at Wave Neuroscience, sees the FDA clearance as an important step toward treatment decisions guided by objective measurements of brain function.

“The FDA clearance of the MeRT System reflects decades of scientific research using electrophysiological biomarkers in identifying abnormal brain activity profiles and guiding personalized intervention strategies,” Prichep said. “We believe this represents an important advancement toward a more objective, data-informed model of mental healthcare.”

Walke believes that giving patients clearer information about their brain activity could also help change public attitudes toward mental illness.

He compared the idea to treating a broken bone with a cast. By making mental health conditions easier to understand through measurable physical information, the company hopes to reduce stigma and help patients better understand their experiences.

“One of the goals of the company is to create a platform for mental healthcare that uses objective imagery to help patients understand why they’re experiencing life the way that they are,” Walke said. “That insight, compounded with proven technologies, promises incredibly high efficacy when personalized.”

An important next step will be ensuring that patients can actually obtain the treatment. Wave Neuroscience and Texas A&M Health are working with other stakeholders to expand availability and make the therapy more affordable through insurance coverage.

“Our hope is that this research and technology can be a catalyst for more widespread development and innovation of more effective, personalized and precision-guided treatments,” Won said. “This is proof positive that population-scale personalized medicine is not just an aspirational goal but is an achievable reality today.”

The project combines years of work across medicine, neuroscience, clinical research, and technology, all directed toward improving the lives of people affected by PTSD.

For Ramos, the opportunity to help veterans carries particular significance because of Texas A&M's longstanding involvement in military and veteran initiatives.

“Texas A&M has a very rich history of military and veteran priorities,” Ramos said. “It’s a no-brainer that Texas A&M faculty would be interested in and supportive of an initiative that hopefully would bring positive solutions to a big problem. And anything we could do to help alleviate that problem is going to go a long way towards making a difference in the individual’s life as well as the people who are close to them. I feel very fortunate that we at Texas A&M were meaningfully involved in this project.”

7% of respondents got their start by employing themselves. This is a clever idea that honestly never even occurred to me. You get 100% valid work experience, and you're under no obligation to tell future employers that you were your own boss!

If you've struggled to get traction through other avenues, this might be an idea worth pursuing, if your situation allows for it.

For developers who do go the traditional route with a CS degree, internships are a standard part of that deal. Amongst self-taught folks, though, only 14% got their start this way.

As a career coach, I've worked with a couple students who got their start this way. One student I worked with was offered a paid internship by her top choice, and she was able to parlay it into a permanent junior-dev role within a few months.

Because the first job is the hardest to land, an internship can provide a gentler on-ramp. In the ideal case, it can very quickly turn into a typical full-time dev role. And after that point, many more doors open up.

Companies hire interns for lots of different reasons. Good companies see it as a great way to bring in enthusiastic aspiring developers. The salary is lower because the company recognizes that interns will require mentorship, which can lower the output of senior team members. Unscrupulous employers aren't thinking in those terms, though. They're interested in hiring the highest number of developers for the lowest amount of money.

Sites like Glassdoor can help you figure out whether a company is hiring interns for the right reasons. Be very wary of any company with a pattern of bad reviews. If the company is a small start-up without much info on Glassdoor, you can try to sleuth out how solid the company is in the interview. Try asking questions like "What is the ratio of junior to senior developers?" and "How does the company ensure that interns receive mentorship?".

A company would have to be pretty devious to try and take advantage of aspiring developers by offering an unpaid internship. Anecdotally, it seems to me that these types of roles are becoming less common, but if you do come across one, I suggest you run the other way.

Even if you're comfortable enough financially to work for free, it's probably not worth it. A company that does one bad thing probably does lots of other bad things as well. You probably don't want a company like that on your resume.

17% of folks said that their first developer job was for a company they were already working for in another capacity.

The thing I like about this path is that it shows that raw technical skills often aren't the most important thing to employers. Many will take an underqualified candidate that they know is a good worker over a qualified candidate they haven't worked with before.

In fact, I know at least 1 bootcamp student who got a free ride to the program from their employer. This student had previously worked in a non-technical role, and his company was willing to pay for him to go through the bootcamp to gain the foundational skills needed for a technical role!

If you currently work at a job with an engineering department, you might be surprised by what opportunities are already around you. Maybe it's worth talking with your manager!

25% of developers say that referrals played a role in getting their first job.

I think when many of us think of referrals, we imagine something pretty nefarious, like the boss hiring their unqualified nephew instead of the well-suited-but-unfamiliar applicant.

These replies paint a different story, though. It's (usually) not about convincing a relative to give you a job, it's about community involvement. If you meet someone at a meetup and are able to speak confidently and fluently about software development, it sends a strong signal about competence.

If you live in an urban area, look for local meetups. These events are usually free to attend. Recruiters do attend these events, but often the strongest connections are made with fellow developers.

Most companies have an internal referral process, so anyone you meet that works for a company can help put you on the recruiters' radar. Referrals are rarely enough to secure a job offer outright, but it's a great way to stand out from the stack of applications.

The most popular answer! 42% of developers say that the things they've built played a part in getting their first job.

Sometimes, folks worked on an amazing portfolio piece, whereas other folks did some paid work freelancing. Either way, they created a body of work that gave prospective employers a window into their skills, a demonstration of their abilities.

For the folks that work on passion projects, there's another advantage. When companies hire junior developers, they're often looking for candidates who are really enthusiastic and excited about software development. That energy can be contagious, and it can reinvigorate the entire team. By showcasing a project that is significant to you, it sends a strong signal to employers that you're ambitious and engaged.

It's important to recognize that having hours and hours of free time a week to spend hacking on side projects is a luxury that not everyone has. If you have young children, or if you're a caretaker for a sick relative, you likely won't have the time or the energy to fire up VS Code on the weekend. This is even more true during the COVID-19 global pandemic—many folks are scrambling right now with office and school closures. It may not be realistic to start working on a passion project right now.

Fortunately, it's not the only way to show enthusiasm. After all, 58% of people didn't think side-projects played a part in their own career success!

Finally, I have an umbrella category, which I've labeled “Personality & Skillset”.

Some of the folks in this category were able to get a job because they had exceptionally strong technical skills. Others had a diverse skillset that the employer appreciated. Others still were very persuasive, or excelled in the interview process.

Critically, folks in this category didn't fit into one of the others. They didn't credit their side-projects, or their community outreach, or an internship, or any of the other things we've looked at.

15% of respondents fit into this category. What this says to me is that 85% of developers had some sort of ace up their sleeve. Most developers don't find success by filling in dozens of generic applications and hoping for the best.

For every open junior developer position, companies typically receive dozens of applications. This can be discouraging, but there's another way to look at it—most applicants have not figured out a way to differentiate themselves. If you pick one or two of the strategies outlined in this article, you'll have a much better chance of standing out from the crowd and getting an offer.

#2686. Beat the Heat with Science – Five Science Hacks to Stay Cool

If you’re in the middle of a heatwave or just generally need to cool off, I’ve got five easy hacks to help. When the temperatures soar, and you don’t have air con, it can get pretty uncomfortable. I currently have a fan pointed at me, the curtains closed, and a big, icy drink, and I’m still sweating. It’s time to hack the thermodynamics! Get ready to find out how to use science to keep everyone cool and happy this summer.

If you find that a fan is just pushing warm air around, try putting a bottle of frozen water in front of it. There are two cool reasons this science hack works.

As hot air from the fan passes over the frozen water bottle, the bottle absorbs some of the heat, causing the ice to start melting. As the air has lost heat to the ice, it cools down.

The frozen water bottle cools the air around it. If you just left the bottle on a table, you wouldn’t really notice the effect, but the fan creates a convection current in which hot air is constantly pushed towards the bottle and cooled air is spread around the room.

Try again, but this time add 3 tablespoons of salt to the water bottle before freezing. Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so the bottle will feel much cooler than plain frozen water. When you place the salty water bottle in front of the fan, it should cool the room much faster than the plain water did.

White and light-coloured clothes help you stay cool as they reflect sunlight away from you, while dark colours absorb light and heat, warming you up.

You can investigate this by placing ice on light and dark coloured plates and observing where it melts first.

In the evening, when it’s cooler outside than inside, point your fan out of one window, a few feet back, and open the other windows. This blows warm indoor air outside, creating a low-pressure area inside the room and forcing cooler outdoor air through other open windows, creating a lovely cooling breeze.

The fan blows warm air outside. Bernoulli’s Principle states that fast-moving air has lower pressure than still air.

The warm air leaving the room creates a low pressure zone inside the window, but the still air in the rest of the room is at higher pressure. To create a balance again, the high pressure air inside moves towards the low pressure window area to even things up.

As hot air is forced out of the window with the fan, cooler air rushes in from outside to fill the gap.

It’s not a pretty solution, but in a heatwave we do what we can. A room where the sun enters through a window can get hot quickly. When sunlight lands on indoor surfaces and floors, they absorb the light and radiate it out as heat, which warms your room.

Aluminium foil reflects most of the light that hits it, and doesn’t easily absorb heat, so instead of sunlight entering a room, the foil reflects most of the energy back outside.

The best way to set this up is to cut a thick sheet of cardboard (like from a box) to the size of your window. Cover one side with foil, making sure the shiny side faces outwards.

Place the foil covered cardboard on the outside of the window. If you can’t get to the outside, you can put it on the inside, but you must use the cardboard too, or the window could crack due to it getting too hot.

The foil reflects the light and heat away from the window, and the cardboard acts as an insulator, blocking the heat.

Swap the water balloons for sponges! Soak clean small sponges in cold water and use as an alternative to water balloons. There’s no plastic mess to clean up, and sponges hold a lot more water than a standard water balloon, so they stay cold for longer.

Cool a drink fast with ice and salt. This easy hack works in about 5 minutes, giving you a lovely cold drink much faster than putting it in a freezer.

Use the same salt and ice trick to make a magic fruit slushy.

Set up a selection of ice excavations. These are inexpensive, easy to set up and keep kids busy for a long time on a hot day.

#2689. MIT scientists build a tiny robot that swims using living muscle cells

A swimming robot does not necessarily need a bulky motor or powerful mechanical parts to move through water. MIT researchers have demonstrated that a single, carefully engineered layer of living muscle cells can generate enough force to propel a tiny robot forward.

The engineers developed an extremely thin swimming robot that uses living muscle tissue to move its fins. Described in a new study published in Advanced Functional Materials, the device has a flexible gel framework roughly the length and width of a stick of gum. The gel forms two fins, each coated with a layer of muscle cells far thinner than a human hair. These cells have been genetically modified to contract when exposed to light.

By directing light onto either fin, the researchers can trigger muscle contractions that make the fin flap and push the robot through water. Adjusting which fin receives light and how frequently it is illuminated allows the team to control both its speed and direction.

In experiments, the remarkably thin robot successfully swam and turned through a simple underwater maze. At maximum speed, it covered approximately four times its body length per minute. Although that is much slower than an Olympic swimmer, who can travel up to 65 body lengths per minute, it is comparable to the swimming pace of a cow shark moving leisurely through the ocean.

"It takes a lot of force to move through water versus air," says study author Ritu Raman, associate professor of mechanical engineering at MIT. "The robot's quite strong, given its size. "

A New Generation of Robots Powered by Living Muscle

The device represents the first demonstration of a very thin, two-dimensional robot that can move from place to place using living muscle tissue.

Most existing biohybrid robots rely on relatively large, three-dimensional structures made from laboratory-grown skeletal muscle. Producing these structures typically requires millions of cells. Raman explains that thinner designs could reduce manufacturing costs while potentially improving movement efficiency.

"Currently, biohybrid robots from our group and others' are built from bulky, 3D chunks of lab-grown skeletal muscle that require millions of cells to fabricate," says Raman, who notes that thinner, less bulky designs such as the team's new bot could be cheaper to build and could move more efficiently. "We believe that biohybrid robots powered by living muscle could one day perform delicate jobs like exploring environments too fragile or unpredictable for conventional hardware, because living tissue is soft, responsive to its surroundings, and can heal itself."

The MIT research team also included first author Maheera Bawa, along with Arielle Berman, Laura Schwendeman, Ferdows Afghah, and Seanbiron Johnson.

From Artificial Muscle to a Swimming Robot

The new swimming robot builds on an earlier achievement by Raman's laboratory. Last year, the researchers created an artificial muscle disk inspired by the human iris, demonstrating how a thin layer of living cells could produce precisely controlled movements.

For that project, the engineers patterned a gel disk with grooves extending outward from its center and forming concentric circles. They then placed living muscle cells on the surface, where the cells grew into a thin layer following the patterned channels.

When illuminated, the muscle tissue contracted in different directions, causing the disk to stretch and compress. Its movements resembled the way the iris of the human eye changes shape as the pupil expands or narrows.

The experiment established that researchers could grow extremely thin muscle layers in intricate arrangements and use light to control their movements along multiple directions.

"People hadn't seen this muscle architecture engineered from scratch before," Raman says. "And the cells were moving in multiple directions. But they only moved about 100 microns. From a robotics perspective, their movements were tiny."

For the latest project, the researchers wanted to dramatically increase the amount of movement and force generated by the muscle cells. Their goal was to produce contractions powerful enough to propel a robot through water.

They determined that achieving this would require a better foundation for the living muscle tissue.

The earlier artificial iris had used fibrin, an exceptionally soft gel, as its underlying structure. However, the team found that fibrin could quickly shrink under the forces exerted by contracting muscle cells, limiting the effectiveness of the resulting movement.

To overcome this problem, the engineers investigated three characteristics of the gel foundation: its chemical composition, its stiffness, and the dimensions and geometry of the grooves used to organize muscle growth.

"For engineering any type of tissue, it's known that these are knobs you can tune," Raman says. "And we wanted to optimize all these parameters to support live muscle cells."

Engineering a Stronger Muscle-Powered Robot

Finding the right supporting material required testing gels with different formulations, stiffness levels, and surface patterns.

The researchers compared several groove shapes, including narrow channels with square profiles and longer channels with curved bottoms. They discovered that muscle cells aligned more effectively inside square-shaped grooves.

This alignment was important because muscle cells that grow in the same direction are more likely to merge into fibers. Those fibers can then form stronger muscle tissue that contracts in a coordinated way. The square grooves therefore provided a more effective structure for producing force.

The team also replaced fibrin with gelatin methacrylate (GelMA), a material commonly used to grow and engineer biological tissue.

By adjusting the formulation of GelMA, the researchers produced supporting gels with varying degrees of stiffness. They then examined how muscle cells developed on each surface and measured their ability to generate force.

Stiffer gels produced the best results. On these firmer surfaces, muscle cells grew in better alignment and generated stronger contractions.

The engineers next investigated how thick the gel should be. They determined that a film measuring approximately half a millimeter offered an effective balance. It was sturdy enough to support a single layer of muscle cells while remaining sufficiently lightweight to prevent the contracting cells from detaching from the surface.

The team further improved muscle performance through a form of exercise. Repeated pulses of light stimulated the cells to contract, strengthening the muscle tissue before it was incorporated into the swimming robot.

With the gel structure optimized and the muscle cells strengthened, the researchers assembled their swimming device.

The finished robot consisted of a thin gel framework divided into two fins. Both sides of the gel were patterned with square-bottomed grooves and covered with living muscle cells. As the cells developed, they fused into organized muscle fibers capable of contracting together.

Each fin could be activated independently using light, giving the researchers a simple way to control the robot's movements.

"You can think of the robot as having two independent muscles," Raman says. "If we shine a light on just one, only that muscle moves. If shining on both, they both flap."

To test the device, the researchers placed it inside a large petri dish filled with water. They then moved a light source by hand, illuminating the fins to guide the robot through a maze positioned inside the dish.

As the muscles contracted, the fins flapped and propelled the robot through the water. By changing the light stimulation, the engineers successfully controlled its movement and helped it navigate the obstacles.

Although the robot demonstrated the ability to swim and steer, its current shape remains relatively simple. The primary objective of the experiment was to establish that an exceptionally thin layer of engineered muscle tissue could generate enough force for swimming.

The researchers now plan to refine the robot's body to improve its speed. Beyond demonstrating a new approach to biohybrid robotics, the technology could eventually be useful for monitoring aquatic environments or operating in locations where conventional rigid machines might cause damage.

"Our next goal is to optimize the body design to enable faster swimming," Raman says. "But even at slow swim speeds, one could imagine a muscle-powered swimmer being used for purposes like environmental monitoring in aquatic environments."

To understand the problem, we need to first dig a little into how frameworks like Gatsby and Next.js differ from traditional client-side apps built with React.

When you use React with something like create-react-app, all of the rendering happens in the browser. It doesn't matter how large your application is, the browser still receives an initial HTML document that looks something like this:

The page is fundamentally empty, but it includes a couple JS scripts. Once the browser downloads and parses those scripts, React will build up a picture of what the page should look like, and inject a bunch of DOM nodes to make it so. This is known as client-side rendering, since all the rendering happens on the client (the user's browser).

All of that stuff takes time, and while the browser and React are working their magic, the user is staring at a blank white screen. Not the best experience.

Smart people realized that if we could do that rendering on the server, we could send the user a fully-formed HTML document. That way, they'd have something to look at while the browser downloads, parses, and executes the JS. This is known as server-side rendering (SSR).

Server-side rendering can be a performance win, but the thing is, that work still needs to be done on-demand. When you request your-website.com, React has to transform your React components into HTML, and you'll still be staring at a blank screen while you wait for it. It's that the work is being done on the server, not on the user's computer.

The galaxy-brain realization is that huge chunks of many websites and apps are static, and they can be built at compile-time. We can generate the initial HTML way ahead of time, on our development machines, and distribute it immediately when a user requests it. Our React apps can load as quickly as a vanilla HTML site!

This is exactly what Gatsby does (along with Next.js, in certain configurations). When you run yarn build, it generates 1 HTML document for every route on your site. Every side page, every blog post, every store item — an HTML file is created for each of them, ready to be served up immediately.

Unfortunately, a lot of this language is used interchangeably, and it can be kinda hard to follow. Technically, what Gatsby does is server-side rendering, since it renders the React app using Node.js using the same ReactDOMServer APIs as a more traditional server-side render. In my mind, though, it's conceptually different; "server-side rendering" happens on your live production server in real-time, in response to a request, whereas this compile-time render happens much earlier, as part of the build process.

Some folks have started calling it SSG, which either stands for "Static Site Generation" or "Server-Side Generated", depending on who you ask.

The apps we build nowadays are interactive and dynamic—users are accustomed to experiences that can't be accomplished with HTML and CSS alone! So we still need to run client-side JS.

The client-side JS includes the same React code used to generate it at compile-time. It runs on the user's device, and builds up a picture of what the world should look like. It then compares it to the HTML built into the document. This is a process known as hydration.

Critically, hydration is not the same thing as a render. In a typical render, when props or state change, React is prepared to reconcile any differences and update the DOM. In a hydration, React assumes that the DOM won't change. It's just trying to adopt the existing DOM.

Have you ever noticed that the expiration date on cereal clearly wasn't printed at the same time as the rest of the box? It's stamped on, after the fact:

There's a logic to this: cereal-box printing is a two-step process. First, all of the "universal" stuff is printed: the logo, the cartoon leprechaun, the enlarged-to-show-texture photograph, the random pics of smart-watches. Because these things are static, they can be mass-produced, printed millions at a time, months in advance.

They can't do that with expiration dates, though. At that moment in time, the manufacturers have no idea what the expiration date should be; the cereal that will fill those boxes probably doesn't even exist yet! So they print an empty blue rectangle instead. Much later, after cereal has been produced and injected into the box, they can stamp on a white expiration date and pack it up for shipment.

Two-pass rendering is the same idea. The first pass, at compile-time, produces all of the static non-personal content, and leaves holes where the dynamic content will go. Then, after the React app has mounted on the user's device, a second pass stamps in all the dynamic bits that depend on client state.

The downside to two-pass rendering is that it can delay time-to-interactive. Forcing a render right after mount is generally frowned upon.

That said, for most applications, this shouldn't make a big difference. Usually the amount of dynamic content is relatively small, and can be quickly reconciled. If huge chunks of your app are dynamic, you'll miss out on many of the benefits of pre-rendering, but this is unavoidable; dynamic sections can't be produced ahead of time by definition.

As always, it's best to do some experimentation of your own if you have concerns around performance.

#2692. Arctic Wildlife Research Suffers as Geopolitical Competition Grows

A polar bear on sea ice in the Arctic Ocean. Sebnem Coskun / Anadolu via Getty Images

As national security and the hunt for rare minerals come to dominate Arctic agendas, governments are slashing support for wildlife research. Scientists say the loss of monitoring is unprecedented and comes as climate change dramatically reshapes life in the Far North.

Decades ago, one of the world’s largest subpopulations of nesting eider ducks spent its summers on tiny Mitivik Island, near the mouth of Hudson Bay. But as concern grew, in the 1990s, over their steep decline globally, the Canadian government set up a rudimentary research station near the island in the hopes of forestalling a collapse.

The modest investment paid enormous dividends over the next 30 years as Inuit partners from nearby Southampton Island, who hunt common and king eiders for food and feathers, worked with scientists from all over the world to solve the mystery of where the birds spent their winters and the causes of, and remedy for, their initial decline. In the field for months at a time, researchers also gained critical insights into how climate change, disease, diminishing sea ice, and polar bears were affecting bird populations.

As remarkably successful as the pioneering research station was, though, Canada’s Environment and Climate Change department quietly shut it down in 2024. By then, the number of nesting eiders had crashed again, from a high of 8,000 pairs to just a few hundred.

Today, federal research continues in only three of the 14 migratory bird sanctuaries in the Arctic. Hundreds of other wildlife research endeavors in the circumpolar world, including in Greenland and the U.S., have also been shuttered in recent years.

Like many other agencies and programs operated by Environment and Climate Change Canada, the Canadian Wildlife Service has suffered from the high cost of conducting research in the Arctic, the increasing likelihood of polar bear encounters as sea ice recedes, and a series of crushing funding cuts. In the most recent blow, the Canadian government in 2025 committed to reducing the Environment and Climate Change workforce by 10 percent.

In the past, funding to monitor wildlife in the Arctic often had to be justified by its relevance to oil and gas development and how roads, pipelines, and oil wells would affect animals such as migrating caribou, according to Larry Hinzman, an expert in Arctic change at the University of Alaska, Fairbanks. But as the search for Arctic oil and gas began to ramp down in the late 2000s, due to low natural gas prices and breakthroughs in fracking for oil and gas that spurred new production in the south, climate change became a priority.

The current circumpolar decline in funding, Hinzman said, is not just about rising costs. It stems from governments shifting priorities once again, this time from climate change to national security issues and the search for rare minerals. According to the Harvard Kennedy School’s Belfer Center for Science and International Affairs, geopolitical tensions are also undermining the work of the Arctic Council, an international forum comprising the eight Arctic countries that plays a key role in monitoring environmental changes in the region.

Wildlife technician Uli Steiner tags a king eider netted on Mitivik Island before research efforts were halted in 2024. Grant Gilchrist

While support for research in the Arctic wanes, Canada is investing $405 million to advance the construction of a new highway to the Arctic coast to facilitate resource development and connect remote communities, part of a $40 billion plan to assert sovereignty over its Arctic territory.

Research in Alaska has been particularly hard hit by the suspension of hundreds of climate-based collaborations with Russia following its invasion of Ukraine, and by the closure last year of the Arctic Research Consortium of the United States after the National Science Foundation eliminated its operating grants. Between 2021 and 2024, the number of U.S. research publications on climate change in Greenland fell by 32 percent. And in July the Greenland Institute of Natural Resources, the territory’s leading environmental research center, temporarily halted any new collaboration with U.S. partners when President Donald Trump threatened to take over the island.

“There have always been ebbs and flows in the support for environmental monitoring in the Arctic,” said Hinzman. “But what we are seeing now is unprecedented. It’s heartbreaking because we need to know more than ever how to respond to a warming Arctic that is affecting almost every facet of life.”

The decline in Arctic research comes at a time when at least 96 Arctic and sub-Arctic mammal and bird species may be responding to warmer winters, earlier spring snowmelt, and profound landscape changes by migrating earlier in the year and traveling farther afield, sometimes in new directions.

But dozens of studies have shown that these journeys don’t always lead to more food. And they often expose birds to additional parasites and pathogens and put them at greater risk of predation. The new migrations also happen at a time when critical stopover sites — like the Salton Sea, in California — are being badly degraded by drought and development.

Polar bears and eiders are among the many animals that appear to be struggling with rapidly warming northern marine environments, from the Baltic to the Canadian Arctic Ocean to the eastern seaboard, stretching from Newfoundland to Maine. But without researchers in the field, it’s difficult to predict how or whether these species may adapt.

The situation was far different when eiders began their mysterious worldwide decline in the 1990s. Then, researchers working in Mitivik, in the East Bay Migratory Bird Sanctuary, which would later be named the Qaqsauqtuuq Migratory Bird Sanctuary, implanted satellite transmitters into the abdomens of eiders and learned where they were overwintering. The findings led to tighter regulations on eider hunting in Canada and Greenland that dramatically boosted eider numbers.

That is, until they crashed again due to disease and polar bear predation. In 2005, Grant Gilchrist, the founder of the research station, and his colleagues identified an outbreak of avian cholera — which is rare in the Arctic and likely linked to global warming — that would kill as many as 3,000 nesting females in 2006 and about half the population over the eight seasons it spread through the nesting colony.

U.S. soldiers conduct cold-weather training near Fairbanks, Alaska, in February, after President Trump threatened to invade Greenland. Victor J. Blue / Bloomberg via Getty Images

Gilchrist was also on hand to witness how receding sea ice, brought on by climate change, forced polar bears onto land just when the eiders were sitting on their eggs. “The bears were like grazing cows leisurely moving through a pasture, stopping to feed [on eggs] whenever they were hungry,” he said. And herring gulls, which can’t force an eider off its nest, learned to eat the eggs that the bears had missed while the eiders were away.

Canadian Wildlife Service scientist Jennifer Provencher and her colleagues discovered that even though the eiders nesting in Mitivik hosted larger-than-expected communities of gastrointestinal worms and flukes, which benefit from warming temperatures, the parasites reduced the ducks’ exposure to contaminants such as mercury and lead. The parasites appear to absorb and clear contaminants from eider tissues. This is significant because mercury and lead, which are leaching from melting permafrost in many parts of the Arctic, can result in reproductive failure in ducks, and they can be harmful to humans who rely on ducks for food. The Inuit have higher levels of mercury than the general Canadian population.

Other researchers found that eiders, like some other animals exposed to deadly disease, eventually developed an immunity to avian cholera.

“Had we not been there, we would never have been able to document any of this,” said Gilchrist, who says he has little doubt that most of what Mitivik researchers discovered is happening in other parts of the Arctic.

In the past, Indigenous hunters have reported dramatic changes in the Canadian Arctic, such as sharp declines in ivory gull numbers; orcas moving into the region and killing narwhal and bowhead whales; and grizzly bears migrating north, mating with polar bears, and producing hybrids. But it is almost impossible to say what is going on in the vast areas that are too far afield for Inuit hunters without putting scientists in the field. In Arctic Canada, there are approximately 150,000 people living in an area that is about half the size of the Lower 48 states. Only a fraction of these people lives above the Arctic Circle.

Environment and Climate Change Canada did not respond to questions about funding cuts and the cessation of research in Mitivik. In a statement, the department said it is “committed to the long-term conservation of biological biodiversity and abundance,” noting that experts are working on new research programs with territorial governments and Indigenous organizations, and with new technologies to help track and understand birds and other animals, including polar bears.

Still, Andrew Derocher, a University of Alberta scientist who has conducted research over the past 40 years on polar bears in Norway, Canada, and Alaska, notes that support for polar bear research in Canada is at an all-time low. Determining how polar bears are faring in Canada — which is home to 13 of the world’s 19 polar bear populations — is becoming increasingly difficult, he says, if not impossible, because of a lack of government funding.

Eiders found dead after an avian cholera outbreak on Mitivik Island in 2006. Grant Gilchrist

“We’ve pretty much stopped any research beyond the long-term monitoring in Western Hudson Bay and a project in James Bay” funded by a private foundation, he says. “There is almost no other research on polar bears in Canada now.”

Ornithologist Mark Mallory spent 12 years conducting research at migratory bird nesting sites across the Canadian Arctic while working for the Canadian Wildlife Service, but he left that job for a research chair at Acadia University in part because of the high cost of conducting research in the polar regions and the endless struggle to secure consistent funding. Mallory is still very much interested in what is happening in the Arctic, but his years in the field and as co-editor-in-chief of the journal Arctic Science have given him a broader perspective on the challenges scientists face when trying to figure out how a warming climate is affecting wildlife.

“No credible scientist would deny that not enough is being invested in long-term monitoring,” he says. “But the bigger problem is not enough is being done to act on what we know is happening… Do we really think 10 or 20 more years of data showing ranges shrinking north, or clutch sizes reducing, or skinnier species is going to change anything in the minds of politicians or the public?”

Gilchrist appreciates Mallory’s frustrations with governments that refuse to act on climate change, but he — like many Arctic researchers — still believes that long-term monitoring with Inuit partners is essential. “Somebody should still be out in nature monitoring wildlife and the natural environment, and quantifying changes that are occurring or not occurring,” he said.

He doesn’t know whether the eiders of Mitivik Island will eventually return or whether they have found safer areas to nest. Because of the high costs of operating at Qaqsauqtuuq and the increased odds of running into polar bears, Gilchrist is now focusing his research at a site on Baffin Island, where it is cheaper to work and where there are fewer bears.

#2696. Easy DIY Water Sprinkler

If you’re feeling the heat this week, a DIY sprinkler is a fun way to cool down. All you need is a plastic bottle and some water. This fun backyard science activity is a fantastic demonstration of gravity and air pressure.

How can tightening the lid of a bottle stop water from flowing out of the holes at the bottom? Challenge your mini engineers to find out!

When the lid is unscrewed, gravity pulls the water down and out through the holes, reducing the water volume in the bottle. Air rushes in through the loose lid to replace the lost water, which keeps the pressure inside and outside the bottle equal.

When the lid is tightened, air can no longer rush in to replace the water, so any water leaving the bottle reduces the pressure inside, which stops the water escaping. This allows the higher outside pressure to push back against the holes, trapping the water inside.

#2697. Factcheck: UK Conservatives double the ‘cost of net-zero’ after spreadsheet blunder

A booklet published by the UK’s opposition Conservative party includes a “cost of net-zero” that appears to have been doubled by a spreadsheet error.

The “common sense” policy document argues that “what people ultimately want is a government competent enough to solve the problems for which it takes responsibility”.

In a section that says “sophisticat[ed]…modelling” should not be a substitute for “political judgement”, the “Right Way” document disparages various estimates of the cost of net-zero.

The Conservative document then claims – incorrectly – that the government’s official adviser, the Climate Change Committee (CCC), had put the cost of net-zero at close to £1tn. It says:

“In 2020, the CCC estimated that its route to net-zero would cost £957bn.”

In fact, the CCC’s 2020 estimate was exactly half this amount – £478bn – and last year it published a revised figure of £108bn, largely as a result of the falling cost of electric vehicles (EVs).

The Conservative party’s erroneous claim appears to stem from another report that had accidentally added up numbers twice, using a spreadsheet published by the CCC in 2020.

The 2020 spreadsheet contains a table listing the additional investments that would be needed to build a net-zero economy, from low-carbon electricity generation through to heat pumps and EVs.

These extra capital expenditures, listed as “CAPEX”, add up to a total of £1.38tn over the 30 years of 2020-50. They are set against operational savings, listed as “OPEX”, of £0.90tn.

Added up over 2020-50, the combined CAPEX and OPEX figures come to a total of £478bn.

In addition to the annual sectoral CAPEX and OPEX figures, the CCC’s 2020 spreadsheet also has a line giving combined totals for each year. It appears that someone has added all of these numbers together, resulting in the savings and costs being counted twice.

This double-counted total for the cost of net-zero amounts to £957bn – as shown in the image below – and it appears to be the source of the claim in the Conservative booklet.

At the time of publication in 2020, the CCC said that the £478bn net cost of net-zero amounted to less than 1% of GDP over 30 years – and that the large investment needed would not only result in savings due to lower fossil-fuel imports, but that it would boost GDP overall, by around 2%.

In 2025, the CCC revised its estimates for investment costs and operating savings to £670bn and £562bn respectively, giving a net total of £108bn over 2025-50, or less than 0.2% of GDP.

Earlier this year, the committee said that cutting emissions to net-zero would cost less than a single fossil-fuel price shock and that doing so would have benefits worth £110bn per year.

The erroneous claim in the Conservative document is referenced to the CCC’s 2020 advice on the UK’s sixth “carbon budget”, which, as explained, does not contain the £957bn figure.

The earliest online use of the £957bn figure found by Carbon Brief is a 12 January 2026 article in the Spectator, by retired engineer and self-described “accidental energy analyst” David Turver.

A day later, Turver repeated the mistaken number in a report for the free-market Institute of Economic Affairs. His report cites figure 5.3 of the CCC’s 2020 advice.

However, as set out above, the CCC spreadsheet containing the data for figure 5.3 only adds up to £478bn, half the figure claimed by Turver.

It appears that Turver accidentally added up all of the numbers in the CCC spreadsheet, without noting that it already included a line for the annual total. This results in double-counting the cost.

(Turver’s report also triggered a slew of inaccurate headlines stating that net-zero would cost £7.6tn – or even £9tn. These figures, which came from Turver’s report, were based, among other things, on the implicit assumption that fossil fuels and the cars, boilers and power plants that use them are all free.)

After Turver’s report and article in January 2026, the erroneous £957bn figure was repeated in March by the Great British Think Tank. The organisation has the tagline “data, not vibes” and says of its work: “Every figure [is] sourced from official public bodies.”

The £957bn figure then appeared in the Conservative “Right Way” document in October 2026.

"Parkinson's disease is a brain disorder that causes movement and cognitive problems that gradually worsen leading to disability and a loss of independence," said study author Dong-Woo Ryu, MD, PhD, of the University of Cincinnati in Ohio. "Previous research has shown associations between physical activity and movement symptoms. Our study found physical activity may have dual benefits, with associations with slower declines in movement and cognition."

Tracking Exercise and Parkinson's Symptoms Over Seven Years

The research involved 294 adults with mild to moderate Parkinson's disease. Participants were 66 years old on average, and researchers monitored their health for up to seven years.

To understand their activity habits, participants completed questionnaires estimating how many hours each week they spent being physically active. The questions covered activities performed at work, around the home, and during hobbies or recreation.

Researchers classified activities by their intensity. Moderate activities included brisk walking, doubles tennis, leisurely swimming, and household tasks such as cleaning windows, vacuuming, or mowing the lawn. More demanding activities included running, cycling at higher speeds, participating in team sports, playing singles tennis, shoveling, and carrying heavy objects.

The team examined physical activity in three ways. First, they calculated the total time participants spent doing moderate or vigorous activities. Second, they estimated overall activity volume by combining duration with intensity, giving greater weight to strenuous exercise. Third, they separately measured the amount of time spent doing vigorous activities.

Based on total weekly activity time, participants were placed into three categories. Those in the least active group reported up to four hours per week, the moderately active group reported four to eight hours, and the most active group reported more than eight hours.

Each year, participants also completed assessments designed to track changes in thinking and memory, along with tests evaluating the severity of their Parkinson's symptoms.

More Activity Linked to Slower Memory Decline

One of the most striking differences emerged in cognitive performance. Researchers used a 30-point test of thinking and memory, with lower scores reflecting poorer cognitive function.

Among participants in the least active group, scores fell by an estimated average of 0.478 points each year. The moderately active group experienced a much smaller annual decline of 0.090 points.

For the most active participants, the estimated average yearly decrease was only 0.007 points, indicating that their cognitive scores remained nearly unchanged over time.

The researchers also examined changes in Parkinson's movement symptoms using an assessment scored from zero to 132. Higher numbers represented more severe symptoms, and participants began the study with an average score of 23.

In the least active group, symptom scores increased by an estimated 0.143 points annually, indicating a slight worsening. By contrast, scores decreased by 0.225 points per year in the moderately active group and by 0.783 points in the most active group.

Even after accounting for participants' age, sex, and how long they had been living with Parkinson's, the researchers found that the most active group experienced slower cognitive decline and less worsening of movement symptoms than the least active group.

The connection was not limited to one measure of exercise. Higher total activity time, greater overall activity volume, and more time spent exercising vigorously were each associated with slower deterioration in both thinking abilities and movement symptoms.

Why Consistent Physical Activity May Matter

The results suggest that remaining physically active over time could be especially important for people with Parkinson's disease. Researchers noted that even relatively small differences in the rate of decline might become more significant when they accumulate over several years.

"The possible benefits of physical activity for people with Parkinson's disease may depend on how long they stay active and whether they continue exercising over time," said Ryu. "While our study found small differences between activity levels, these differences may become more meaningful over time. Since participants generally had good thinking and memory skills at the start of the study, our results should be interpreted with caution and further studies are needed."

The researchers also identified an important limitation. Physical activity levels were based on participants' own reports rather than direct measurements, meaning some estimates may have been inaccurate.

Although the findings strengthen the evidence connecting physical activity with better long-term outcomes in Parkinson's disease, additional research will be needed to determine whether exercise itself can slow the progression of symptoms.
