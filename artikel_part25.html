#1201. The hidden treasure in discarded wearable and fast tech

Sales of fast tech like mini fans and disposable e-vapes, as well as wearable health tech such as heart monitors and smartwatches are soaring. They're creating a new e-waste problem but are full of hidden treasure.

From mini fans and the cheap chargers to smartwatches bought as part of a New Year's health kick, some 44% of Europeans own at least six "fast-tech" gadgets. And more than half say they keep unused and broken e-waste items languishing at home or throw them in the regular trash instead of recycling.

That's according to a new report by Brussels-based e-waste association WEEE Forum. It describes fast tech, which is booming, as charging and lighting devices under €10 (circa $11), high-tech items like power banks and fitness trackers, and gadgets and lifestyle appliances like cheap mini blenders and fans, as well as single-use electronic vapes.

"So, these electronics are a bit like fast fashion," Magdalena Charytanowicz, WEEE Forum's Communication Manager, told DW.

Nearly 40% of buyers recalled discarding a fast-tech item without using it at all or within a month of buying it, and nearly 60% said they had used a product for no longer than six months. Yet the vast majority of respondents were generally satisfied with their gadgets despite short-lived functionality.

This suggests consumers accept the "fast-tech bargain" of poor quality that "does the job for a while" and is easily replaced — similar to fast fashion — according to the report.

About 62 million tons of electronics are discarded worldwide annually. "That's the Great Wall of China of e-waste that is produced every year," noted Charytanowicz, adding that the figure is set to grow to 82 million by 2030.

In Europe, roughly 40% of electronic waste is recycled, yet the rate worldwide is just 17%. This means valuable materials are lost, while electronic waste ends up polluting the environment.

In 2022, a million tons of critical raw materials were embedded in the 10.7 million tons of e-waste generated across Europe, including manganese, gold, copper and aluminum.

These materials are at "the center of geopolitical tensions," Charytanowicz said, referring to escalating global tensions over access to rare earths and other critical minerals.

"Even the smallest" devices are worth recycling if they contain a circuit board or battery, she said.

Groups like WEEE Forum say more awareness raising is needed on how to dispose of cheap electronic devices, and that recycling should be made as easy as possible.

But the best way to reduce a product's environmental impact is to extend its lifespan through better design and repair, as recycling raw materials is energy intensive.

Charytonawicz said clearer, harmonized EU rules now under consideration could help improve e-waste collection. The bloc's 20-year-old directive is "not really adapted to the changing reality," she said.

Another growing area of e-waste is digital healthcare. While devices such as diabetes sensors and blood pressure monitors are "transforming healthcare," many are designed to be disposable.

A study published in Nature in December 2025 found sales of wearable healthcare electronics, for example, could reach 2 billion devices annually by 2050, and generate a million tons of e-waste.

Jeremy Faludi, associate professor of sustainable design engineering at TU Delft in the Netherlands, says making health tech — particularly digital devices used in, or issued by hospitals — recyclable or reusable comes with some "much bigger challenges" than normal electronics.

That's in part due to the potential for patient contamination, particularly with digital surgical devices that come into contact with blood or other bodily fluids.

"The way that a lot of things are designed now, you can't recover the stuff because it can't be easily sterilized without the electronics," said Faludi.

He worked on the Digital Health in Circular Economy (DiCE) project, an EU-funded initiative, to help redesign digital healthcare devices to "recover critical materials and reduce environmental impacts without compromising patient safety."

A modular design, for example, allows electronic components to be separated from the parts that need to be replaced between patients, making devices easier to reuse.

The four-year DiCE project, which included citizens, hospitals and manufacturers across nine EU countries, found reusable ECG cables — which connect patients to heart-monitoring equipment — cut environmental impacts by up to 81-94%, for example. The sets are usually designed for single use.

Rules around medical waste can also make reuse and recycling more difficult, particularly when requirements differ between countries and often come down to perceived rather than actual risk, Faludi added. He says some 85% of hospital trash is not infectious but everything ends up in the same bins. Better sorting as well as EU-wide regulations around treating non-infectious hospital waste would help here.

Like with other tech, consumer health devices such as blood pressure and glucose monitors often risk being forgotten in drawers or ending up in the wrong waste, simply because people do not know how to dispose of them, said Charytanowicz of WEEE Forum, which is also involved in DiCE.

Pilot schemes in Belgium, Spain and Slovenia that offered free postal returns and collection machines in public places found that more than 60% of people were willing to return unwanted devices if it was a straightforward process.

"What's really important is the convenience so that it can be integrated in your daily routines," said Charytanowicz, adding that "nudges" such as messages that recycling a device could help the health of another person motivate people to "do the right thing."

This story is part of Image’s May Momentum issue, which looks at art as a sport and sport as an art.

I’m haunted by the perfection of Roxy board shorts from the early aughts. As a teen surfer girl in El Porto, they were my holy grail. In those years, all the cool surf brands made cute surf clothing, but the emphasis was decidedly more on the aesthetic than the function, which was a bummer when it came to, you know, surfing. Roxy board shorts changed that, especially the particular style I’m thinking of: slightly longer to actually prevent thigh chafe from the board, they sat perfectly on my hips and stayed on with their Velcro and lace-up fly. Unlike when I tried to borrow from the boys section, the board shorts weren’t comically long or baggy or cut straight across the waist. These were made for girls who actually surfed. I bought them in every (very cute) color I could find and surfed them until I couldn’t any longer.

Sonia Kasparian, the original designer of Roxy’s board shorts back in the mid-’90s, smiles in our recent conversation when I recount my astonishment at their discovery. She’s grinning because of course they fit — that was the ethos behind her design. “I wanted [the board shorts] to be totally functional, exactly to the same standards that the men’s were, but designed for women. There was a completely different fit for women than men.” And true to Roxy’s style bona fides, the board shorts looked good enough to pair with a T-shirt. “Everything was designed with the idea of being something that women would not only want to wear in the water, but just wear out walking around in everyday life,” Kasparian explains. “But if you were to go out in the water, those shorts would stay put. They would be comfortable — and they would be completely authentically built.” The brand was testing prototypes in the surf, eventually with pros like longtime Roxy team rider Lisa Andersen, but initially with Kasparian and her fellow Roxy and Quiksilver colleagues Lissa Zwahlen, Melissa Martinez and Amy Grace Patrick, among others. They’d paddle out in the board shorts in the morning to try out their designs before heading into the office, their noses dripping saltwater later in the day as they bent over fabric bins and sales reports.

The functional ethos was always part of Quiksilver too. For the uninitiated, Roxy is the women’s brand of Quiksilver, the legendary Australian company that began in 1969 and made board shorts that performed as well as they looked. Their innovative, stylish design quickly became a nonnegotiable for the best and coolest surfers, and when Angeleno Bob McKnight discovered the board shorts on a surf trip in the early ’70s, he knew they’d become ubiquitous among surfers in California too. But when McKnight brought the brand to the U.S., he was met with skepticism. As McKnight tells it during our conversation at Quiksilver HQ, when he first approached Walter Hoffman, the renowned California maker of Hawaiian print fabric and eventual supplier and mentor, Hoffman exclaimed that board shorts were “the worst idea I’ve ever heard in my life.” The apparel business, according to him, was an impossible one to succeed in. McKnight protested to Hoffman, though: “We’re not in the apparel business. We’re making equipment for surfers.” The distinction paid off with pros and wannabes alike, and by the time Quiksilver launched Roxy with Kasparian in 1990, they were a cultural juggernaut. PacSun, anyone?

The beauty of surfing is inextricable with the fear of it. It’s as if the baptismal in the ocean were somehow both the original sin and its curative.

When I ask Kasparian about being a part of my personal archives, about being part of the historical surfwear archives, she’s “just so happy.” Despite Roxy’s eventual runaway success — it’s responsible for about 30% of Quiksilver’s sales — it was hard work to convince others in the industry that there was absolutely a need and a desire for fashionable, functional surfwear for girls. “I mean, you would go into the surf shops and you’d see all this men’s product, and you’d see a poster of the Reef girl with her butt in your face, wearing a thong,” Kasparian recounts. It’s not an exaggeration to say that Kasparian and her team made history, not just for teenage me but for countless other girls who wanted to look and feel confident in and out of the surf. “[Roxy board shorts] changed the dynamic of where women fit in the surf industry. They weren’t just the girls that sat on the sideline with the thong and watched their boys out in the water. They were the ones out in the water. And that was huge.”

I haven’t had any luck in finding the grown-up surfer girl version of Roxy board shorts. I still comb thrift racks and bulk bins for something close enough, even trying on the odd pair of early aughts Quiksilver men’s board shorts, as if, just by wanting it enough, I can somehow manifest the completely different fit that Kasparian was so intentional about designing. But board shorts for women these days just don’t hit the same way, especially the longer ones. They read midlife modesty, not stoke; they’re lacking in the joyous, playful audacity that Kasparian and her team infused into their groundbreaking designs. Maybe the board shorts I’m seeing aren’t the vibe because, well, they’re made for women, not girls, and despite my best efforts to never grow up (see: still surfing), I am in fact an adult woman and no longer a girl. And maybe, most of all, when I say I long for those Roxy board shorts from long ago, what I really mean is that I’m nostalgic for a younger version of myself: a surfer girl who was just discovering clothes that made her feel more like herself, with all the evolutions of that person still ahead of her.

#1207. LULA VICTORY IN BRAZIL!

Sigue nuestra cobertura en vivo en español de las elecciones en Brasil

Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva was reelected to a historic fourth term Sunday, but his defeated rival and Trump ally Senator Flávio Bolsonaro said he would not accept the results and would mount a legal challenge.

The 80-year-old leftist leader won more than 51% of votes, securing a runoff victory that bucks a broader rightward political shift across Latin America and comes after a campaign dominated by concerns over the cost of living, crime and corruption.

Bolsonaro, who trailed Lula by about 4.4 million votes, said his team would challenge the result after the Supreme Court’s last-minute ruling to extend voting by two hours.

“It is impossible to recognize the election result given how these events unfolded,” Bolsonaro told supporters in the capital Brasilia after Lula’s victory was declared.

“We will take legal action. We will ask for this election to be annulled.”

The vote marks the first time since the return of direct presidential elections in 1989 that a candidate has won the presidency after finishing second in the first round.

“I can assure you that we are going to change the way we govern this country. We are going to give the people a greater role in our decisions,” Lula said after his reelection.

Lula thanked the Brazilian people for their “generosity,” especially the country’s youth who “took to the streets to defend democracy.”

In Rio de Janeiro, Lula’s supporters were seen hugging, beeping their car horns and raising the “L” sign. And in Belo Horizonte, capital of the key swing state Minas Gerais, supporters could be seen chanting and singing following Lula’s victory.

Lula’s win is a major setback for the Bolsonaro political movement, with Flávio failing to accomplish what his father, imprisoned former President Jair Bolsonaro, also couldn’t to do in 2022: defeat Lula at the ballot box.

But the results came after a turbulent election day, with voting being extended by two hours following last-minute pleas from both candidates to electoral authorities.

Brazilian Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin granted a request from Lula’s campaign, which asked for an extension citing “incidents” affecting buses that provide transportation to polling venues in several states.

Bolsonaro’s campaign had also requested an extension in some areas, citing difficulties voters faced reaching polling stations because of heavy rain. The extension order came around 5 p.m. in Brasilia, just as polls were scheduled to be closing, and many stations had already shut down.

The election outcome will have repercussions beyond Brazil, keeping Latin America’s largest country on a more independent foreign-policy path under Lula, who has emphasized Brazil’s sovereignty and closer ties with the Global South and China amid tensions with the US administration.

Before polls closed on Sunday, US President Donald Trump said that he was “monitoring” Brazil’s presidential runoff. Although he declined to endorse either candidate, he previously praised Flávio’s first-round performance and described the election as “very important.”

Here’s what to know about Sunday’s high-stakes election:

According to Brazil’s Superior Electoral Court, Bolsonaro secured 2.2 million votes more than Lula in the first round of voting. Neither candidate reached the majority needed to win outright, forcing both campaigns to compete for a shrinking pool of undecided voters while trying to mobilize their supporters in a country deeply divided over its political future.

The result exposed vulnerabilities in Lula’s reelection bid, from frustration over the cost of living and crime to difficulties mobilizing parts of his traditional base. It also showed that Jair Bolsonaro’s movement remained strong, even as the former president served a prison sentence for attempting to overturn his 2022 defeat.

The shift in the first round of the election reflected a broader electoral trend across Latin America, where several presidential elections since April 2025 — in Ecuador, Bolivia, Honduras, Chile, Costa Rica, Colombia and Peru — have resulted in victories for right-wing or center-right candidates.

A July Datafolha survey found 44% of Brazilians leaning right or center-right in their political values, compared with 39% on the left or center-left, reversing the balance measured in 2022.

Despite this, leftist Lula prevailed in the second round.

Public safety emerged as one of the defining issues of the election. A September Quaest/Pax survey found that 28% of Brazilians saw violence as the country’s biggest problem, followed by corruption at 22% and the economy at 19%.

Bolsonaro promised to designate major criminal factions as terrorist groups, expand prisons and lower the age of criminal responsibility. “Those who think I’m radical on public security haven’t seen anything yet,” he said October 13, adding that even El Salvador’s infamous hardline approach under President Nayib Bukele was not enough.

Lula struggled to reconcile his party’s traditional positions with demands for tougher action. A widely circulated clip of him saying last year that drug traffickers were also victims of drug users reinforced what Brian Winter, editor-in-chief of Americas Quarterly, called a “longstanding perception that the Brazilian left is soft on crime.” Lula later acknowledged that he had expressed himself poorly.

Lula faced criticism about the state of the economy and affordability. During his first two terms, a commodities boom helped lift millions of Brazilians out of poverty and into the middle class.

This time, many voters had said they felt little progress. Lula can point to low unemployment and easing inflation, but those indicators had not changed perceptions for households struggling with everyday prices.

Winter compared the dynamic to the 2024 US presidential election, when favorable statistics failed to overcome frustration with prices under President Joe Biden and then-Vice President Kamala Harris.

Still, many voters have remained loyal to Lula because of the social gains made during his first two terms, while fearing that a Bolsonaro presidency could roll back social rights. Flávio sought to counter those concerns by distancing himself from some traditionally conservative positions.

Part of the shift to the right in Brazil is due to the association of the left with corruption, although scandals have shadowed both campaigns. During last Sunday’s debate, Lula challenged Bolsonaro to open his financial records and pressed him over his ties to Daniel Vorcaro, the former head of the collapsed Banco Master, whose alleged connections to powerful figures have embroiled Brazil’s political and judicial establishment, including the Supreme Court. Flávio, meanwhile, accused Lula of corruption and raised allegations involving the president’s eldest son, known as Lulinha. Lula’s previous corruption convictions were annulled in 2021.

The corruption allegations are deepening political fatigue in Brazil, say experts. “It reinforces the disgust and apathy that Brazilians feel with their political system as a whole,” Winter told CNN.

But if there was one key group the candidates needed to win over, both experts and campaigns agreed it was women.

Women account for nearly 53% of registered voters and had been more resistant to Bolsonaro-aligned candidates than men. An Oct. 15 Datafolha survey put Lula ahead among women by 49% to 40%, while Flávio led among men by 52% to 40%.

Malu Gatto, an associate professor of Latin American politics at University College London, said the gender gap widened after Jair Bolsonaro emerged in 2018, as men gravitated toward the right and women became more supportive of the Workers’ Party.

Both campaigns addressed violence against women, particularly femicide, after several brutal cases drew national attention. Brazil recorded 1,571 femicides in 2025, the highest number since the crime was defined in law in 2015, according to the Brazilian Forum on Public Safety.

It’s worth thinking about why the discussion within a fervently traditionalist community played out so similarly to the one on the secular left. The Lakewood rabbis don’t consider generative AI destructive simply because it is new or because it could easily supply users with blasphemous content (though there is that). They also argue that interpreting the Torah is a fundamentally communal activity that requires ameilut, or toil, a philosophy espoused by foundational scholars like Rashi. They fear a future in which Torah study is fully automated and human intellectual exertion nullified. In this, they’re surprisingly aligned with leftists who oppose data centers and big tech. “They might come up with different sources for explaining it,” anthropologist Ayala Fader told The Forward, “but they are actually articulating some of the objections to AI that you can read about in The Chronicle of Higher Education.”

There are, of course, more prosaic concerns. Haredim, like other groups that enforce strict religious or cultural observances, have long argued about when and how to accommodate the demands of the contemporary world, and whether to adopt or shun new technologies. A decade ago, a debate raged among Hasidim (a subset of Haredim) about the Internet’s potential for sacrilege. Today, there are Hasidic TikTok influencers—although they abide by strictly enforced community standards. Many Orthodox Jews today see AI as a similar gateway to pornography or other impurities, and it remains to be seen whether they will accept it as inevitable and just try to put up some guardrails, or succeed in fully eradicating it. The Satmar Rebbe, the head of the Satmar Hasidic dynasty, put out an emergency broadcast this past May: “On every phone in the home, even on a kosher phone, one can connect to…impurity in a single moment,” he said. “There are many victims—young boys, girls, and even married yeshiva students. They are falling and descending directly into hell.” He called upon Satmar Hasidim to abstain from cell phones that preload applications like Gemini or OpenAI.

Anti-AI Haredim are not alone in considering the risks of this new technology to be existential. The threats it poses to labor are obvious, and yet there’s something about it that’s still more troubling, something that can be hard to put your finger on. The debates around AI often take on a strangely religious tone—after all, the most prominent leader to condemn its encroachment on humanity is Pope Leo. The Lakewood rabbis’ objections may help us better understand why both secular and religious AI refuseniks feel that something elemental is at stake. The Haredim who met in January argued that mitzvot—good deeds performed to fulfill a Torah commandment—cannot be replaced by machines. To eschew ameilut is to forgo the sacred work of Torah study. Outsourcing interpretation of scripture can be seen as an attack on the act of exegesis itself. In this way, AI threatens to undermine their way of life more thoroughly than previous technologies.

While it may be dangerous, AI is also increasingly omnipresent, complicating Orthodox communities’ attempts to rein it in. “You have to create rules that people will follow,” Forward staff reporter Louis Keene told me over the phone. “And if a rabbi is setting rules and nobody’s following them, it doesn’t matter how strict he is because nobody’s observing it.” Yet Keene acknowledges that many people intuit that there’s just something off about AI: “A lot of people are either nervous about it or repulsed by it. I don’t think people generally like AI art. Why do we see so much slop? We’re calling it slop. Everyone agrees.” For religious as well as secular communities, that slop presents a kind of spiritual affront.

There is a remarkably religious flavor to the way even atheists and technocrats talk about AI. Advocates frequently refer to it as a god in the making. “If we ever get AI that is strong enough to basically be God and solve all of our problems, it will need to use the same techniques that the actual God uses in terms of maintaining some distance,” rationalist and AI blogger Scott Alexander told Harper’s Magazine earlier this year. I do think it’s possible that the AI will be like, Now I am God. I’ve concluded that the actual God made exactly the right decision on how much evil to permit in the universe. Therefore I refuse to change anything.” Why are self-styled rationalists leaning so hard into such an esoteric framing?

“[T]here comes a time in every ai policy professional’s life when they realize they have to read the talmud to make further progress,” Dean W. Ball, OpenAI’s head of strategic futures, posted this summer. While that may seem like a completely out-of-left-field remark to anyone not versed in Silicon Valley lore, it sparked extensive conversations among AI researchers. The thinking goes that the Talmud offers a model of interpretation and debate that could teach AI the nature of ethics. Researchers often refer to the “AI alignment problem,” which is the difficulty that human designers face in making sure an AI system’s behavior matches the goals and values humans actually want. This is both a practical and philosophical problem, so loaded that it lends itself more easily to the domain of theology than corporate governance. Judd Rosenblatt, founder of the AI consulting company AE Studios and the AI ethics nonprofit AI Alignment Foundation, told The Jewish Forward that he looks to the Talmud for inspiration as he considers the AI alignment problem, believing its tradition of evolving arguments and adaptations could help us design AI systems that can improve exponentially without going rogue. “I think a tradition that reinterprets nothing is the more fragile one,” he said. “A rule that cannot be bent, cannot adapt to a new world and dies out.” Besides, he argues, Judaism has survived multiple implosions, including the destruction of the temple.

Not all Haredi agree that AI is a modern-day Tower of Babel, tempting the wrath of God with our hubris. Users on imamother.com, an online forum for Orthodox women, discuss their use of AI with a mix of shame and excitement, with some saying it motivates them to see doctors or go to the dentist. As one poster asked, “Am I a horrible person for not listening to das torah [a rabbinic pronouncement]? I can’t believe that HaShem would rather I be completely dysfunctional than use this tool.”

Other Orthodox Jews are embracing these tools wholeheartedly, particularly those identified with Modern Orthodoxy, a broad movement that seeks to reconcile Jewish observance with modern life. Orthodox Union, a large US-based nonprofit, is developing AI-powered Torah Study apps that collate large amounts of revered texts for both professional scholars and curious laypeople. Orthodox Union’s Rabbi Simon Taylor discussed the organization’s reasoning behind developing an app: “We have to figure out how to leverage AI to teach more Torah and to spread Judaism. We knew that we weren’t going to get it right the first time and it wasn’t going to be perfect. We worked very, very hard on safeguarding and guardrails for everything that we do as well, lots and lots of testing.” Rabbi Zohar Atkins has a new venture called Yochai, a chatbot that offers AI study support for the Torah and Talmud. The study tool takes its name from the second-century sage Shimon bar Yochai and its logo is a carob tree—the plant that, according to legend, the sage sustained himself on as he hid in a cave for 13 years to escape Roman persecution.

On the other hand, Rabbi Danya Ruttenberg recently delineated a Jewish case for being exempted from using AI in the workplace. She argues that AI is theft, as large language models like ChatGPT culled their data from countless sources without permission or compensation. Halakha, Jewish law, forbids Jews from benefiting from stolen goods. Similarly, claiming authorship for something one didn’t write oneself also a form of theft—something rabbis using AI to craft sermons may find alarming. She also points to the destruction that data centers are wreaking on the environment, and invokes the ethical principle of Bal taschit, which God reveals to Moses in Deuteronomy 20:19: Do not destroy.

Still, it’s hard to imagine that the Haredim who take a hard-line stance against AI will form a political coalition with secular leftists who share their fears about the technology’s existential risks. After all, Haredim who oppose Zionism on religious grounds and occasionally protest in support of Palestine have not exactly reached across the aisle to outsiders to forge an alliance. Nevertheless, the resemblance between secular and traditionalist Orthodox oppositions to AI is uncanny: Both insist that hard work is its own reward, and abhor the way AI destroys the capacity for human cognition. Ameilut, says The Forward’s Louis Keene, is a mitzvah. Toiling is part of the process. “I’ve found that ameilut-maxxing translates pretty well to non-religious life, too. It’s an imperative to embrace the challenge,” he writes. In other words, nothing worth doing is easy.

Technology is reshaping how the devout practice their beliefs, and it’s changing how everyone interacts with the world. AI garners devotees, and seemingly alters reality—this kind of thing was once the province of religion. For some, this development is value-neutral, and for others it poses a deep threat. The collision of tech and theology brings up a myriad of uncomfortable questions. If we are made in God’s image, and AI is made in our image, where does that leave us?

“Past that threshold, our ability to perceive detail and read socially meaningful cues falls off fast,” he says as a Mustang whizzes by. We talk about how when the vast majority of movement in a city happens by car, there are fewer occasions and grounds for unplanned encounters to materialize — and how, given the statistical scarcity, that feels all the more precious here.

I tell them about the last time I was in Chinatown, at Café Triste with Jonny’s bandmates. Four of us start at a table inside the bohemian cave à manger by the mirrored bar, sharing an inviting fig, speck, ricotta dish and a tomato stone fruit panzanella. As more people show up, we spill outside with another chilled red.

I drift from the band toward a stylish knot of people who turn out to be dancers. The lady in the asymmetrically-cropped black top that perfectly frames her baby bump has performed with Beyoncé; the androgynous hapa with the paisley silk bandanna actually worked a year in Singapore, where I was born and raised. We bond over Hainanese chicken rice before he tells me his local booker had wanted his waist-length hair shorn: more masculine, more bookable.

I’m apologizing on behalf of my conservative countryman when a dead ringer for Tadanobu Asano in “Ichi the Killer” passes on the street and waves at the dancer, who does a double take.

It’s an ex-colleague from his stint in Tokyo, getting pizza from LaSorted’s. I’d assumed L.A. wasn’t a “bump into your ex at the bodega” or “see your editor on the subway” kind of city, much less a “run into a foreign colleague on the sidewalk” one.

Yet here they are, yapping about omakases. Had we stayed inside, they would have passed within 10 feet of each other without realizing.

Raise a glass to Old Hollywood restaurants. The martinis, tuxedoed waiter and cigarette stains on a mural are a window to history.

The indoor table’s pleasure is intimacy and predictability. A sidewalk’s social contract is leakier.

Outside, a new piece can always wander onto the board.

When I later tell Zach Jarrett, Triste’s co-owner, about the dancers’ sidewalk reunion, he’s delighted but unsurprised.

“Not to be woo-woo, but there’s an energetic resonance to it,” he says. We’re sitting in the alcove overlooking the sidewalk, drinking a funky rosé as ML Buch plays. “Space, food and drinks are great nonverbal transmitters.” The people who end up here, he tells me, may already be responding to some shared frequency, be it indie music, pet-nat or an acoustically-treated room.

“It’s about what opens up when you share a language.” He pauses. “It’s a desirous thing.”

The sidewalk hang has prerequisites. New York has a slim window when your glam eyeliner won’t melt but you can still wear the halter without a jacket. In Singapore, you’ve perspired through your little black dress before the first round arrives.

L.A. has the weather for the linger: dry air, low humidity, late sunsets, long evenings, flattering light.

Outside the permanently chic Bar Seco in Silver Lake, people with killer cheekbones and lightly-tousled hair take pictures of one another between sips of matcha horchatas and bites of beurre blanc scallops — cute cocktails and delectable morsels you can consume without interrupting the conversation, or the camera.

At times, this buzzy stretch of Sunset Boulevard between Hyperion and Lucile resembles a catwalk. It doesn’t pretend to be blasé; it’s here to be seen. Perceiving and being perceived are folded into the pleasure of this scene. A Miu Miu gabardine hat set at a jaunty angle, wraparound Moncler sunglasses at night. Legs crossed, uncrossed; another picture. Maybe they’re models; maybe everyone in L.A. low-key knows how to pose like one.

FWIW, I too partake in the 0.5 flash selfie, so what I’m really getting at is: This is not an Angeleno’s usual experience of a sidewalk. Especially as a lone woman, a sidewalk is an interstitial danger to overcome en route to a destination.

We’re used to walking at a clip. Scanning constantly. Hand closing around the pepper spray in our purse, just in case — but outside these bars, the social instruction flips, tantalizingly: Stay.

But permission to linger isn’t neutral. Geographical coordinates and the price of a cocktail have done some sorting before you ask a stranger for a cigarette.

The sidewalk offers randomness, but the bar has curated the sample.

In Eagle Rock, the crowd outside Walt’s wraps around the building beneath green neon that still says “CONNOR’S PLUMBING.” It’s unclear where the bar ends and the street begins. Here, where you can play pinball for a buck and get a Hamm’s for five, it’s harder to tell apart a real ramshackle drifter and an unwashed nepo baby.

A Ukrainian bottle blond in a Hello Kitty hoodie tells me she used to make seven dollars apiece collecting Lime scooters when she first got to L.A. after Kyiv got bombed. Someone takes a swing at a piñata strung from a lamppost. When it finally gives, cigarette packs and condoms rain onto the pavement.

I still don’t know if I’m staying for sure, but I buy a beat-up 1988 Ford Bronco from a lesbian influencer in Silver Lake, and move out of Silver Lake.

While looking at our starred places in New York on Google Maps, Jonny and I discover we’d frequented the same cafe — Sweetleaf in Greenpoint — and bar — Night of Joy in Williamsburg — for a few months in 2018, when he was there rehearsing for tour, and I was finishing my first novel. With zero social or professional overlap, a Scottish drummer and a Singaporean novelist could easily never have met, then or now.

We decide not to f— it up this time and move in together.

In Highland Park, we find something we both thought L.A. might require us to surrender: pedestrianism!

Figueroa Street has all of it, walkable: grocers, cafes, bars, restos, not to mention some of the best tacos in the entire city, and a Metro station. It isn’t New York or Singapore, and increasingly I don’t need it to be.

Join a walking group, visit a new neighborhood and feel the ground beneath your feet.

Even though we have a truck now, we take the A Line to Chinatown. It’s just three stops; the ride itself is seven minutes. We drop by Café Tondo. It’s a Tuesday — bolero night. Cozier than the weekend, when it’s packed to the gills, but no less vibey.

Abraham Campillo, who co-founded this chilled-out wine bar right by the station where the trains go by overhead, had told me as we smoked his Camel Blues and watched the crowd pop off to the DJ’s vinyl selection: “I just wanted a place where it’s relaxed…. You don’t need a reservation, you can pull up in a big group. We’re tribal beings.”

Tonight, the singer in the open window has a lovely voice and a kewpie doll tattoo. She cheekily informs us that Alvaro on guitars is single. The night is warm; I’m in a vintage lemon yellow Frederick’s of Hollywood slip. How decadent to have a vermouth on the rocks and sweet treats — affogato, flan, a silky strawberry panna cotta — under the half moon slung low in the sky.

The band sings “Las Mañanitas” for a Gen Zer in kitten heels celebrating her birthday on the bougainvillea-fringed patio. Turns out the abuelita in a white knit dress a few stools down shares a birthday — she pulls the granny up to dance.

They’ve got the moves. We clap for them. People call out birthday wishes in Spanish. It’s a beautiful thing to see, two strangers holding hands. I think about Andres and I discussing whether Angelenos are friendlier than East Coasters because they’re compensating for the relative shortage of chance encounters.

As “Bésame Mucho” starts up, the abuelita’s husband, a mustachioed gent in suspenders, asks her for a dance. Without a word, they move — rumba hips, waltz steps — in perfect, knowing sync.

“How long do you think they’ve been together?” I ask Jonny.

The red neon casting a Hopper-esque glow over the parking lot flickers as a train goes by. He smiles as he watches them.

Jonny and I drop by Capri Club on a weekday afternoon. Look, if you know your local’s weekly specials by heart, you’re probably staying awhile. Mondays, lasagna. Today is Pasta Tuesday.

We share a tortelloni, handmade moments ago, each piece lusciously coated with butter and sage. Francesco Allegro, their Puglian chef, lets me peek into the kitchen, where he kneads a ribbon of dry white into the dough. When he’s done, he joins us for an espresso on the sidewalk.

We’re sharing a Chinese clove cigarette, dissecting what a good hang means. “It’s not even about the drink, you know?” says Pete St. Peter, Capri’s beverage director. That’s a lot, coming from the babe who invented the beloved Capri Sour. “It’s about feeling natural and unencumbered.”

“In Italy when you hang out, it doesn’t feel like an appointment,” Francesco chimes in. “You go to the piazza, you don’t even say a time, because you know the rhythm.”

Right then, a guy in a Talking Heads shirt waves at Francesco. “Hihi.” He’s a regular who just vacationed in Rome, wants to tell Francesco about all the pasta he ate there. “You Italians do everything perfect, except burgers.” Francesco belly laughs.

After he leaves, Francesco marvels. “This is just what we were talking about, no?” He pauses. “Something that you couldn’t plan feels like home.”

Cities are made of buildings and roads, zoning and transit. You can do it by rote, or you can let your routines be greased by chance.

Jonny and I move to the corner stools by the bar — our usual spot.

Paradoxically, at this time of the day, the light is more golden on the inside than the outside. A brunette with a Bobby Special — beer and a shot of Ferrari, fernet branca and a house blend of red bitters — reads Virginie Despentes’ “Dear Dickhead” alone at the bar. We run into our pal, Travis, two martinis deep with his manager in a booth. Francesco sends over a delicious amatriciana made with crisp-edged guanciale. Almost behind my back, a collection of chance encounters has been acquiring the density of a new life.

I look at Jonny, and think: I live here now.

I don’t realize I’ve said it aloud until he pulls me closer.

Slowly, and then fast all at once, the sun goes down. Behind the bar, warm orange lights come on. Sixties Jamaican dub plays at a perfect volume; the last bottle of green Chartreuse is getting decanted into shot glasses. One gets passed to me, then Jonny.

Everyone looks everyone else in the eye as we raise the glasses.

It’s L.A.; we’d rather die than risk seven years of bad sex.

We clink glasses, bash them once against the counter. Salud.

Germany has long resisted tougher action against China, fearing retaliation. But rising pressure on key industries has pushed Berlin to seek stronger EU trade-defense powers to protect its industrial base.

Germany's cautious approach toward Beijing is cracking. For years, Berlin blocked a harder line on what the European Union describes as China's  unfair trade practices.

In the last few months, that caution — a fear of Chinese retaliation that could hurt German exporters — has given way to a willingness to escalate.

Last week, Chancellor Friedrich Merz and French President Emmanuel Macron wrote jointly to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen seeking a new tool to cut a trading partner off from the EU's single market if unfair practices continue.

Clearly aimed at Beijing, the letter warned the measure was necessary against countries that "deliberately seek to undermine ... fair market conditions by political or economic means."

China's Commerce Ministry warned Europe would "suffer the consequences" if it adopted such a "kill-switch" plan, which was timed to coincide with crucial EU-China trade talks in Beijing last week.

Noah Barkin, a senior adviser with research consultancy Rhodium Group's China practice, told DW that the threats to Germany's export powerhouse "have convinced Merz and his closest advisers that the benefits of action outweigh the costs of inaction."

Before the COVID pandemic, the two economies rose in tandem and German industry benefited from China's ascent.

Since 2021, however, China has fallen from Germany's second-largest export market to its ninth, according to data from Germany Trade & Invest for the first half of 2026.

Chinese competitors have quickly closed the technology gap and benefit from much lower costs and state subsidies. That pressure is being felt especially in Germany's automaking, precision engineering and chemicals industries.

German vehicle makers have suffered a large sales drop in China, which was once their second-largest market. According to data from Rhodium Group, German car exports to China plummeted by 66% between 2022 and 2025.

Big German brands now face heavy rivalry from a new wave of Chinese electric vehicle (EV) manufactures in other major markets, including Europe, which is forcing a wave of layoffs and possible factory closures.

In Germany alone, imports of new Chinese-made cars rose 121% in the first seven months of the year, according to Destatis, the federal statistics agency. Overall, Chinese-made vehicles accounted for nearly 14% of total vehicle imports.

"After continuously losing market share in China ... it [the rivalry] seems to be hitting their business within the European automotive market," Andreas Mischer, an associate fellow at the Berlin-based Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS), told DW.

Mischer said pressure from Germany's powerful automotive lobby was key to Berlin's "shifting attitude toward trade defense instruments."

Among the many German business leaders who have demanded a more hard-line approach in recent months, Oliver Richtberg, head of foreign trade at VDMA, Germany's mechanical engineering association, was perhaps the most blunt:

"We are currently being deindustrialized through unfair measures — and most companies are no longer willing to accept this," Richtberg told Welt am Sonntag newspaper in August.

According to Eurostat, the wider EU's goods deficit with China was around €360 billion ($402 billion) last year — effectively €1 billion per day — and widened again in the first half of 2026.

While Brussels has been pushing back against the dumping of cheap Chinese goods, a separate industrial protection law and other anti-coercion measures are running into trouble from Beijing and at home.

The EU's Industrial Accelerator Act, proposed in March, aims to pull manufacturing investment back to Europe by favoring EU-based firms in public contracts and subsidies.

Foreign investments in key sectors would only be approved if European partners keep majority control, which Beijing has framed as discriminatory.

#1234. 125 years of Thomas Mann's 'Buddenbrooks'

This over 1,000-page masterpiece offers a panoramic view of society in the 19th century and exposes the collapse of a merchant family: unrecognized sons, disillusioned daughters and the loss of middle-class ideals.

Who would have imagined it in the beginning? A 26-year-old man without a high school diploma, but greatly ambitious, writes a two-pound book with a melancholy undertone. "I'm not exactly delighted," his publisher initially responded. But he ended up publishing the novel anyway, more out of goodwill than conviction. And the rest is history.

For many, Thomas Mann's Buddenbrooks is considered THE German social novel. It is indeed a classic, and one that garnered the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929. Global fame, an international best-seller, and required reading for many German students. Anyone who mentions Thomas Mann thinks of Buddenbrooks in the same breath. Why is that?

Does the explanation lie in the autobiographical connections between the work and the author?

Thomas Mann was born in Lübeck to a merchant family, but his artistic aspirations did not fit in well in the middle-class mainstream. This struggle inspired Mann's first work. The inability of this sensitive bohemian to carry on the family's time-honored business after the death of his father was sniffed at and derided.

Reflecting the situation of his novel's character Hanno, Mann had a privileged life in a wealthy family, but one that was at risk of collapse. The aspiring novelist also had to withstand disappointing the expectations of others. He was forced to defend his dream of an artistic life. Yet as much as he was inspired by real life, he forcefully altered these biographical details to place his unique literary mark on his writing.

But does that really matter in light of people who choose not to make the distinction between an author's life and his work? Even though the name "Lübeck" does not appear in the novel, citizens there felt markedly insulted after the publication of Buddenbrooks in 1901.

That likely lies in the fact that the image the author portrayed was not one of an idyllic northern German city, but one of a place that had difficulty blending tradition with modernity.

This contradiction is also seen in each of the figures in the novel. Nothing is like it appears. Mann lays down this foundation of this skepticism from the very beginning, in a scene taking place on a very ordinary Thursday in October 1835. The Buddenbrook family comes together for their ordinary late lunch.

"There they all sat, on heavy, high-backed chairs, consuming good heavy food from good silver plates, drinking full-bodied wines and expressing their views freely on all subjects. When they began to talk shop, they slipped unconsciously more and more into dialect, and used the clumsy but comfortable idioms that seemed to embody to them the business efficiency and the easy well-being of their community. Sometimes they even used an over-drawn pronunciation by way of making fun of themselves and each other, and relished their clipped phrases and exaggerated vowels with the same heartiness they did their food."

There sits the leader of the family company, Johann Buddenbrook the Elder, and his wife, his son and counsel Jean, along with his family. The grandchildren are also there: Tony the dreamer, the quarrelsome rebel Christian, and Thomas, the eldest grandchild.

It is Thomas who is supposed to later take on the responsibility of continuing the family tradition, regardless of the cost. Mann weaves his narrative work starting with this main family, and then across several generations.

But appearances in the family are deceptive. The reader senses it's all headed toward a downfall. The new house is taken over from a family that was once as illustrious as the Buddenbrooks, but faced financial ruin and had to leave.

Johann and his family aim to make a better go of it. They make decisions on what is demanded economically, but fail — financially, interpersonally, and mentally.

Tony and Thomas end up falling in love with people not befitting their social standing, but do not have the courage to stand up to their parents. They enter marriages of convenience, yet it is these partnerships that paradoxically set disaster in motion.

From an early age, Thomas Mann was influenced by Schopenhauer's pessimistic notion of the will being a blind force at work in the world. Thus, there is likewise no escape for the Buddenbrooks. They speak of emotions and morality, yet it is money and economics that steer the ship. Failure, decline and even death seem to be the only viable outcome.

Mann also punctuates this hopelessness in a stylistic manner, by consistently repeating entire passages word for word, or evoking motifs such as the family registry or the estate in all their various forms.

The extent of this demise appears linguistically molded and exaggerated. The Buddenbrooks, this proud north German family of refinement, actually speaks the vernacular studded with empty French expressions. "Je, den Düwel ook, c'est la question, ma très chère demoiselle!"

The lowly servants, the courtesans, and the demi-monde: all of them speak the language of the street, raw and unpolished. In a virtuoso manner, Thomas Mann orchestrated this mass of voices into a panorama of society in the 19th century. The snobbishness on the part of the Buddenbrooks thus appears as a relic of a long lost time. It's a symbol of their inability to adapt to change and place their faith in progress.

Yet the fact that this extensive work spanning several generations does not get weighed down by aesthetic excess is purely due to Mann's brilliance as an author. He is able to bring every one of his numerous characters to life in words. Life that is full of complexity, contradictions and ambiguities. Some of these characters accompany the reader page for page; others appear only briefly.

And yet one begins to contemplate how someone like Grünlich could become a dodgy, unscrupulous husband and catches oneself wanting to chase the fearful Hanno out of the music conservatory and yell to him: "Go, throw yourself into life! You are not a failure." It is precisely this empathy that spurs one on to read through the night and prompts one to chuckle at all the intricate sentences and philosophical digressions.

#1237. The Fight for Educational Equality Is Also a Fight for a Just Supreme Court

The court has severely weakened our ability to make an education system that fulfills the promise of Brown.

Last month, a piece by New York Times journalist Nikole Hannah-Jones sparked conversations across America about race, education, and parenting. In the piece, Hannah-Jones described in painstaking—and often painful— detail the consequences of the deliberate decision she and her husband made to send their daughter to a mostly Black, lower-income, underperforming New York City public school rather than a school with better educational prospects and, perhaps, fewer Black students. Hannah-Jones’s story is personal, but it also represents one that Black parents in the United States have faced for generations: How do we make the best choices for our children in an education system that has never kept its promise to them?

For Black parents, the question of where to send our children to school has never been simply a matter of parental preference. It has been shaped by government decisions regarding where we are allowed to live, how resources are allocated, how school districts are drawn, and ultimately what the Supreme Court of the United States considers to be a fair and just education for our kids.

While much of the current debate has boiled down to scrutiny over which choices are right for Black parents, it is imperative that we ask the deeper question running through Hannah-Jones’s piece: Why has any Black parent been forced to make such consequential trade-offs in the first place?

Long before the Legal Defense Fund, the organization I lead, won the Brown v. Board of Education case in 1954, ending state-sponsored racial segregation in public schools, Black Americans understood education as essential to freedom. Immediately after Emancipation, Black communities got to work building schools, training teachers, and raising money to invest in educational pursuits because they knew this would help smooth their rocky path to full citizenship.

In doing so, Black communities were helping to establish the very infrastructure of universal public education. Across the South, according to the National Park Service, newly enfranchised Black voters and elected officials fought diligently “to create the frameworks for public education in the 1860s and 1870s.” In 1868, Black state legislators in South Carolina helped pass laws that created one of the first statewide systems of public schools in the nation.

Brown was a constitutional milestone in the ongoing struggle to ensure that Black children could receive a quality education in the same public school system their ancestors and communities worked to build. Yet, over 70 years later, Black families must still navigate the education system of a country that fails to make it real, too often forcing them into choosing between their child’s psychological safety and the quality of their educational opportunities.

That’s in part because, in the decades after Brown, the Supreme Court has repeatedly weakened the structures that might have lessened the need for Black families to consider these trade-offs. As a result, our public schools continue to struggle with hyper-segregation.

Take 1973’s San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez. In that case, the court upheld a Texas school funding system that overly relied on local property taxes, despite the fact that such a system produced significant differences in resources available to different districts due to state-sponsored racial residential segregation and its residual effects. This ruling, in effect, failed all students seeking government protections from an unequally funded school system, and was reinforced by the fact that education is not a fundamental right under the United States Constitution.

Nearly 30 years later, in Alexander v. Sandoval, the court held that individuals and other impacted parties could not use civil rights statutes, like Title VI, to challenge policies and practices, including those in the education system, that disproportionately harm certain children on account of race.

And in 2006’s notorious Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School District No. 1, the Supreme Court cannibalized its own legacy by striking down valiant efforts by Seattle school districts to maintain racially diverse schools through parent-led initiatives.

Taken together, these decisions cut off avenues to enact true change and fulfill Brown’s promise, instead placing the burden on families to navigate a defective system. That is the true tension at the heart of the school-choice debate that Hannah-Jones’s confessional conjures. And that is why LDF is fighting to advance policies that aim to expand educational opportunities across the country, including in New York, where we represent a multiracial coalition of students seeking an equal chance to compete for admission to New York City’s specialized high schools.

The broader debate over the future of the Supreme Court cannot be separated from the debate over the future of public education. The court, ironically, once described public education as “the very foundation of good citizenship.” But it has issued critical decisions that undermine that project and ignore that high-quality, equally accessible public education is essential for our multiracial, multiethnic democracy to thrive.

The lesson from Hannah-Jones’s experiences is not that individual parents need to make “better” decisions; it is that our institutions need to give every family a meaningful choice. At its core, Brown promised America’s children equal educational opportunity and equal citizenship. Our branches of government, including the judiciary, must ensure that our society is legally and lawfully equipped to fulfill that promise.

For decades, the Supreme Court has been unwilling to do so, at the expense of tens of millions of children subjected to a woefully substandard education in taxpayer-funded schools. A court that is unwilling to fully uphold the breadth of its canonical decision on equality cannot be entrusted to define the boundaries of our Constitution and the rules of our democracy without appropriate checks on its authority. That’s why the fight for educational equality also requires a fight for a just Supreme Court. Otherwise, most Black families will continue to have school choice in name only, the scourge of school segregation will persist, and America’s foundation of good citizenship will remain unlaid.

#1242. DR MAX PEMBERTON: Heart disease is the hidden killer of women over 40. It's alarming doctors often miss these symptoms. It kills twice as many women as breast cancer. But you can improve your odds

As a champion of women’s fitness and wellbeing, Davina McCall has long been a trusted voice in highlighting important health issues faced by women in mid-life and beyond.

Not only has she been influential in urging women to take the menopause seriously but she’s also been remarkably candid about her brain tumour and breast cancer treatments – speaking out in the hope her experience would help others.

So her disclosure last week, in her podcast Begin Again, that she also has cardiovascular disease, was typically brave as well as important.

Davina, 59, said she’d had high cholesterol for ‘quite a long time’, emphasising that heart disease is rife in women after the menopause – yet it’s an issue that is often overlooked.

Cardiovascular disease, or CVD, is an umbrella term for conditions affecting the heart and blood vessels.

The four main types are coronary heart disease (where the arteries supplying the heart become narrowed by fatty deposits), stroke, peripheral arterial disease (which affects the arteries to the legs) and diseases of the aorta, the body’s main artery. Between them they cause about a quarter of all deaths in the UK, roughly 170,000 a year, which is one every three minutes.

More than eight million Britons are living with CVD, and over four million of them are women. Yet ask most women what they fear most and they’ll say breast cancer.

Davina McCall is a trusted voice in highlighting important health issues faced by women in mid-life

In fact, coronary heart disease kills more than twice as many women as breast cancer in this country. We still think of the heart attack as a man’s problem – the overweight businessman clutching his chest – and that misconception is costing lives. Research funded by the British Heart Foundation found that women are 50 per cent more likely than men to be given the wrong initial diagnosis when they have a heart attack, and women often wait longer before getting help.

And midlife is when the risk for women changes dramatically. Before the menopause, oestrogen offers a degree of protection, helping to keep blood vessels supple and cholesterol in check. As it falls, that protection goes. A large American study that tracked women through the menopause found that levels of LDL, the so-called bad cholesterol, rise sharply in the year either side of a woman’s final period.

The cruel thing about CVD is that its early stages are usually silent. High blood pressure and high cholesterol cause no symptoms at all, which is why CVD is often only discovered after something has gone wrong.

Chest pain or tightness remain the most common signs of a heart attack, but these may be accompanied by, or overshadowed by, breathlessness, nausea, sweating, light-headedness, or pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back.

Angina, the chest discomfort that comes on with exertion and eases with rest, is another warning the arteries are narrowing, as is getting unusually breathless or exhausted doing things that were once easy.

A woman in her fifties was referred to me because her panic attacks weren’t responding to treatment. She described a tight chest and breathlessness. But when I asked her more, it became clear that the episodes came on when she climbed the stairs or hurried for a bus and eased within a few minutes of stopping. That’s the classic pattern of angina.

I wrote to her GP asking for an urgent referral to the rapid access chest pain clinic, and a scan showed narrowing of the arteries supplying her heart.

The consequences of ignoring CVD are severe.

Untreated, it can lead to heart attack, stroke, heart failure and vascular dementia. But the good news is that most of the risk is modifiable, and the earlier you act the bigger difference it makes.

The good news about cardiovascular disease, says Dr Max, is that it's preventable

Start with food. A Mediterranean style of eating – with plenty of vegetables, pulses, wholegrains, oily fish, olive oil, nuts and seeds – has good evidence behind it.

Cut back on saturated fat from processed meat, pastries and cheese, keep salt down, and get plenty of fibre, which helps lower cholesterol. Keep alcohol within the guideline of 14 units a week and, if you smoke, stop.

Aim for at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, the kind that leaves you a little out of breath, and add some strength work twice a week.

Look after your sleep, too, and keep an eye on your waistline, as fat around the middle is bad for the heart.

Then get your numbers checked. If you’re aged 40-74 and don’t already have a heart condition, you’re entitled to a free NHS Health Check every five years.

If you haven’t been invited, ask your GP surgery or pharmacy. Tell them if a close relative had heart disease or a stroke before the age of 60.

If your risk is 10 per cent or more, you’ll usually be offered a statin, and I’d urge women not to dismiss them out of hand because of scare stories online.

Above all, if you get chest pain or any other symptoms of a heart attack, call 999. Don’t wait to see if it passes, and don’t worry about making a fuss.

I hope Davina’s revelation gets more women in midlife to improve their heart health.

It’s hard for books about technology to age well. They always risk becoming outdated as companies rise and fall, and as one hot new product is replaced by the next.

But last year, “Nerd Reich” author Gil Durán declared that it was time to revive the career of Paulina Borsook, who he said “saw it all very clearly and tried to warn us 25 years ago.” Later that fall, The New York Times reported that Borsook’s “Cyberselfish” — first published in 2000 — was finding new fans who want to understand why the tech industry “lost its way.”

Now, long after going out of print, “Cyberselfish” is returning in a new edition from Tin House.

Borsook told me in a Zoom interview that the book is meant to be “almost a travelogue” of her attempts to understand what she calls the techno-libertarian influence on Silicon Valley culture.

“That’s a 12-year-old view of the world,” Borsook said, describing it as “a kind of basket of beliefs” that’s “violently anti-regulation,” despite the fact that “no sector of society had benefited more and suffered less from the government than these guys.” In her telling, “even after the dot-com crash, that attitude was just there, percolating around, contaminating the aquifers. It was uncorrected, and it just spread out more and more.”

Borsook has yet to publish a second book, and she said that after “Cyberselfish” was first released, “no one was interested in anything I might have to say about anything, tech or not tech, forever after.”

But last year, she began to notice that prices for used copies were beginning to creep up, and she was starting to see “very out-of-the-blue, I-did-not-know-these-people posts on social media about how important this book was,” as well as inquiries from “European academics,” eventually leading to more press coverage and the new Tin House edition, with a new introduction by Durán.

Do you want to talk a little bit about how your interest in techno-libertarianism started, and how you decided that there was a book in this?

Everything with me is always inadvertent. I started knocking around tech circles, however we define it, in the early-middle ‘80s, in the Bay Area. And then I would say, starting around maybe the early ‘90s, I would hear these — to me — very weird, offhand remarks that some people would make that I couldn’t make any sense out of.

It’s not my nature to argue with people. It’s my nature to listen, and so I didn’t have the term for it except libertarian. But it seemed very screwy because initially, I grew up in Pasadena, which was [the home of the] Caltech-JPL space program, and so the parents of the kids I knew were another STEM culture. When I first got into Silicon Valley, I thought, “Oh, this is just another STEM culture like I’m familiar with.” And those guys [at Caltech and JPL]  had a very clear sense of what the government can do for you, were not into enlightened self-interest. It was a different personality type, and they certainly didn’t self-anoint with how glorious and grand and magisterial they were, even though they were doing great things.

I was just listening, I guess, and being confused. Even then, I knew no sector of society had benefited more and suffered less from the government than these guys. So why are they acting like this?

So, backstory: My agent picked me up right after my MFA in Columbia, and he said to me, “You know, it’s really hard to get fiction published. Do you have a nonfiction book in you?” And I said, “Well, there’s this weird subculture that I’ve been observing that I don’t understand, and any time I don’t understand something, I want to explore it.” That got me down the rabbit hole of looking at this stuff and writing about it, and I consider it really almost a travelogue, in the sense of “Black Lamb and Gray Falcon” or that kind of stuff, almost more than, “This is a polemic.” It’s more like: snapshot, snapshot, snapshot.

I just fell into it, and also I’m sufficiently narcissistic that I felt I was uniquely qualified to write about this, and no one else was paying any attention, and that was very important to me.

I haven’t done a backread of all the other books written about the tech industry before “Cyberselfish.” My sense is that you do recognize, say, reading Steven Levy’s work that there is this alternative philosophy, libertarian streak running through Silicon Valley. That’s really true. And then most of the writing — certainly in the ’90s, but maybe later — was very celebratory. It wasn’t, “Wait a minute, what’s going on here?”

He said, “Silicon Valley is a multicultural place.” By which he didn’t mean your country of origin. What he meant is there’s all these different kinds of cultures in there, and he was always more focused on these countercultural [elements,] that kind of thing.

I’m never saying that wasn’t there. I’m just saying there was this other thing that I was more interested in. So they’re running in parallel. I don’t even think they’re in conflict.

I named them after George Gilder, who was this repellent thought leader of the early ’90s, very conservative, very reactionary, very backward-looking. Another weird thing we always have to point out is that the [San Francisco] peninsula votes blue, still. Ro Khanna is the congressman from there. We can point to people who are in charge, who have these [right-wing] beliefs or are allied with the Trump administration. There’s another executive class that maybe doesn’t have exactly the same beliefs, but is able to make themselves very comfortable with it.

#1247. Could Mississippi Really Send a Democrat to the Senate?

Think about the races that are likely to determine control of the Senate in this year’s midterm elections. The chances are pretty high that the Mississippi Senate contest doesn’t feature on your list. After all, the last time the state sent a Democrat to the Senate was in 1982. But a growing number of observers on the ground in that deepest of Deep South states believe there’s more than a fighting chance that Democratic nominee Scott Colom, the district attorney for Mississippi’s 16th Circuit, could unseat sitting GOP Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith.

Hyde-Smith, whose lead in recent polls ranges from 3 to 6 percent, has what critics might call “baggage.” In November 2018, she raised a storm of controversy by stating that if she were invited to a public hanging, she would want a front-row seat. In a state with an ugly history of racist mob killings, this was widely taken as a shout-out to vigilante lynching. More recently, Hyde-Smith has hailed both the Supreme Court’s evisceration of the Voting Rights Act and the stampede toward mid-decade redistricting in the South, aimed largely at erasing Black-majority congressional districts. She has also been a particularly vociferous Trump supporter. She failed to stand up to Trump on tariffs, which have hit farmers in rural states such as Mississippi particularly hard, and she uttered not a peep of protest against last year’s “one big, beautiful bill,” even though the bill’s vast cuts to Medicaid and SNAP hit a poor state like Mississippi, which gets far more back in dollars from federal programs than it sends to DC in taxes, particularly hard. More than 100,000 of the state’s residents will lose Medicaid in the coming years because of the bill that Hyde-Smith voted for; tens of thousands of Mississippians will also lose their SNAP benefits, and hundreds of local grocery stores, reliant on income from customers using SNAP, will be put at risk of going under.

In fact, the only thing on which Hyde-Smith has distanced herself (albeit ever-so-mildly) from Trump is his recent decision to flood the beef market with imports of cheap beef—a move that, as a cattle farmer herself, Hyde-Smith knows is likely to hurt the American cattle industry.

Historically, Mississippi, whose population is roughly 37 percent African American, has been one of the most racially divided states in the country. For a hundred years after the Civil War, it was the epicenter of the Jim Crow South, and the Mississippi Democratic Party was a redoubt of segregationist white politics. In the civil rights era, as the national Democratic Party embraced the Voting Rights Act and other reforms that, cumulatively, dismantled the Jim Crow infrastructure, the parties flipped: Increasingly, whites in Mississippi aligned with a GOP that was veering ever more rightward, while the African American electorate moved into the Democratic Party’s camp. The divisions have remained so rigid in recent decades that, even as the African American electorate expanded, the white majority’s overwhelming adherence to the GOP ensured continued Republican dominance in statewide elections.

But the electoral math has been slowly changing in recent years. Since 2018, the size of GOP victories in statewide races has frequently shrunken to a few tens of thousands of votes. In 2023, incumbent Republican Governor Tate Reeves won reelection by only 27,000 votes—the state’s closest gubernatorial race since 1999. “We’ve been chipping away over the last more than half-decade,” Charles Taylor, executive director of the Mississippi NAACP, told The Nation. “Mississippians are fed up. That’s going to translate at the ballot box. There’s a lot of voter education going on. Voters in Mississippi and across the country are seeing the midterm elections as an existential crisis.”

In an election year in which many GOP voters are increasingly uncomfortable with the country’s direction under Trump, and with African American voters hyper-motivated to vote in the wake of the recent assaults on voting rights from the Supreme Court and a string of Southern state legislatures, Democrats have an opportunity to over-perform in the November Senate election. “We have a fighting chance here,” says Taylor. “And the country will be surprised by what happens.”

In a year with high African American voter participation—Democratic turnout in the 2026 primary was up 90 percent compared with the previous electoral cycle—only 20 percent of white voters would need to vote for Colom for the Democrat to flip the Senate seat. Given the conservative bent of white Mississippians, it’s still a long shot, but certainly not an impossibility. “There are a lot of social justice and civil rights [themes] bubbling up in Mississippi and throughout the South, and one of those has to do with what the US Supreme Court did,” Mississippi-based civil rights attorney Carroll Rhodes explained. “There’s a lot of hostility and anger and angst—and motivation within the African American community to vote and not to lose their right to vote. There’s probably going to be a bigger turnout than usual.”

“The demographics have been trending in this direction,” Derrick Johnson, president and CEO of the NAACP, told The Nation. “The current economic climate and restlessness with policies that have not worked, all of those things—people see it is possible to win a statewide race.” For Johnson, the confluence of national and state issues represents a potential tipping point for Mississippi. “Any candidate that speaks up to an administration in this moment who are taking resources out of Mississippians’ pockets will do well. The quality of life for Mississippians is not improving. We don’t have a single Fortune 500 company headquartered in this state. People are looking for a change.”

Colom, an African American attorney whose mother was a judge and father was a Republican who worked for Ronald Reagan, believes he can tap into that desire for a new politics. “We’re a state that can’t afford the corruption we’re seeing in DC right now,” he told The Nation. “Our farmers are already starting to feel the tariffs; fertilizer prices are spiking because of the war in Iran; and now the president is importing cheap beef. To me, what’s happened is a betrayal. They told the farmers, ‘We’re taking your votes for granted.’ It’s so stark, and the betrayal is so personal.”

Colom believes that in the lead-up to the election he can erase Hyde-Smith’s small but persistent lead in the polls. “A lot of people are going to be surprised,” he said. “But I’m not going to be surprised, and a lot of people in Mississippi won’t be surprised. You defeat the corruption by showing up and using your power in a moment when they’re trying to take it away.”
