#1551. Jürgen Klopp faces myriad of issues after rough start with Germany

Jürgen Klopp has done things a little differently since becoming Germany coach. Casting a wider net for talent and trying to connect with players and country haven't made an immediate impact, but he is not bowed.

Four games, three goals, two draws, one loss and one win. Jürgen Klopp would have hoped for better results from his first international break as Germany coach. But though the comfortable win over Serbia (2-0), draws against the Netherlands (1-1) and Greece (0-0) and a shock loss to Greece (0-1) in Germany leave them third out of four in their Nations League group, Klopp saw mostly positives.

"Everyone showed what they're made of; everyone earned their place. We've shown signs that we can do some things better than people gave us credit for. I wanted to get something going, and I definitely saw that happen," he said after Sunday's 0-0 draw in Thessaloniki.

For all the positivity of the former Liverpool and Borussia Dortmund coach, the four matches have, in reality, been a mixed bag for Germany.

Having fallen in the last 32 of the 2026 World Cup and exited in the group stage in 2022, Germany's footballing stock has never been lower.

Klopp tried to address this by casting his net wider, announcing a 44-man squad that split over the matches. He then said this would be a one off, but that his strategy of trying to find a diamond in the relative rough who could adapt to the international game had given him valuable insights.

"Hard work, lots of information — these guys are brimming with talent," he said of those given a surprise opportunity.

"People were saying that German soccer was at rock bottom and that there was no talent. ... They're not lacking in talent."

While Klopp may have seen potential, and has credit in the bank from his past work, he will need to turn that into results before too long.

While much has been said about Klopp's success in imprinting his trademark Gegenpressing (counter-press) style early on, he is also a coach who places a high value on connection. Throughout the international break, he has worn a cap emblazoned with the slogan "Einer von 83 millionen” (One of 83 million) in a reference to the German population and an attempt to unify in a country divided by the rise of the far-right AfD party.

He addressed the issue in his opening press conference and then reiterated his position after some social media users branded the team "not white enough" after their loss to Greece.

"There is no reason to give these people any thought. They aren't getting any of my attention. I have little time for stupidity in real life and none on social media either," Klopp said in answer to a question posed by DW.

As well as speaking honestly about his country, Klopp has been open with his new players.

"He has a very good rapport with every player. He tries to reach out to every player and make them feel good, that they're important and that they deserve to be here," said Florian Wirtz, who went on to label his coach as a "master" at managing relationships.

With limited time together, forming bonds between player, coach and country can elevate a national team. It may prove to be more important than any tactical plans.

One of the key men in the more senior squad that started the opener against the Netherlands, Kai Havertz limped off after 29 minutes. He was replaced by Eintracht Frankfurt's Younes Ebnoutalib while Nick Woltemade (on loan at Juventus from Newcastle) and Jonathan Burkhardt, also Frankfurt, were also tested up front over the four games.

None of the replacements scored, with Woltemade guilty of a particularly bad miss against Serbia and Burkhardt having a goal disallowed against Greece on Sunday. While all have potential and have enjoyed strong patches of form in their careers, none is tried and tested at the top level, and none is playing in the Champions League. With Havertz being somewhat injury prone, Klopp needs someone to emerge as a viable backup.

Injury is also, sadly, beginning to be a major feature of Jamal Musiala's career. He picked up a thigh injury in the warmup to the Netherlands game that may keep him out for a month. He's also missed games this season due to a neurological condition and missed the first half of last season with a broken leg, which came soon after another lengthy spell out. At his best, he is probably Germany's brightest attacking spark, but it's been a while since that level has been seen.

For Wirtz, the issue is slightly different. He was excellent in the win over a limited Serbia team, scoring one and creating the other. It was a reminder of a talent that has been largely dormant at Liverpool where he's shown his creative best only in glimpses. He followed it up with a decent display against Greece but saw Burkahrdt inadvertently block his goalbound header. There were signs Klopp's system may suit him.

"He's really gotten us on board with his philosophy," said Wirtz of his coach. "I enjoy it myself, too. I get to show my strengths when we have possession."

Klopp will need more of the same from Wirtz, who captained the side in Greece, if Germany are to move forward.

#1558. Where Is the Outrage Over Trump’s Racist Targeting of Ilhan Omar?

The establishment press and some Democrats are strangely mute over the president’s flagrant abuse of the legal system.

Donald Trump has long fantasized about deporting Representative Ilhan Omar. In 2019, Trump lashed out against Omar and her congressional colleagues Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Rashida Tlaib, and Ayanna Pressley—all left-wing Democrats and women of color and part of the “Squad.” Deploying a familiar xenophobic jibe, Trump taunted, “Why don’t they go back and help fix the totally broken and crime infested places from which they came.” For three of the Squad, Trump’s threat was vile but empty: Ocasio-Cortez, Tlaib, and Pressley were all born in the United States.

But for Omar, Trump’s words carried a direct intent of harm. Omar was born in Somalia, came to the United States at age 8 as a refugee, and was eventually granted citizenship at age 17 in 2000. On Thursday, The Washington Post reported that in 2019, the same year Trump was telling Omar to “go back” to where she came from, his administration ordered immigration officials to comb through her personal records to find evidence of fraud that could lead to her removal from the US. This fishing expedition came up empty-handed.

Never one to be defeated by a mere lack of evidence, Trump has persisted in calling for Omar’s deportation ever since—calls that have ramped up over the last year. In January, he said Omar should be sent “back to Somalia,” a call he repeated the following month. In March, Vice President JD Vance joined in, bluntly declaring, without evidence, that Omar “definitely committed immigration fraud.”

There are ample reasons why Omar has been one of Trump’s favorite punching bags. As a leading leftist in Congress, she is a natural antagonist. But, more importantly, as a fearless Somali American woman, she excites all the passions of racism, xenophobia, and misogyny that animate Trump and his MAGA movement.

Given the long history of Trump’s vendetta against Omar, it was ultimately not surprising when the Department of Homeland Security raided her home this week in search of evidence of immigration fraud, a move that The Wall Street Journal reports is motivated by a goal of stripping her of her citizenship.

The campaign against Omar stinks to high heaven of partisanship and bigotry. It is also part of a larger pattern of Trump’s weaponization of law enforcement to punish perceived enemies. As The New Yorker documented recently, Trump’s persecutions have done immense harm to the Department of Justice:

Numerous attorneys have been pushed out or fired after refusing to prosecute such Trump enemies as the former F.B.I. director James Comey and the New York attorney general Letitia James. When these vindictive prosecutions eventually fell under scrutiny in courtrooms, they didn’t fare well. It is extremely rare for grand juries to dismiss charges brought by federal prosecutors, but the case against James, who was accused of committing mortgage fraud, has been rejected twice. Indictments of James and Comey were initially secured by Lindsey Halligan, a lawyer and a former beauty-pageant contestant whom Trump had appointed as the U.S. Attorney for the Eastern District of Virginia, even though she’d had no experience as a prosecutor. But a magistrate judge subsequently reprimanded her for a “disturbing pattern of profound investigative missteps,” including repeatedly misstating the law to grand jurors. Another judge then dismissed both cases, ruling that Halligan had been appointed unlawfully.

But there’s a striking fact that sets the malicious prosecution apart: Unlike earlier victims of Trump’s lawless abuse of the justice system, Omar isn’t getting a full-throated defense from the political and media elite.

Omar does have people fighting on her side. As my Nation colleague John Nichols reports, she remains popular with her constituents in Minnesota, and the state’s Democratic Party has rallied to her side. House minority leader Hakeem Jeffries decried Trump’s “vile personal attacks” and the fact that Trump’s “violent mass deportation machine has descended upon the home of a duly elected Member of Congress and her family.” Even former Republican representative Marjorie Taylor Greene was critical of Trump. In a post on X.com, Greene asked how “pretending to get ready to deport Ilhan Omar is going to bring gas down under $2, make health insurance freaking affordable, make a family’s weekly grocery bill make sense, and stop sending our beloved military off to fight for another fucking foreign war???”

Greene’s pointed rebuke of Trump stands in sharp contrast to Senate minority leader Chuck Schumer, who in the five days since the raid has yet to say one word in defense of Omar.

Schumer’s silence is hardly surprising. Because Omar has been outspoken in her criticism of Israel and the Israel lobby, establishment Democrats such as Schumer have often kept their distance from her.

Omar’s criticism of Israel and her larger anti-war stance are overwhelmingly popular with Democratic Party voters and have majority support with the general public. According to a recent poll by Project 270, 67 percent of the public want to end overseas war, and 51 percent believe Israel has committed genocide in Gaza (as against 29 percent who deny that genocide has occurred). But these positions are less popular with the political and media elite.

Other victims of Trump’s wrath have been turned into media heroes. But Omar is being treated differently. Writing in The Intercept, Adam Johnson drew a pointed contrast between the Omar raid and the August 2025 raid on the home of John Bolton, Trump’s former national security adviser turned critic.

The very day of the Bolton raid, we saw scathing editorials from the New York Times, Washington Post, and Wall Street Journal. Over the next week, each of these newspapers also published separate pieces from their opinion staff opposing the raid, casting doubt on its legitimacy, and otherwise leveling outrage at the obvious power grab. The raid was explicitly condemned in Politico, The New Yorker, and The Atlantic, which published four different indignant columns.

The raid on Omar’s home has thus far received zero of the above media interventions. No editorials, no opinion columns in the Times, Post, or Journal; no preening in The New Yorker or Politico or Atlantic about the erosion of “rule of law.” The Atlantic did run an article on the Omar raid but it was equivocal and Both Sides in its tone…

As Johnson notes, the reason for the difference in treatment is simply one of identity: Omar is a critic of the establishment, while Bolton is a fixture of the national security state.

On his Unpopular Front Substack, John Ganz also noted that the response to the Omar raid has been “muted” and “tepid.” He suspected the reason might be complacency, due to the “repeated failure of this regime to prosecute its enemies.” But Ganz rightly notes that the attack on Omar is one of the most disturbing events of Trump’s presidency, since it upturns the core principles of liberal democracy:

[T]he attack on citizenship itself is both the very essence of the Trump movement and its most dangerous feature. From birtherism to the stolen-election myth, Trumpism can be understood as the notion that some Americans are not really Americans, that their political rights are illegitimate, and that the nation must be purged of these interlopers.

Ganz is right to highlight the dangers of Trump’s actions. One could add that there is a dual threat: Trump’s subversion of liberal democracy is aided and abetted by an establishment that is so committed to fending off outsiders that it can’t be bothered to defend Ilhan Omar at this crucial moment.

#1559. Samsung Galaxy Z Flip8 Review: The Littlest Smartphone That Could

If you crave a compact phone that doesn’t feel like a brick in your pocket and slips easily into a small bag, the flip style is for you. Samsung’s eighth-generation clamshell is seriously refined, and as svelte and slick as it comes. It may not offer a tablet-sized screen or the fashionable new passport shape, but it is arguably the most practical type of folding phone.

Like almost every other phone this year, the Z Flip8 offers tiny improvements over its predecessor and costs $100 more. If you’re wondering whether it’s worth updating from last year’s Z Flip7, it’s not. The Flip8 is a teeny bit lighter and slimmer with a newer processor, and that’s about it. But if you’re looking to dip a toe in folding-phone waters or just want something small, the Z Flip8 should be in the conversation.

Everyone is excited about the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8’s sexy new form factor, but Samsung continues to iterate on the flip phone format for folks who prize compact devices. The Z Flip8 shaves off 0.02 inches (0.55 millimeters) and a third of an ounce (8 grams) compared to its predecessor, making it the slimmest and lightest option in this category. The improved hinge makes the crease slightly less noticeable and a little easier to open. I still found it a bit tricky to open one-handed, but you get the knack after a while. The new hinge is also supposed to be more durable.

The Flip8 has an IP48 rating, so you don’t need to worry about dropping it in the bath, but tiny dust and dirt particles (less than 1 millimeter) can still cause damage. It comes in pink, like my review unit, as well as graphite, cream, and mint. It still has a physical SIM slot, and the power button has a fingerprint sensor in it that works pretty well.

The screens are the same as last year's. It has a sharp 4.1-inch AMOLED display on the outside with a 60- or 120-Hz refresh rate. Open it up to reveal the 6.9-inch main display, also AMOLED but with a variable refresh rate (1 to 120 Hz) that’s better for battery life. Both screens are reasonably bright (2,600 nits peak), and I never struggled to read the screen outdoors.

Flip phones used to get palmed off with lesser processors, but the Z Flip8 boasts the Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 for Galaxy (the same cutting-edge chip that's in its two bigger siblings). It has 12 GB of RAM and 256 GB of storage. You can jump to 512 GB for an extra $200, but there’s no option to increase the RAM.

I encountered no stutters or lag and found performance to be generally excellent, whether jumping in and out of apps, firing up the camera, or gaming. The Z Flip8 can get warm, and this may throttle the processor somewhat if you’re playing a demanding game for a while (more than an hour). This is mostly down to the compact design.

The stereo speakers are better than I expected. They get quite loud without distortion, and they’ll serve you just fine for watching videos. Call quality is also solid, and slamming it shut to end a call never gets tired. I also like opening it to answer calls, but you must enable this in the Phone app settings under Flip options. Connectivity includes 5G (no mmWave), Wi-Fi 7, and Bluetooth 5.4, but there’s no ultra-wideband (UWB) for more precise positioning. The Fold8 models have Bluetooth 6 and UWB.

While it’s very capable, the Z Flip8 is only average when it comes to battery life, another drawback of its size. Samsung put in a 4,300-mAh silicon-carbon battery that’s good enough for casual use. If you’re on your phone a lot, you’ll want a power bank handy. Charging speeds are disappointing at 25 watts wired and 15 watts wireless, and I’m annoyed by Samsung’s refusal to implement the magnetic feature of Qi2.

If you’re a keen photographer, you can get many better camera systems in a range of Android phones for the same money or less. That said, the main 50-megapixel shooter is perfectly good in many situations, and most folks will be happy with the photos it captures. Some noise creeps in when it’s dark. The 12-megapixel ultrawide is useful when you want to cram a lot in or take a group selfie, but it’s a definite step down in quality. Sadly, there’s no telephoto lens, but you can emulate 2X zoom without losing detail by cropping, and it offers passable results in good lighting up to 10X.

I don’t see much reason to use the 10-megapixel punch-hole selfie camera outside of video calls, since you can flip it to get a cover-screen preview and use the main camera for selfies (a big benefit of this design). I also love Samsung’s Horizon Lock, which lets you shoot video with a steady horizontal plane no matter how you turn or move the phone.

Another new AI feature from Samsung is My FanCam, which lets you pick a subject to focus on in group videos and reframes them as though your chosen subject is the star. Handy for school plays or soccer games, if you remember to use it. (You can also apply it to old videos.) Speaking of AI, Samsung offers Now Brief to float up calendar events, reminders, and useful info like local weather, and Now Nudge to suggest adding dates to your calendar from messages, sharing locations or photos, and auto-filling forms.

The cover screen, or FlexWindow, has been a weakness for Samsung’s Flip line, and it’s still limited in the Flip8. On the plus side, you can use more apps with the cover screen, including most of Samsung’s apps (Health, Calendar, Browser), YouTube, Netflix, Google Maps, and Messages. But if you want other third-party apps, you still have to install Good Lock, then the MultiStar plug-in, and select the apps you want to use. It’s stupidly convoluted, and I don’t understand why it isn't preinstalled, especially since Samsung has virtually all its other apps on there, along with a few third-party apps I never asked for (Facebook, Spotify, and Instagram). This is admittedly a bugbear of mine, but it's disgraceful that Samsung is including bloatware on a phone this expensive.

I like that you can have YouTube on the cover screen, as you can prop it up and there’s a little toggle at the bottom to switch between full screen, above the camera lenses, or standard vertical format for scrolling. You’ll mostly use the inner screen, but I have sometimes used the Flip8 this way to prop up a video I’m half-watching on my desk, and it’s a nice option to have.

There’s only really one competitor for Samsung if you’re set on a flip phone, and that’s Motorola's Razr line. You can step down to a less powerful but much cheaper Razr at $800, or spend a little more to snag the superior Razr Ultra. The latter has a better camera, more RAM and storage, and charges much faster, but it costs $1,500. The awkward middle child, the Razr+ at $1,100, is a tougher sell (it has an older processor and shorter software support). You might consider last year’s Z Flip7 if you can find it discounted.

Sadly, this might be your last chance to get a Samsung flip phone. Persistent rumors suggest Samsung will sunset this form factor as the Fold8 takes off and goes head-to-head with the iPhone Duo, though Samsung hasn't officially confirmed it. If you do pull the trigger on the Flip8, you get an excellent seven years of platform and security software updates, potentially taking you all the way to 2033. Who knows, maybe small phones will be back in fashion by then.

#1560. Latest Windows update disables key security setting – but there’s a workaround

A bug in Microsoft's October Windows update prevents you from turning on a security setting that blocks malicious or suspicious apps. A Registry hack fixes the glitch.

Microsoft’s monthly Windows updates always seem to sneak in some new bug that impacts an important feature or setting. And that’s the case with this month’s update.

Also: 5 apps I always install on new Windows PCs – and most are free

Rolled out on October 13, the latest Patch Tuesday update for Windows 11 has a glitch that disables a security setting for Smart App Control. Added to Windows 11 in 2022, Smart App Control blocks potentially malicious or suspicious apps and files from infecting your computer. Stronger and stricter than some of the other built-in security settings, this is designed to protect you against new and emerging threats.

If you’ve already enabled the feature, then it’s still in effect. But if it’s currently off, the bug in the October update grays out the button to turn it on, so there’s no way to activate it. I checked three Windows 11 PCs on which I installed this month’s update and one PC that hasn’t yet been updated. The option is indeed grayed out on the three updated computers, but is still available on the one I haven’t updated.

Microsoft says that it’s working on a fix for this bug and will provide an update in a future release. In the meantime, you can get past the glitch by adding two keys to the Windows Registry. Microsoft has provided the steps for doing that.

Also: This free Windows 11 tool can rescue your PC when things go wrong

First, though, you’ll want to see if you’re affected. Go to Settings, select Privacy & security, and then click Windows Security. In the section for Protection areas, select the one for App & browser control. In the new window that pops up, click the link for Smart App Control settings. If the option is currently set to Off and the one for On is grayed out, then you’ve been hit by the bug.

To get past this, type cmd in the Search box to open a command prompt window. But choose the option to run as administrator; otherwise, you won’t be able to modify the Registry.

At the command prompt, type the following commands one after the other:

reg add “HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Policies\Microsoft\FeatureManagement\Overrides” /v 2883193997 /t REG_DWORD /d 1 /f

reg add “HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Policies\Microsoft\FeatureManagement\Overrides” /v 2850574478 /t REG_DWORD /d 1 /f

After each command, you should receive a confirmation that the operation completed successfully. In that case, close the command prompt and reboot your PC. Log back in and head to the same Smart App Control settings screen. You should now see that the On option is once again available, allowing you to activate the feature.

Also: My 5 favorite Windows Insider features

In general, I think it’s a good idea to enable Smart App Control to defend you from potentially malicious apps and files. The only downside is that it can get in your way if you download or install a lot of files from unfamiliar sources. In that case, you can always turn it off. But try it for a while to see if you can live with it, as it can prevent unknown malware from infecting your system.

#1563. "NAZA": Israeli military issues statement on film

The award-winning documentary "NAZA" continues to spark controversy in Israel. The Israeli military has now published a multi-page statement calling the film's portrayal "distorted."

When it premiered at the Venice International Film Festival in mid-September, "NAZA" received a 25-minute-long standing ovation and the Special Jury Prize. But in Israel, the documentary has fueled heated debate.

Israeli Culture Minister Miki Zohar described the work of filmmakers Rachel Szor and Yuval Abraham "treason against the state" and urged that their citizenship be revoked. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has also sharply criticized the documentary.

Now the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) has also rejected the film's allegations about its conduct during the war in Gaza. In a multi-page document, the military accuses the filmmakers of presenting a "distorted, misleading and disingenuous picture." The film, it says, is based on "substantial factual errors" resulting from "manipulative misrepresentations of reality."

The 80-minute documentary examines the Israel Defense Forces' operations during the war in Gaza. It is based on interviews conducted with 24 anonymous military and intelligence personnel, who describe internal procedures and decision-making processes involved in selecting targets in the Gaza Strip. According to the interviewees, high civilian casualty estimates were, in some cases, deliberately factored into these decisions.

The IDF rejects those claims. It says it has never planned or carried out attacks while anticipating hundreds of civilian deaths. It also disputes the film's claim that artificial intelligence selected targets without human oversight, saying people have always made decisions about targets and attacks.

The military also accuses the filmmakers of omitting important facts. According to the IDF, the film fails to show the role of military legal advisors and commanders in reviewing and authorizing attacks or the measures it says it takes to protect civilians. It also argues that the film does not adequately address the Hamas attacks on Israel on October 7, 2023, in which over 1,200 people were killed and 250 people were taken hostage.

Several human rights organizations and a UN commission have accused Israel of committing acts of genocide in Gaza. According to health authorities in Gaza and other sources, at least 74,000 Palestinians have been killed since the war began.

Speaking at the awards ceremony in Venice in mid-September, co-director Yuval Abraham said: "We believe that the denial of a crime is what helps it persist. That is why it's really, really especially important to us that Israelis watch this film."

Rachel Szor and Yuval Abraham also worked on the film "No Other Land," which won an Oscar for Best Documentary Feature in 2025.

#1568. Split Tunneling Is Your VPN’s Best Feature. Here’s How to Make the Most of It

While you may well be familiar with virtual private network apps, you might not have come across one of the best features available inside these privacy protectors: a feature called split tunneling.

In simple terms, split tunneling lets you choose which apps or sites use the protection of a VPN and which don't. While VPNs are worth using for extra security and privacy online—foiling trackers, spoofing your location, and stopping your internet provider from seeing your browsing—they do by their nature come with some compromises in speed.

If, for example, you want to get the fastest possible speeds with your online games, you might want to leave your games outside of the VPN's remit. Likewise, since VPNs typically obfuscate your actual location, if you want to make sure you can always check movie showtimes in your local area, you can use split tunneling to keep those ticketing apps outside your VPN. Putting a VPN in place can also interfere with access to other devices on your local network, like smart home gadgets and printers.

For all these reasons, you might want the extra control that split tunneling gives you, and it's available in a lot of VPN packages. Here's what you need to know about it.

VPNs work by offering an encrypted bridge for data going to and coming from the web. This bridge means your internet provider, law enforcement agencies, and people connected to the same Wi-Fi can't see what you're doing online. Split tunneling lets you route some online traffic away from that bridge, without you having to constantly turn your VPN on and off.

App-based split tunneling is the most common type you'll come across, as it's the most straightforward and easiest to understand. Some of your apps use the VPN, and some don't. So maybe you want your banking app connected to a VPN for maximum protection, but you're less concerned about a weather app, and you don't want to see forecasts from whatever location the VPN is spoofing.

Depending on the VPN, you might also see URL-based split tunneling. This lets you set the rules based on domain names. It's the same idea as with apps: For websites that need fast internet access and where privacy is less important, there's no VPN. So you can keep the VPN turned on for a search engine page where you're typing information you want protected, but off for a local news site.

There are other types of VPN tunneling too, including route-based tunneling (based on specific IP addresses on the internet) and policy-based tunneling (essentially a set of overarching rules), but these are less common in consumer VPN software, and you're unlikely to come across them unless you're somehow managing other users.

It's not a complicated technology to understand, and it can be genuinely useful—but it's also a feature that users tend not to notice or think about when they're enabling and disabling VPNs on their devices.

The exact steps needed to enable split tunneling will vary depending on the VPN software you're using, but they shouldn't be too difficult to find. Most, but not all, VPN programs now offer some kind of split tunneling feature. If you're using a popular app, then you should find the option somewhere, especially if you're on a paid tier.

To give you some idea how it works: In ProtonVPN for Windows, on the main console there's a split tunneling link on the right, together with other features like a kill switch and port forwarding. Click Split tunneling, and once it's enabled, you can start picking your apps. You can either select a list of apps to be included by the VPN (with everything else excluded) or a list of apps to be excluded (with everything else included).

For NordVPN on Windows, you can access the feature by clicking the cog icon in the bottom left of the main interface, then choosing Split tunneling and enabling the Split tunneling toggle switch. Again, you'll be able to choose which apps are included in the VPN protections and which ones aren't.

On mobile, it's a similar process: Delve into the settings for whatever VPN app you're using, wherever those settings might be. Note, though, that iPhones don't allow as much freedom for VPNs here as Android devices and desktop platforms: You'll typically be limited to selecting websites and IP addresses to exclude, rather than apps.

Most of the time, it's going to be a set-it-and-forget-it type routine, and you're not going to need to keep diving back into the options screen to change around the apps that have VPN access—though you can if you want. It means VPN peace of mind where you need it, without the inconvenience of having a VPN applied where it's not useful.

Since retaking office, the president has made good on that promise. The Department of Justice has issued subpoenas to doctors and clinics providing gender-identity care to children, while the Trump administration has moved to implement an executive order directing the Department of Health and Human Services and the DOJ to investigate and restrict such care. More recently, the federal health bureaucracy issued a report, “Wolves in White Coats: How Doctors and Hospitals Pushed and Profited From the Fraud of ‘Gender Medicine,’” alleging potentially fraudulent insurance-coding practices in gender-affirming care for minors. If the Trump regime and its allies cannot credibly run on a record of economic success, at the very least they might direct voters’ attention to someone else who, they claim, has been picking taxpayers’ pockets.

For a document that is ostensibly about healthcare, the provenance of its authors—mainly conservative women’s groups and Christian policy shops—is striking. Its most decorated author, May Mailman, is a veteran of the first Trump administration’s immigration team who returned to the White House to orchestrate the 2025 executive order “Defending Women From Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government.” Its few medical experts are Quentin Van Meter, the former president of the fringe American College of Pediatricians, which broke away from the prestigious American Academy of Pediatrics over the latter’s support of adoption rights for gay and lesbian parents, and Eithan Haim, who previously collaborated with the conservative activist Christopher Rufo to expose Texas Children’s Hospital’s defiance of the state’s criminalization of trans healthcare as potential child abuse. The report itself presents what one could call a trans-exceptionalist view of the US healthcare system’s flaws. That is, it portrays the real deficiencies of a highly privatized approach to care as a problem of “ideological capture” and radical transgender activism rather than one of marketized medicine. It attempts to shock readers with sensationalized language about the “unique and lifelong revenue streams” that “sex-rejecting procedures” afford providers. Those relatively low-paid and prestige-hungry pediatricians, the report’s authors claim, stumbled upon a “strategic area of growth” in gender-affirming care, one that greedy “elite” hospital administrators were more than happy to encourage.

There is truth but far more fiction in this account of trans medicine. While medical transition does indeed require the persistent use of hormonal medications, these are often available as cheap generics. Yet the report explicitly alleges that pediatric gender-medicine programs generated substantial revenue and created groups of “captive patients” requiring long-term care. There is limited evidence, in the report or elsewhere, that leaders of hospitals consider gender-identity clinics, with their tiny patient populations, to be an especially lucrative “growth area.”

At its worst, “Wolves in White Coats” takes real problems in healthcare billing and attributes them to providers of trans care. The authors make a scandal of how some providers used creative diagnostic codes to convince insurers to pay for medically necessary treatment. For instance, the report alleges that a physician might diagnose a patient with an “unspecified endocrine disorder” rather than gender dysphoria, or file claims for adolescents with a diagnosis of “precocious puberty.” In the eyes of the Trump administration, however, these acts of clever compassion are nothing more than fraud, a violation of the False Claims Act that should be met with punishment.

#1579. Turtle Beach Vulcan II XP Review: Clicky Compromise

Turtle Beach is no stranger to competitive-grade gaming keyboards. In recent years, it has focused on its mid- to high-tier Vulcan range, with the Vulcan II XP as its newest entry.

It has a lot going for it—Titan HS mechanical switches, easy modification and customization options, and a suite of pro-tier gaming features available through its support software. However, while it arrives as a full-size keyboard (NumPad included!), a series of trade-offs and compromises ultimately make it feel very middle-of-the-pack.

Measuring 462 x 153 x 32 millimeters, it looks undeniably sharp thanks to its low profile and floating-key approach, all sitting atop a premium brushed-aluminum top plate. Unfortunately, the first compromise is the disappointing underside: a cheap-feeling basic plastic shell basin. Thankfully, the legs feel sturdy when popped up, adding around 10 mm in height and a comfortable angle to the board.

I also like the included soft foam wrist rest, which snaps to the front underside and adds nice support when gaming or typing. It's not memory foam, though, so I fear it might lose some of that cushion over time. The Vulcan also has integrated media controls on the top right with back, play/pause, and forward buttons, plus a media wheel with a push-to-mute feature.

It’s a $150 wired-only device (Turtle Beach is launching a wireless version, the Vulcan II XP Air for an extra $50) that connects via a detachable 1.8-meter USB-C to USB-A cord. A physical connection means zero input latency, which competitive gamers will benefit from.

The corded approach does make the Vulcan II XP a breeze to set up. Plug it into a Windows PC, and that's it. But you’ll need Turtle Beach’s Swarm II app to get the most out of it, especially given that the default RGB lighting is a blinding rainbow cascade.

Swarm II brings out the best in this keyboard. Visually, it offers 10 built-in customizable light effects—you can adjust brightness, pattern speed, and colors for some templates. They're pretty gimmicky—especially the Snake one—but it makes for a fun showcase. What's cooler is that you can adjust every LED, perfect for the 11th “custom” slot where you can choose colors, speed, and one of six patterns. You can turn the LEDs off entirely, but only through the app.

Beyond the eye candy, Turtle Beach packs in an impressive slate of features. You can remap inputs down to the individual key and store those configurations as one of five onboard profiles—perfect if you want layouts tailored to specific games, or a more work-oriented profile.

Critical to the keyboard’s gaming credentials, it includes a macro manager that lets you create custom inputs and command combos, down to how many milliseconds of input it should take before activating a keypress. It supports dozens of popular games by default, from competitive favorites like Counter-Strike and Overwatch to single-player epics like Skyrim, which have popular modding scenes and can benefit from bespoke controls. It even has presets for office apps and Photoshop, adding to the Vulcan’s overall versatility. You can easily import new layouts, too.

I’ve been testing the version with linear switches—Turtle Beach also offers a model with tactile switches, both available in black or white. If you’re new to mechanical keyboards, the main difference is that linear keys press all the way down smoothly, while tactile ones have a little “bump” of resistance as the internal switch activates, which you can feel as the keystroke registers. It’s ultimately personal preference.

For the most part, this is a joy to type on, with precise spacing between keys, and the keys themselves having a satisfying “clackiness” when fully pressed. The only small issue is that the factory-lubed linear keys are so smooth and silent until you get that clack as they hit the base, that it’s easy to accidentally long-press a key. Until you’re used to that, it can catch you off-guard—not an issue when typing, where the worst is an errant stretch of letters whennnnnnnn pausing to think mid-sentence, but it tripped me up a couple of times in games until I was used to it.

Unfortunately, unlike Turtle Beach’s earlier Vulcan II TKL Pro, the keys here aren’t Hall effect, but they are hot-swappable—another trade-off, exchanging long-term reliability and accuracy for the ability to replace or upgrade keys easily. The board supports three-pin or five-pin mechanical switch keys and makes swapping them easy. The flat keycaps also pop off without trouble, which makes cleaning simple. Turtle Beach claims the switches last 50 million keystrokes, which should be plenty, but only a third of the TKL Pro's 150 million.

In gaming, the Vulcan II XP offers a 1,000-Hz polling rate for a 1-millisecond response time, which is competition-grade but falls short of the higher-end 8,000-Hz keyboards on the market. Hopping back to Swarm II, you can activate its more advanced features, like Easy-Shift, allowing for key duplication—basically putting commonly used features within easier reach—and ReacTap, assigning two commands to the same key, useful for games that require very swift inputs. (This might be banned in some competitive scenes under automation rules, though.)

These features can be a little fiddly to set up and map, but with a little experimentation, you can build layouts and input fields that work exactly as you want or need. I tailored a bunch of controls for Borderlands 3 and was dropping Psychos like never before. A minor annoyance, though: Reverting a specific input while trying to create a custom setup would sometimes revert the entire profile, which meant I had to redo everything.

While the Vulcan II XP offers great performance for the price, I do have concerns about the overall build quality. On the unit I received, the media wheel was inverted by default—turning it counter-clockwise toward the minus symbol turned the volume up. This was even after I updated the board’s firmware through the Swarm II app, and I had to remap the inputs for it to work as intended. It’s good that the Vulcan II XP’s customization is specific and nuanced enough to remap even the dial direction, but I shouldn’t have had to.

I also found that the LEDs below the “0” and “.” keys on the NumPad don’t seem to work properly, glowing a stubborn, glaring white no matter what color or pattern is assigned. I even tried turning off every other key’s light and manually assigning colors to just those—to no avail.

It’s another reason the board feels middle-of-the-road. Like the shiny metallic top plate hiding the cheaper plastic base, the Vulcan II XP dazzles at a glance, but the closer you look, the more flaws and small annoyances are revealed.

For the price, some of those annoyances are forgivable. For anyone who hasn't upgraded to a mechanical keyboard, this is a decent entry without breaking the bank, delivering a lovely typing experience backed up by enough pro-tier gaming features to feel like a real upgrade—but more demanding or professional players will want to aim higher.

Thankfully, the studio used that extra runway to its advantage. Modern Warfare 4 may not have returned Call of Duty to the highest highs of the original trilogy that ended in 2009 (and which Infinity Ward rebooted over the last decade), but this is a leap in the right direction for a series that has become increasingly sloppy in recent years.

Modern Warfare 4 still suffers from some of Infinity Ward’s worst impulses, including open-world campaign sections, but this is still one of the better modern Call of Duty games. If this is a formula that works for you, MW4 executes it pretty darn well.

Modern Warfare 4 is a tighter game than Infinity Ward’s previous project, and it’s leagues better than Black Ops 7.

At the same time, it has a complicated campaign to crack open (and not because the plot is hard to follow). Americans spearhead the fight against imperialism; forget about the Strait of Hormuz and gun down these unambiguously bad guys. The whole plot hinges on a somehow less-subtle Red Dawn-type of scenario, and the gung-ho military politics are absolutely nauseating this time around. Sure, Price is framed as morally gray — he’s working with a cartel to achieve his goals, and there’s boring melodrama where he comes to blows with his former allies — but he’s vindicated in the end because he is the type of Call of Duty leader that prevents global conflict.

Modern Warfare 4 also features an obligatory shockingly graphic mission, which is something of a tradition for the series. In it, you play as a North Korean soldier rounding up and executing the occupants of a small village for hiding a defector. During my playthrough, I earned an achievement for successfully interrogating a villager without beating them down. The whole affair is gratuitous, gross and gamified in a problematic way, exacerbated by the bizarre decision to continue making this kind of mission a mainstay in each game.

It’s a shame that the story is so trite and icky because the tentpole action is really good. The best mission takes place early in the campaign, when Price infiltrates a New York nightclub to search for a Russian oligarch. When the player’s squad initially counterattacks North Korean airspace, they’re shot down and have to survive behind enemy lines with scavenged gear. It’s an interesting setup that is completely wasted on a Warzone-like, open-world mission with a checklist of more than 50 optional collectibles to seek out.

The few puzzles that exist are mind-numbingly easy — despite my best efforts, I checked out after completing around half of the activities. I focused on the main objective to end the mission as soon as possible, which is just about the worst feeling to have while playing a game.

I don’t know if there’s an edict from up top to put one of these open-world missions in each new game to funnel players into Warzone, but they don’t work at all and are the worst part of any campaign.

Still, the folks that play the campaign will probably have a good time here. Players can explore tons of cool locales, and the enemies are surprisingly difficult this time around. Still, if you care about the loose narrative implications of a Call of Duty campaign, it’s tough to wash the bad taste out of your mouth.

DMZ, a fairly accessible extraction shooter game mode, returns with new content for the first time since its “beta” ended in 2023. Players have been mourning what could have been for years, but it seems like the developers at Infinity Ward have been tinkering with it in the background for quite some time.

And their hard work has paid off because I’m confident that DMZ is the standout mode for Modern Warfare 4.

This extraction shooter is perhaps simpler than even ARC Raiders, but that’s part of the appeal. Players drop into North Korea to loot, fight each other and take down key targets throughout the map.

Everything is streamlined and easy to understand, including the loot. During play, you will come across several types of scrap, an assortment of healing items and tons of weapons, so you don’t have to think too hard about what to grab. Just move, shoot and pull out high-value items, then run the loop again.

The enemy AI is surprisingly aggressive and fun to battle against, especially when you run into an overwhelming number of troops. Armored vehicles and helicopters will try to smear you into the dirt. It’s an adrenaline rush, and as far as I’m concerned, Infinity Ward nailed the really important part of DMZ. It’s tough to get the player-versus-AI balance right, but these enemies feel like a real threat.

Other players might also come for your loot, which is what sometimes puts folks off of the extraction genre altogether. But DMZ’s in-depth bounty system provides a foil to player-versus-player gameplay.

Unveiled at CES 2026, the TDM Neo is a pair of on-ear headphones that transform into a Bluetooth speaker. It caught the imagination of the attending media, won a handful of awards, and inspired countless column inches.

Given the current state of the portable audio industry, it's easy to understand why. Most designs seem to revolve around incremental updates and new colorways rather than genuine technical innovation. Neo is an exciting alternative, and it’s little wonder the startup brand went on to smash its Kickstarter targets.

I’ve spent the past couple of weeks listening to the headphones and Bluetooth speaker, and have lost count of the number of times I’ve showed off the clever twist-and-fold mechanism to friends. But has TDM managed to produce a genuine tech twofer, or designed a novelty product that gives you the worst of both worlds?

The twist-and-fold mechanism on the Neo is clever and addictive. Rotate the ear cups around the cushioned but flexible headband, bring them together, and strong magnets snap everything firmly in place. Once connected, the headphone drivers disable, and the outward-facing Bluetooth speakers kick in. Twist them apart and you've got your headphones back.

It’s innovative, and the engineering is impressive. Once in speaker mode, the two rubber TDM logos sit closely together and form a protective base, while the asymmetrical twisted headband cocoons the ear cups together. It works well—it never once popped open accidentally. The action is smooth, the connection reassuringly solid, and switching between modes works exactly as intended.

The Neo has four 40-mm drivers—two for the headphones and two for the speakers. The outward-facing speakers are each rated at 5 watts (for a total of 10). Ten watts is not a lot for a modern Bluetooth speaker, but wattage doesn't automatically tell you how loud or good a speaker will sound, especially in the current age of digital signal processing. The highly regarded $55 JBL Go 5 has just 4.5 watts of output, for instance.

Battery life is quoted at more than 200 hours for headphones and 16 hours in speaker mode, which is pretty impressive, and a quick five-minute charge gives you an extra eight hours.

On-device control is basic but effective, with rubber power and volume buttons together, and a separate one for Bluetooth. One quirk of the twist design is that in speaker mode the volume button is inverted, and on first use I instinctively turned the volume down instead of up. The power and 3.5-mm AUX input are also left facing up, rather than being hidden from view. This also means the USB-C cable sticks out the top—not the most aesthetic solution.

The TDM companion app allows you to customize what happens when the headphones are twisted. You can switch between headphone and speaker playback, pause the music, or power off. You can also lock them in speaker mode to listen with the Neo around your neck, but please, if you have any love for your fellow commuters, don’t do this on the train. There’s also basic EQ settings for both sets of speakers and Find My–style device tracking too.

The TDM are on-ear headphones with no active noise canceling (ANC). That’s something of a rarity these days, with brands eschewing this style in favor of larger, plusher over-ear designs with powerful noise cancellation. See Sony, Bose, Apple, Nothing—you get the idea …

While the TDM don’t have ANC, they do have relatively firm earpads and a tight fit that blocks out noise the old-fashioned way: with noise isolation. Unfortunately, maybe for this very reason, the earpads are not as plush as I’d like, and the whole thing feels tight on my admittedly large skull and less than dainty ears. It’s not eerily quiet like using the best noise-canceling headphones, and I can’t imagine that’s a compromise many users are willing to give up easily.

At 11.9-ounces, the Neo are also heavy for on-ear headphones. It’s no surprise, given that they’ve got four 40-mm drivers inside, but it is noticeable. For comparison, they’re 3 ounces (or 34 percent) heavier than the Sony WH-1000XM6, 1.9 ounces heavier than the more affordable Soundcore Space One Pro, and a full 4.25 ounces heavier than the on-ear Beats Solo 4.

The Neo sound detailed and pleasant, with clear bass and appreciably distinct vocals, but the presentation feels quite boxed in. Music lacks the scale and openness I expect from great headphones, and individual instruments are crowded for space. If they were comfier to wear, I think I’d enjoy the audio a bit more, but I found it all quite tiring, and certainly not on a par with similarly priced alternatives.

They are not awful, but they do feel compromised, and given the price, they also present a bit of a quandary for travel. You gain a Bluetooth speaker at your destination but may spend the journey wishing you'd packed more comfortable headphones with proper ANC.

Just to reiterate, I really like the engineering and execution of the Neo, and I love how small the headphones can fold away. The Bluetooth speaker looks cool too, and it gives the headphones a second function. Sadly, the TDM can’t compete with the best Bluetooth speakers I’ve tested.

There's enough volume for casual listening, but the sound is thin and underwhelming. It keeps pace with the tiny-but-mighty JBL Go 5, but given that it has twice the power and double the drivers, it really should sound better. I also played it alongside my favorite current portable speaker, the $120 JBL Flip 7, and the difference between the two was like racing a speedboat and a paddleboard.

Having a Bluetooth speaker on hand whenever you have your headphones is convenient, and the ability to share a song instantly might appeal, but compact Bluetooth speakers have become so good that the TDM’s subpar audio quality is hard to ignore.

The obvious benefit to the Neo is having just one device to carry and charge. But is that enough to justify the compromises?

The $200 Soundcore Space One Pro are impressive, midrange ANC over-ear headphones that came out on top in WIRED's blind headphone listening test. They're comfortable, adjustable, and foldable, with excellent sound. They’re frequently on sale for $130 or $140, too. Pair them with the $150 but-often-on-sale-for-less JBL Flip 7, and you can get top-tier headphones and a top-tier portable speaker for nearly the same price as the TDM Neo.

Admittedly, there's more to carry, and you lose the instant gratification of twisting your headphones into a speaker. But the Soundcore sound better, are significantly more comfortable, and fold impressively small, while the 1.23-pound JBL Flip 7 is hardly going to weigh you down.

I admire what TDM has achieved. The Neo are clever, entertaining, and a refreshing reminder that innovation doesn't—and shouldn’t—rely solely on stuffing in AI features, adding a fresh colorway, or creating another smartphone app. The mechanism works beautifully, and taking one audio product on the go instead of two has genuine appeal. I just wish I enjoyed listening to them as much as I enjoyed twisting them.

#1599. A Dating App Is Offering ‘Chivalry Mode’ and ‘Marriage Timeline’ to Get Gen Z Married

When Adreanna Pulliam signed up for the Christian dating app Upward over the summer, she was prepared to come across men who identified as hyperconservative.

So when she swiped on a guy’s profile that listed “a progressive donkey or a ‘Trump is King’ elephant” as his relationship deal-breaker, she was shocked. “I hadn’t expected to see a moderate libertarian” on the app, she says.

It only got more bizarre from there. There was the guy advertising his Snapchat with the username “Yaboi Blaze reallysexgod.” Another dater, for reasons she still can’t fathom, included a photo of him at a funeral, standing in front of a poster-size image of the deceased.

While Pulliam was apprehensive about the prospects, she wanted to see how different Upward, one of the lesser-known apps in Match Group’s sprawling portfolio of dating platforms, was from Hinge and Tinder. She hadn’t had much luck with either of them. Many of the profiles she encountered on Upward, she tells WIRED, were “MAGA types of men” who “fit more of that red-pill, trad wife, conservative values” category of dater.

A 25-year-old opera singer in Bloomington, Indiana, for whom marriage has always been “the end goal,” Pulliam is the kind of person Upward is trying to appeal to since relaunching last month.

The relaunch was redesigned around two key data points from Match Group’s 2026 Singles in America study: 72 percent of Gen Zs want to get married, and a quarter of singles aged 18 to 34 “identify with a combination of traditional values around family, faith, and community,” a news release about the survey noted.

The pivot away from an app exclusively for Christian daters was about opening up its doors for people “who share those same goals, but may not lead with a faith-first mindset,” says Lauren DeFord, director of brand marketing at Match, of the app’s new focus on Gen Z singles.

Upward’s expansion is representative of the ongoing change in the dating market. Dwindling engagement and the introduction of AI have pushed companies to reassess user growth. For some, that means abandoning their original mission.

Upward, which runs on a tiered subscription, works similarly to Tinder. You can swipe left or right on profiles, and users are encouraged to include a Faith Statement on their page, explaining what they value most about their relationship to God. Among its new features are Chivalry Mode, which, if you’re familiar with how Bumble used to operate, persuades men to message first, and Marriage Timeline, which lets daters share when they hope to get married with potential suitors.

But while a marriage-focused app like Upward may be tapping into a genuine desire for commitment, that doesn’t mean young people are returning to traditional relationship norms en masse, says Daniel Cox, author of Generation Uncoupled: America’s New Gender Divide and the Crisis of Human Connection. In fact, the opposite is happening.

In his book, Cox argues that Gen Z is entering adulthood with a less clear sense of their “relational priorities,” and a decline in social and civic life has given rise to a “hyperindividualistic culture” that doesn’t always prioritize things like marriage. As a result, fewer Gen Zs are coupling up today. An American Institute for Boys and Men survey found that 44 percent of Gen Z men have had no relationship experience at all during their teen years, double the rate for older men. “This movement makes it a lot harder to ... want to sacrifice and to be there for someone,” he says.

Upward’s relaunch framed the app as the “home of modern courtship,” including video testimonials of singles who’d found love on the app. But aesthetically, there wasn’t anything “modern” about its promotional rollout. In one video, newly married Zach donned a cowboy hat and blue jeans, doing all the things men should presumably do for their significant others: gift them flowers, carry her through a meadow, open the car door. A bold text overlay on the video read “chivalrymaxxing.” According to the company, branding was inspired by an “Americana” theme, and its “Wanted: For Life” campaign draws on Western-style imagery.

Cox, who is the director of the Survey Center on American Life, is convinced that the trad trend is “a reaction to the lack of cultural norms around courtship. People are wanting something over nothing,” he says, adding that daters are desperate for “a script” with direction and defined roles, given how nontraditional approaches to partnership, such as polyamory and relationship anarchy, are increasing among young people. “And something that’s nice about the trad script is it’s very clear. Though I don’t think it’s close to ideal for a lot of young people.”

A 2023 Pew Research survey found that only 23 percent of adults considered being married “extremely” or “very important” to living a fulfilling life, compared with 61 percent who said the same about having close friends. In the last 30 years, the percentage of high school seniors who said they were likely to marry someday had fallen from 80 percent in 1993 to 67 percent in 2023, according to Pew. The decline was driven mostly by changing views among young women who, despite the portion of married adults slightly increasing from 50 percent to 51 percent between 2019 to 2023, remain more likely than men to be unpartnered.

The trend is likely to continue in that direction. “There’s increasingly a belief among young women that marriage makes them less happy,” Cox says. “So it’s not only that they have less of a desire to get married. There’s a concern that if they get married, they won’t be as happy as if they are single. That seems like a view that is potentially not going to change.”

Upward, which declined to share information regarding the number of active users on the platform, is betting that it will.

When setting up her account, Pulliam was baffled to find out that Upward let users list their body type, ranging from “fit” and “curvy” to “chubby,” a feature typically reserved for hookup apps. She was also confused that Upward allows married people on the app. Some of the stranger options for relationship type included “it’s complicated” and “I’m not into labels.” “I’m not knocking open relationships or anything like that, but it was interesting to see such a progressive take from a more of a conservative demographic,” she says.

Tauti, a social work student who lives in California, lasted less than 48 hours on the app, disturbed by the overwhelming conservative majority looking for domesticated partners. Sometimes the hook with mission-specific apps like Upward is that the religious identity associated with the platform can create an assumption of compatibility before users have really gotten to know one another. “I did not know me being Christian and leaning liberal was a big ask,” she said on TikTok. “It’s turned me off from Christian dating altogether.”

Some users believe the recent pivot is diluting the experience for daters who joined seeking out people with purely Christian beliefs. “Why are you on there if you’re agnostic?” Heidi Marais, a teacher and single mom, asked on TikTok in September. “And Upward, why is it an option?”

DeFord, who maintains that Christian singles remain at the core of Upward’s mission despite describing the relaunch as a “brand shift,” pushes back against the assertion that the experience is worsening. “Those users can still interact and match with people who share the same denomination, the same religious views, the same cadence of how they like to practice.”

Since its debut in 2020, Upward has struggled with various hiccups that include everything from functionality issues to user perception. Many reviews in the Apple App Store complain of terrible user support, poor filtering features, and automatic bans, with one user calling the app “janky as hell.”

Cox notes there is ample reason to be wary of Match’s claim that a majority of Gen Z want to get married. Given that Gen Z is younger, it is likely that more people have never tied the knot. “So if they’re comparing Gen Z to millennials or Gen Xers in the survey, it could be that they have already been in relationships, maybe even divorced, so they are not as eager to get married again.” Match’s survey, which the company says is “demographically representative” of the US Census, sampled 5,000 US singles over the age of 18 who were not married, living with a partner, or in a committed relationship. In any case, Cox says, the “norm around what is the ideal age to get married” has vanished.

Pulliam still wants to get hitched but doesn’t consider it a priority right now. She attended a Christian college and recognizes the potential for Upward to be a hit “within communities of Christian private universities,” she says. “But I think the only way that the app works is for maybe someone who has a little bit more of a closed mind.”

#1600. Turkey: Stock market scandal hits half a million investors

Asset managers are alleged to have artificially inflated the value of their funds. Turkish authorities have made multiple arrests, and a leading AKP lawmaker has resigned.

Investors in Turkey want one thing above all else: for their money to retain its value. Given inflation of around 30%, that is not exactly easy. As a result, many people are buying gold, foreign currency or stocks and increasingly, mutual fund shares that promise high returns.

But the stock market has become a trap for around half a million Turkish investors. In mid-September, the benchmark BIST 100 index plummeted by 6% in a single day and lost about 12% within just a few trading days.

This sharp drop in stock prices was triggered by a major market manipulation scandal. Approximately 455,000 investors have been affected, more than 45 people have been arrested and a high-ranking politician from the ruling Justice and Development Party (AKP) has resigned.

Everything started when some investment fund providers had trouble making timely repayments to investors. Among those affected were funds managed by the companies Pusula Portfoy and Tera. Two Tera funds alone held assets totaling around €6.5 billion ($6.37 billion).

In response, the Capital Markets Board (SPK) intervened and shut down the funds of seven asset managers, including Tera, Pusula and Atlas. According to the state-run news agency Anadolu, a total of 131 funds are set to be liquidated.

The Istanbul Public Prosecutor's Office is now investigating charges of fraud, violations of the capital markets law and the formation of a criminal organization, Justice Minister Akin Gurlek announced.

The suspicion: The fund companies are alleged to have artificially inflated the value of their funds, while in reality their funds contained worthless "junk stocks."

Among the 45 people arrested so far are executives from investment firms. In addition, the assets of 42 individuals and 46 legal entities have been frozen, according to Gurlek on X.

The Istanbul Stock Exchange has also taken action: it excluded 27 stocks from the BIST 100 benchmark index as of October 1.

The funds are being liquidated: Their assets will be sold and the proceeds distributed to investors in proportion to their fund shares. The deadline for this has been extended from three to six months. According to Aysel Gundogdu, an independent financial advisor in Istanbul, this is intended to reduce selling pressure so that stocks are not sold at fire-sale prices. However, this does not mean that the liquidation of each fund will take six months. Depending on market conditions, it may be completed more quickly.

Only, there is no guarantee. An investment fund is not a savings account and any loss in value affects the investor. Gundogdu expects that money market funds will largely be redeemed. With hedge funds and equity funds holding "junk stocks" that are virtually untradeable, however, the situation could prove more difficult.

It is important to distinguish between market losses and damages resulting from potential irregularities.

Economist and columnist Guldem Atabay emphasized that a distinction must be made between price losses and manipulation: Investors bear the market risk, but they do not have to accept false information, artificially inflated prices, or a lack of oversight.

If funds are closed or liquidated, the shares and other securities in the portfolio can be sold. The question is how much they are worth and how easily they can be sold.

Financial expert Gundogdu believes that the fund's assets will not cover investors' claims. Furthermore, she says there have been structural failures. "The crisis is far greater than a simple liquidity crisis. There are flaws in management and oversight."

She therefore considers it possible that the assets of the fund founders and managers could also be tapped to make up for the shortfall.

Economist Atabay calls for those responsible to be identified and for the damages to be recovered from them. Following the liquidation of the funds, it must be clarified who is responsible for any legal violations. "Liquidation is no substitute for accountability," Atabay said.

The crisis has already led to the resignation of a high-ranking female politician in the ruling AKP party. Fatma Betul Sayan Kaya, deputy party chair and former minister of family affairs, asked Turkish President and AKP leader Recep Tayyip Erdogan to "relieve her of all her posts." This has been a phrase frequently used within the AKP for years when someone is forced to resign.

She was facing allegations regarding stock transactions by her and her husband, Ilyas Kaya, in connection with the fund crisis. Political responsibility, she wrote on social media, goes beyond legal responsibility. She said her position should not hinder the investigation.

The opposition now intends to bring the issue before Parliament. Selcuk Ozdag, deputy leader of the Yeni Yol parliamentary group, announced that as soon as Parliament reconvenes, he will submit a motion for a parliamentary investigation. The investigation is intended to determine whether politicians are involved in the manipulation scandal.
