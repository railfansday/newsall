#701. The AI attack-ad nightmare is coming true

AI is producing reality-bending midterm ads about defaced Bibles, upscale dinners and a board game with a "Death to America" playing card.

Why it matters: The warnings about deepfakes are coming true. Election laws haven't kept pace with a world where campaigns can put words in opponents' mouths and create realistic-looking scenes that didn't happen.

  • The Wesleyan Media Project found $137 million had been spent on 220 AI-generated ads through Oct. 11, mostly from Republicans.
  • Four states have laws prohibiting certain uses of AI-generated deepfakes in political ads, while 27 more require some form of disclosure about their use.
  • No federal law specifically regulates AI-generated political ads.

The big picture: An Axios analysis found numerous political ads partly or fully made by generative AI, sometimes undisclosed.

Georgia Senate: Senate Leadership Fund, a super PAC aligned with Senate Republicans, has run a series of AI-generated deepfake ads depicting Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff getting beauty treatments.

  • The ads mock Ossoff's reported campaign spending on hair and makeup, saying he "talks pretty" but has "an ugly record" and "a lot to cover up."
  • Ossoff said in a C-SPAN interview: "It is deeply troubling to see ... deepfake videos used to fabricate political advertising. And it's a slippery slope toward the destruction of truth in political debate, and the ad should come down immediately."
  • The ads don't disclose AI use, which Georgia law doesn't require.

Michigan Senate: A super PAC called Front Line Action used AI in an ad depicting Democratic candidate Abdul El-Sayed in a fabricated family photo with podcaster Hasan Piker and four women wearing niqabs.

  • Another AI-generated scene has El-Sayed playing a Risk-style board game with his half-sister, Eman Abdelhadi, and Piker. It depicts cards bearing statements that include "Death to America," "Do not Celebrate Thanksgiving" and "Celebrate 9/11."
  • The group has defended the ad, telling CNN it is "grounded in El-Sayed's own public statements and documented reporting and records concerning him and individuals associated with or supportive of his campaign."
  • The ad features both a written and spoken disclosure that it was "created in whole or in part with the use of artificial intelligence."

New York governor: Republican Bruce Blakeman's campaign and allied super PACs have repeatedly used generative AI in ads attacking Democratic Gov. Kathy Hochul, with varying degrees of disclosure.

  • One AI-generated ad released by the Blakeman campaign depicts the Tooth Fairy struggling to come up with cash to give to children, saying she "just can't afford to be the Tooth Fairy in Kathy Hochul's New York."
  • Another Blakesman spot says onscreen: "We asked AI what a truthful Kathy Hochul ad would look like." The ad features AI-generated scenes of Hochul attending an upscale dinner and standing outside a police precinct while a man asks her: "Want some crack?"

Pennsylvania governor: A new ad from Republican candidate Stacy Garrity features an AI-generated version of Democratic Gov. Josh Shapiro talking about taxes, abortion and data centers.

  • The spot puts this quote in his mouth: "This ad was created with AI, thanks to big, beautiful data centers. Fact is, I was the one who fast-tracked data centers right here in PA, right after they handed me $5 million in political contributions."
  • Shapiro spokesperson Manuel Bonder, in a statement to Axios, dismissed the ad as "cheap, dishonest AI bullshit."

Texas Senate: Democratic candidate James Talarico has been a consistent target of AI-generated ads from both Republican Ken Paxton's campaign and outside super PACs.

  • A Paxton campaign ad uses AI to depict Talarico defacing a Bible with crayon and saying, "In the beginning God was non-binary, and he wasn't a Christian. And God said, 'let there be six genders,' and there were."
  • Talarico told NBC he "tried in the legislature to ban AI deepfake videos in political campaigns because they are misleading. They are lying about a candidate in this race."

Kentucky's 6th District: No Going Back PAC, backed by President Trump's MAGA Inc., has run several ads using AI to boost Republican congressional candidate Ralph Alvarado.

  • One ad features multiple AI-generated deepfakes of Democrat Zach Dembo, including one scene in which he stares into the camera.
  • "I expected them to lie about my record, but I did not expect them to lie about my appearance," Dembo said in a statement to Axios, adding that "using AI to create fake or misleading images is the beginning of a very dangerous path for our democracy."

Serbia has entered a new political era after Aleksandar Vucic conceded defeat to a historic student-led protest movement in an election on Sunday, bringing an end to his long grip on power.

The streets of Belgrade erupted in celebration following Vucic's announcement, as drivers sounded their car horns and students chanted "victory" and "he's done".

The snap elections came following nearly two years of student-driven demonstrations against perceived corruption and creeping authoritarianism, which were triggered when a train station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad in 2024, killing 16 people.

"Dear Serbia, tonight the voice of the people is being heard," said Radomir Sajcic, a professor at Belgrade University speaking on behalf of the students at their headquarters. "Tonight we celebrate victory, tomorrow we build the future."

Voter turnout reached nearly 70 per cent, representing the highest participation since the breakup of Yugoslavia in 1992. Long queues snaked from polling stations throughout Serbian towns and cities on Sunday, reflecting a widespread desire for change.

"It is clear that we have lost these elections, the Student List has won, they will have between 135 and 140 seats and we will have 105, no one else has entered parliament," Vucic said, basing his projection off preliminary results.

A representative sample from pollsters CESID-IPSPS showed the student candidate list winning 138 deputies, with Vucic's SNS party securing 104 seats in the 250-seat parliament. The remaining seats will be shared among ethnic national minority parties.

"Unbelievable happiness and joy and we have finally got rid of this mafia. I am so happy I cannot speak," said a woman named Tanja who was celebrating on the streets of Belgrade.

Five candidates are vying for Seat 2 on the board of trustees for the Los Angeles Community College District, where they will oversee some 200,000 students and an annual budget around $10 billion.

Spanning nine colleges, the district is the largest in the state and one of the biggest in the nation.

Board members will face a number of critical issues if elected. Many students struggle to afford basic needs like housing and food, making it difficult to complete their education.

Enrollment, while up in recent years, still is below prepandemic levels, which affects revenue because state funding is based on the number of students served.

The district also is in the midst of spending $5.3 billion in bond money voters approved in 2022 to renovate campus buildings, upgrade water and power infrastructure and build student housing.

Members of the board are elected to at-large seats and do not represent a specific area. In addition to Seat 2, 11 other candidates are running for two other seats in November.

The Times sent a questionnaire about district issues to all five Seat 2 candidates — four answered the Times’ questions and one candidate said he has suspended his campaign. Replies have been edited for brevity and clarity.

#704. Behind the Settler Attack on Palestinians and a Washington Post Journalist

On Friday, a group of Israeli settlers attacked Palestinian farmers who were harvesting olives in the West Bank. In the process, they also beat up an American Washington Post photographer, Heidi Levine, who was there to cover the annual olive harvest. The attack prompted rare criticism of the Israeli government from the United States Embassy in Jerusalem, which requested an investigation. The office of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said that an investigation was warranted, while also casting doubt on what occurred. (On Saturday, after settlers also attacked a group of European diplomats during a visit to the West Bank, Netanyahu raised the prospect that it was part of a “coordinated campaign to smear Israel.”) Over all, in 2026, violence against Palestinians in the West Bank has increased significantly from prior years; the next Israeli election is in late October.

I recently spoke by phone with Jasper Nathaniel, a journalist who covers the West Bank for his Substack, and who was present during the assault on Levine. During our conversation, which has been edited for length and clarity, we discussed how the Israeli government has been increasingly aiding extremist settlers, the changing attitude among Israeli settlers toward Americans in the West Bank, and what happened during the attack on Levine and others.

How many times have you been to the West Bank in the past couple of years?

This is my fifth trip since January of 2024. Each trip is around a month, sometimes a little more, sometimes a little bit less. For this trip, I arrived on October 6th, and I am definitely staying past the election, which is on October 27th.

How have things changed over those nearly three years?

There are real material changes, because there are lots of villages that I visited as recently as last year that just don’t exist anymore. They have been literally wiped off the map and replaced by either an outpost, or in some cases a new neighborhood of a settlement, or a closed military zone. [Outposts are officially unauthorized by the Israeli government, although many of them eventually become retroactively legalized.] So there have been actual material changes to the geography and the demographics.

Since this government came in, in 2022, and certainly since October 7, 2023, the settlers have been emboldened. And the threshold for when they turn to violence has just gotten lower and lower. In other words, it used to be the case that many settler attacks would start with settlers coming into a village and accusing Palestinians of something like stealing a goat, and then they would go house to house, kicking down doors looking for the goat, and then eventually it would turn into a fight.

Now what you’re seeing is, literally every single day, huge mobs of settlers put on masks, wear all black, pick up clubs and assault rifles, and launch coördinated attacks. And these attacks are not random at all. The way that the map has changed in the West Bank is precisely what Bezalel Smotrich, the finance minister, has laid out numerous times when he’s described how Israel is going to create Jewish sovereignty across the West Bank. So, for example, take the attack on Friday in Khirbet Abu Falah. There is an outpost that exists there, and it’s not there for no reason. It’s a strategic piece of land because it cuts off a point between two villages. It creates continuity between different settlements, and the Israeli government wants that piece of land, and so the settlers are out there doing their dirty work by terrorizing the Palestinians. They have effectively taken over all of the farmland there. You may see some Israeli officials condemning Friday’s attack because it was so gnarly and involved an American, but, at the end of the day, the most violent settlers are working toward a state project of taking over land in the West Bank. And so, yes, the violence has increased both in intensity and in scale.

The settlers seem to be acting with more confidence, brazenness, and violence. How would you define their mentality?

I think the biggest thing is that they appear to be much more organized. I’ll give you an example. In Masafer Yatta, which is in the Hebron Hills, there’s been settler violence there for a very long time, but only in the past couple of years has what is essentially a gang formed. It’s run by a guy named Shimon Atiya, and he runs a farm, and there are lots of young men and teen-agers who work for him, and some of them actually live with him. And these guys go from village to village, vandalizing property, letting their livestock feed on Palestinian land, and beating up residents in some cases.

They appear like a gang because what happens is, first, there will be a couple of maybe twelve- or thirteen-year-old kids who come and try to plant an Israeli flag somewhere. And then when people try to stop them, a couple of older kids will come and start pushing and shoving and punching, and then maybe Atiya or one of his right-hand men show up with a gun and start waving the gun around and actually scaring the crap out of people to make them leave. So I am seeing a level of organization with what are essentially settler militias that I did not witness three years ago. And part of that is because the settlers are so well supported and resourced by the government and by the military. I mean, they have the ability to basically create these little org structures.

A guy like Atiya or Elisha Yered or Amishav Melet has a direct connection to the I.D.F., or a commander in the I.D.F. that they work directly with. And that I.D.F. commander is responsible for giving them an official I.D.F. guard, who basically serves as their personal security and their enforcer in some cases. [In a statement, the I.D.F. said, “The claim that the IDF provides personal security guards to the leaders of the outposts is entirely unfounded.”] So settlers have this sort of direct material connection to the military that, in years past, may not have existed at all. At other times it was probably more tacit, but now it’s direct. There’s a guy in the I.D.F. central command whose job is to meet with outpost leaders and make sure they have everything that they need, and that includes money, too.

And so then that money is used to pay shepherds to work for them. And part of what the shepherds do is they bring their livestock onto Palestinian land to destroy their vegetation, so they kill off the farmland. So they’re operating in this very strategic way that, from a distance, appears to be random. And I think at one time in the past it was more random, but now you can just see that it’s a well-oiled machine.

So the approach by authorities has changed from letting violence happen to actively working with the perpetrators?

Absolutely. I think that’s the biggest change. Smotrich, in addition to being the finance minister, also heads up something called the Settlement Administration, which he created within the Ministry of Defense so that he could basically take charge of the military occupation of the West Bank and clear all the red tape involved in authorizing new settlements, or new roads, or things like that. So, for example, it is now much, much, much easier for a settler leader who wants to create a new outpost to get whatever resources he needs for that, because all that red tape that used to be there doesn’t exist anymore. Now it’s a bunch of messianic settler ideologues like Smotrich in the government, who are just rubber-stamping most of what they want.

And that also trickles down to the fact that these settler groups are unbelievably well armed. Settlers have been basically deputized into these groups called Hagmar, where they just become settler-soldiers, who are basically wearing full I.D.F. attire and have rifles. Smotrich has what is called a sovereignty plan, which he unveiled last year, to basically squeeze Palestinians into six major population centers, which include Ramallah and the other big cities. And the way that the settler violence has unfolded recently, the way that the outposts are popping up and the villages are being emptied—it’s in direct alignment with his plan.

I have heard people say that the attack on Heidi Levine only got so much attention because she was American, when in fact this type of thing happens constantly to Palestinians, including during the attack on Friday, with much less attention. This is surely true, but I also think it’s important to understand how the settlers and the Israeli government view the risks of attacking Americans, especially American journalists. How has this question changed since you started making these trips, which was during the Biden Administration?

A Palestinian American who I talked to about this told me that, under Biden, if an American was beat up or they had their home burned or something, or an American was brutalized by the I.D.F., somebody from the Embassy would come and send their condolences, and then they would leave, and the victim would never hear from them again. With the Trump Administration, they have not even been making the trip. In other words, there’s no material difference between the two. It’s just that one of them was putting a dressing on it to act like there was some care.

I think that Palestinian Americans are a good measure for this because they look like other Palestinians, but they have blue passports. And so it’s sort of like you’re controlling for a variable here. There are Palestinian communities in the West Bank which are, like, eighty per cent Palestinian American. Because of their dual citizenship, they used to be pretty insulated from the violence of the occupation, from the settlers. But that privilege disappeared on October 7th. And now those Palestinian Americans are coming to terms with the fact that they’re actually just Palestinians. There’s no distinction.

As far as Americans who are not also Palestinian, like myself, or Heidi—I mean, look, it’s anecdotal, but I will tell you that, in my five trips, the level of violence that I have directly faced myself has quite literally increased. My first three times, nobody ever actually laid hands on me. I was in cars that were attacked. I was chased. I had guns pointed at me, but nobody ever struck me. The last time I was here, in June and July, I was attacked multiple times. This trip, I’ve been here for ten days, and to tell you the truth, I’ve lost count of how many times I’ve been attacked already. And, listen, I don’t look Palestinian. I’m usually shouting in English. A lot of the time I’m wearing a press vest or I’m shouting that I am a journalist, and they just don’t give a shit. They don’t even blink. It doesn’t faze them. They still come after me just like they would anyone else.

It was the olive-harvest day in Khirbet Abu Falah, a Palestinian community in the West Bank. And, like I said, there’s this outpost that exists on the top of a hill that overlooks the olive fields, and the Palestinians are routinely attacked by settlers that live there. So, because of these attacks, the olive fields have basically been off limits for the Palestinians, but this is their livelihood, and this is the harvesting season. And so, basically, what a lot of these communities do is pick a day, or a couple of days, when everybody will go out together, and the hope is that there will be protection in numbers.

But, from the minute we got there, we were watching the settlers at the top of the hill prepare for an attack. I mean, we were literally watching them suit up, put their masks over their heads, pick up their clubs, and it looked like a group of people getting ready for battle, which is exactly what they were doing. The olive harvest is supposed to be this peaceful and celebratory family affair, but in this case, because of the risk of attack, it was only men who went to harvest. There were no women or children there, and they were just racing as fast as they could to get the olives off the tree, because we were watching the settlers get ready to attack. About an hour into it, they start charging down the hill, and initially they are just hurling really big rocks, and they have these slings that they use. So these are really big rocks that are coming in from a distance, really fast, and, if you get hit by one, it can do real damage. So first they’re just charging down with rocks. There’s probably forty to fifty of them. And you have to keep in mind that the olive fields—and this is the case in a lot of Palestinian villages—are basically the buffer between the outpost and the village itself, the residential core of the village. And so, if the settlers are able to push right past the olive fields, they’re now in the village, and they’re going to start ransacking homes and basically doing a pogrom.

So, in other words, the Palestinians had an interest in defending themselves, not just to defend themselves so they could continue picking olives but because it’s existential. It was sort of a remarkable thing to witness, but dozens of young Palestinians just put masks on and picked up rocks and charged back at the settlers, and they beat them back. And so there was about ten to fifteen minutes where it was like a battle. The settlers would make a push and hurl rocks, and then the Palestinians would charge back. Then things changed when the settlers started setting trees on fire, and so now there’s smoke in the air, and the Palestinians are having a harder time pushing forward because of that. And then the military showed up and started tear-gassing the Palestinians. So, once there’s tear gas—you can’t run through tear gas. So everybody’s basically now in retreat, and then out of the tear gas emerges a white pickup truck with, I think, two or three settlers, in black hoodies, holding rifles, shooting them into the air. And, as soon as we heard machine-gun fire, everybody just turned around and ran.

And you’re running along with everyone else?

Oh, yeah. I was right at the front line and, as soon as I heard the machine-gun fire, that’s it.

When you say you’re at the front line, you’re observing as a journalist here?

Yeah. I’m just there taking cover and filming what’s happening. And so, as everybody’s running backwards, that must be when Heidi was caught. I’m not sure if she fell or if she was just overtaken. I mean, she’s sixty-three years old, and these settlers are maybe a third or fourth her age. But, basically, after we retreated and the charge stopped, I look back, and I see somebody in a press vest on the ground with five or six settlers on top of them, just beating them over and over with clubs. It was truly barbaric. At one point, we saw them lift her up by her collar, and I was, like, “Oh, are they helping her up?” And then somebody came and kicked her and knocked her back down, and then they continued beating her. They stole everything off of her: her phone, her camera, her multiple cameras, her wallet, her passport.

At this point, I actually didn’t know who was being beaten, but I was worried this person was going to be killed, and we were trying to figure out how to get to her because there was still this mob of settlers attacking us, plus there was tear gas in the air. A group of young Palestinian men appeared and led the way, started throwing rocks, and pushed the settlers back. And so the reason that the settlers stopped attacking Heidi is that they were forced to retreat. And that’s how I was then able to reach her. I saw her and I was shocked. I was, like, “Why is my Jewish mother here on the front lines of this battle?” I helped her up. She was in really, really bad shape. At first, I didn’t think we were going to have to run, because the settlers were retreating, but then they charged us again, and so then we had to run. I mean, in the video, you hear her say, “Let me die, let me die.” Fortunately, we were able to get her to the back, and some Palestinian paramedics picked her up. Again, the whole time, the only reason we were not completely overtaken is because these young Palestinian men were protecting us with rocks.

It ended after that happened, which was the last of the major settler violence that day. But then the I.D.F. soldiers who have been there, who’ve been tear-gassing the Palestinians and providing cover for the settlers, stormed the village. Rather than go after the settlers who have just nearly killed a woman, they stormed the village and started firing their weapons, and they shot a Palestinian man in the leg. The bullet shattered his femur and left countless bullet fragments that surgeons were unable to remove. He had to have a major surgery. I heard the gunshots, ran over, and was trying to document it, and soldiers were pushing me around, trying to throw me on the ground. One soldier aimed his gun at the paramedics. The New York Times reported that they were demanding the wounded man show his I.D. or he wouldn’t be able to go to the hospital. And then at some point, he was put in an ambulance, which took him away, and then the soldiers hung around for a bit. They shot a little more tear gas, and then they left. And then, actually, what was cool was that the Palestinians then went back out to their olive harvest. I mean, frankly, the feeling on the ground was that, despite two really grave injuries and other injuries, it was sort of a win for the Palestinians, because they did beat the settlers back and return to their olive fields.

The reason I keep bringing up the Palestinians throwing rocks to protect Heidi is because, I mean, I can’t emphasize enough how much these young men are . . . #724. Louise Glück’s Life in Restaurants

If Maria Rondeau and JuanMa Calderón were literalists, they might have put nasturtiums on the menu at Rosa y Marigold, the Peruvian restaurant they opened in April, in Boston. They took the name from the novella “Marigold and Rose,” the final book published by their friend Louise Glück, the Nobel Prize-winning poet, before she died, in 2023. The story is told from the perspective of infant twins with divergent personalities—Rose is unguardedly buoyant, whereas Marigold is watchful and introspective. “The garden relied on nasturtiums, which Mother and Father ate,” the narrator observes. “It was strange to see the grilled fish with nasturtium butter melting over it. . . . But once you try it, Mother said, the taste is magic. The twins knew what magic was. It made the sun rise. Now they knew it had a taste.”

Rondeau, who grew up in Guatemala, is an architect; Calderón, her partner in life and in restaurants, is a filmmaker and chef from Peru. In September, I sat at Rosa y Marigold’s sleek U-shaped bar with Rondeau and the poet Dan Chiasson, a regular contributor to this magazine and another close friend of Glück’s, sharing thin strips of charred beef heart, tendrils of squid blanketed in a silky, lavender-hued olive mayonnaise, and a formidable mound of chaufa de mariscos—Chinese Peruvian seafood fried rice. To drink, Chiasson ordered his usual, a gin Martini, recalling fondly how Glück would fish the olives out of his glass. As an epileptic, she abstained from hard liquor, but she loved the flavor.

“The whole book is about this idea of duality,” Rondeau said. “It’s an allusion to her two grandchildren”—Glück’s only child, Noah Dranow, is the father of twins—“but it’s also Louise. I think many people are two sides.” Rondeau designed Rosa y Marigold’s dining room to have two sections, each with a mirrored-steel slab hanging overhead, slightly angled to offer a glimpse of the other side.

That afternoon, we’d visited Celeste, the couple’s first restaurant, which they opened in the spring of 2018 in a tiny triangular storefront in Somerville. Glück, whose apartment was nearby, soon became a regular, with a regular order: a cold beer, the arugula salad with lime and Parmesan, and the ceviche, sometimes followed by the sudado de pescado, a Chinese Peruvian dish of steamed fish and vegetables. She always came with a friend, occasionally two, and often requested that the music be turned down.

The couple knew little about her until, one day, Glück left behind an envelope return-addressed to Yale, and Rondeau looked her up, to get it back to her. After that, Glück made her reservations with Rondeau directly. “If anything was off—the salt, the lime, the fish—I would get a text,” Rondeau said affectionately. “She had a perfect palate!” Chiasson added. “She was our in-house critic,” Rondeau continued. “And I would always be extremely grateful, because she kept it real.”

One evening, Rondeau and Calderón mentioned that they’d been approached about writing a cookbook. The next time they saw her, she handed them a typewritten foreword, detailing how she’d first encountered Celeste in Boston magazine, which had published a photo of the ceviche. “It was a summer day and I wanted that ceviche immediately,” she wrote, admiring how it was “not drowned by the chef’s noisy creativity in shards of coconut or a confetti of pomegranate seeds / A very pure looking dish of glistening blue cod.” At the restaurant, Glück became “more like a citizen” than a regular, she wrote. “And Celeste is a world. But a world so much more wonderful and reliable than our real world.” Glück ate dinner at Celeste on the day she won the Nobel, surrounded by friends of Rondeau’s and Calderón’s, whom they’d invited to insure her privacy.

I first learned about Glück’s passion for restaurants in the spring of 2020. At the time, I was this magazine’s weekly restaurant critic, and I had shifted, suddenly, from dining out all over New York City to chronicling the industry’s attempts to stay afloat during lockdown. Glück e-mailed me to commiserate, and to ask if I had ideas for how to help. “Among so many horrors, the collapse of these enchanted worlds seems somehow desperately poignant,” she wrote.

I was lightly shocked to hear from her. I knew and revered her as one of the great living poets, and imagined her as particularly godlike, too high-minded to care about something as earthly as food. There was also the fact that she had struggled with anorexia as a young woman, which she wrote about in her 1989 lecture “Education of the Poet.” We struck up a sporadic correspondence. “I love to cook and have been a terrific and fastidious home cook,” she wrote with refreshing candor, but she yearned for the ritual of dining out. (Later, I learned that she’d bought a heat lamp for Celeste so that she could eat there outdoors.)

In “Education of the Poet,” Glück, who was born to a wealthy family in New York, in 1943 (her father, a Hungarian Jewish immigrant, helped invent the X-Acto knife), described her anorexia as an attempt to “establish a self with clear boundaries.” “The conflict played itself out most fiercely with my mother,” she wrote, who “only wavered when I began to refuse food, when I claimed, through implicit threat, ownership of my body, which was her great accomplishment.” By all accounts, Glück’s mother, Beatrice, was a wonderful cook; Glück’s papers, archived at Yale’s Beinecke Library, make clear that she grew up among people who loved to eat. In one letter from summer camp, addressed to her younger sister, Tereze, Glück wrote, “I miss—sundaes, shrimp cocktails,” next to tiny sketches of each.

Glück recovered from anorexia through years of psychoanalysis—years during which she also studied poetry with Léonie Adams and Stanley Kunitz. She published “Firstborn,” her début collection, in 1968, when she was twenty-five. Later letters detailed lavish hotel meals in Europe with her first husband and dinner parties in Plainfield, Vermont, where she taught writing at Goddard College and worked part time at a café, baking pastries in exchange for child care. (Noah, her son, grew up to be a sommelier.)

In 1980, John Dranow, Glück’s second husband and the director of the writing program at Goddard, co-founded the New England Culinary Institute, a small cooking school that operated restaurants in Montpelier. Glück sat on the board of directors until the nineties, when they divorced and she moved to Cambridge. In one poem from “Meadowlands,” an unsparing, darkly funny collection from that time, an unidentified husband and wife argue over food. “I stopped liking artichokes when I stopped eating / butter. Fennel / I never liked,” he says. “Living with you is like living / at boarding school: / chicken Monday, fish Tuesday.”

“Why do you call it rigidity?” she retorts. “Can’t you call it a taste for ceremony? Or is your hunger for beauty / completely satisfied by your own person?” The husband has the last word, accusing her of joyless pleasure-seeking: “When you make artichokes, / make them for yourself.”

The word “poet,” Glück wrote, “must be used cautiously; it names an aspiration, not an occupation. In other words: not a noun for a passport.” She could go years without writing, though she rejected the notion of writer’s block, perceiving instead a liminal state of waiting for poems to arrive. During fallow periods, Glück occupied herself with teaching, which she loved, gardening (she claimed to have read nothing but catalogues for two years), and eating at restaurants. “Dinner takes a certain shape,” the poet Katie Peterson, who ate with her frequently at Chez Panisse when Glück taught at Stanford, observed. “A familiar menu is like a rosary.”

In Cambridge, Chiasson pointed out Koreana, the restaurant where he, as a worshipful Harvard grad student, had first spotted Glück, in 1997, having dinner with the poets Robert Pinsky and Frank Bidart. (Bidart, Chiasson’s mentor, eventually introduced them.) Chiasson showed me an unpublished poem that he’d written for Glück after she died: “back before anything happened / yet it was still the bitter end / three poets sat around a lazy susan / kimchi, Tsing Tao, bibimbap, gossip.”

Earlier that day, we’d dropped in on the poet Henri Cole, in Boston’s South End. The two men, who had each shared countless meals with Glück, but never together, delighted in comparing notes, remembering her habit of sampling several wines before inevitably choosing something crisp and dry; her insistence that no meal was complete without greens; and the decisiveness with which she could turn on a place she’d loved. She almost always ordered the fish. She almost never ordered dessert, but would happily pluck the raspberry off of someone else’s crème brûlée. She preferred to eat early, and would wait outside her home, ready to be picked up.

Chiasson and Cole had last seen each at La Royal, another of Rondeau and Calderón’s restaurants, at a memorial for Glück, packed with her dinner companions. When Rondeau first told Glück, in 2021, that she and Calderón were considering opening a new place, Glück advised against it, then immediately changed her mind on a visit to the empty storefront, in a beautiful brick building from 1910, facing Royal Avenue in Cambridge. Not long before she died, Glück weighed in on the menu—both the dishes served and the printed layout—and chose the restaurant’s name.

Chiasson theorized that his friend’s love for both restaurants and poetry stemmed from her interest in “primal urges.” “We build these institutions to contain them and express them,” he said. “Marriage, restaurants, homes—they’re frames for containing all this passion.” In 1999, Glück imagined a creation story of sorts, describing small worlds that appeared in the fissures of a larger, shattered one. “On Huron Avenue they became / a block of stores,” she wrote, “whatever / they were or sold, they were / alike in their function: they were / visions of safety. Like / a resting place.” She called the poem “Formaggio,” after her favorite cheese shop, in Cambridge. ♦

Fifteen seats on the Los Angeles County Superior Court are up for election June 2, but only 11 of the contests are competitive with two or more candidates.

Binh Q. Dang (Office 39), Ann M. Maurer (Office 60), Mariela Torres (Office 141) and Candice J. Henry (Office 196) are running unopposed for seats on the bench.

Three of the competitive seats are held by incumbents, while eight races are wide open. Candidates who earn more than 50% of the vote in the primary win the seat outright. If no candidate receives a majority, the top two vote-getters move onto the November ballot.

Of the competitive races, two are unusually contentious.

In one, a judge is seeking reelection even though he has been removed from hearing cases because of what a presiding judge deemed deteriorating mental and physical health. The candidate, however, argues that he suffers from Parkinson’s disease, which has not affected his mental fitness.

The judge says he believes the commissioners — who found no ethical or moral wrongdoing — took issue with the way he criticized litigants rather than the substance of what he actually said.

The county court system, which hears criminal cases as well as a multitude of civil matters, is vast with locations as far north as Lancaster and as far south as Catalina Island.

The Judicial Elections Evaluation Committee of the Los Angeles County Bar Assn. has rated the 28 candidates in contested races in this election. Robert S. Draper, Superior Court judge Bar rating: Not qualified

Draper joined powerhouse firm O’Melveny and Myers in 1968 and made partner in 1976. He represented major national and international clients and focused on complex civil litigation, including antitrust, business disputes and other matters. He started his own firm in 2005.

In 2012, then-Gov. Jerry Brown appointed Draper to the Los Angeles County Superior Court, where he now presides over high-value business disputes and complex civil matters. Previous assignments include juvenile dependency and civil litigation.

Priorities in his courtroom include fairness and efficiency.

“That means keeping cases moving, requiring preparation from counsel, and making timely decisions so litigants are not left waiting,” he said in an email.

The election arrives as Draper, 84, faces professional scrutiny. In January, the Commission on Judicial Performance issued a 56-page inquiry, launching a proceeding that will examine whether he committed willful misconduct in office, exhibited a persistent failure or inability to perform duties, or acted with prejudicial conduct.

The seven counts include an appeals court decision invalidating a $10-million judgment reached in his courtroom. The appellate court noted that Draper made “extreme and bizarre comments” at a post-trial motions hearing and, during the trial, issued “arbitrary and capricious evidentiary rulings.”

Draper said he intended to fight the charges.

“The charges rely on statements that have been taken out of context and do not accurately reflect the record,” he told The Times, adding that the pending litigation limits his ability to respond. “I welcome a public proceeding where the facts can be fully examined and understood.

Draper, who has been working in the world of law for about six decades, is on leave from the bench. A presiding judge determined he was unfit to hear cases, mainly because of his health. Draper told The Times he has Parkinson’s disease, which does not affect his intellectual capacity to hear cases.

“Serving on the Superior Court has been the greatest honor of my professional life. I have taken that responsibility seriously every day, and I intend to defend my record and continue serving,” he said.

The Los Angeles County Bar Assn. rated him as unqualified for office, one of only three candidates across all races to receive the designation.

Priorities include ensuring access to justice within the courtroom, partly by “being attentive to language and cultural barriers.”

“I have seen firsthand how the justice system affects everyone involved — victims, defendants and families alike — and that perspective informs my approach to every case,” she said in an email.

Priorities include bridging a trust gap between the public and the legal system. She aims to improve “access to justice,” partly by reducing “unnecessary delays.”
