Former Democratic President Barack Obama has unloaded on Donald Trump for the Iran war, mocking the Republican president’s diplomatic strategy.

“These are not serious people. They’re not serious people. And the problem is you end up having to pay the price for their lack of seriousness,” Obama said Saturday while stumping for Democrats in Ohio.

The U.S., alongside Israel, started launching strikes against Iran in late February, with Trump claiming that Americans were in imminent danger over the Middle Eastern country’s nuclear ambitions.

But Obama argued that the issue had already been dealt with under his administration — until Trump unraveled it.

“Let’s think about this Iran war. The original rationale was, we’re going to go in there and get them to give up their nuclear program, their nuclear weapons,” Obama said. “Now, the thing was, Iran didn’t have nuclear weapons, because I put a deal in place that everybody acknowledged — kept them from getting nukes. And I didn’t have to fire a missile to do it; I just had to engage in diplomacy.”

Obama was referring to the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, commonly called the Iran Nuclear Deal, which the Middle Eastern country signed in 2015 and Trump withdrew from during his first term.

The deal restricted Iran’s nuclear program to ensure it was only using those materials for peaceful purposes in exchange for sanctions relief.

But Trump argued when withdrawing from the deal in 2018 that it “allowed Iran to continue enriching uranium and — over time — reach the brink of a nuclear breakout.”

Trump has insisted throughout the war that it would be a quick operation, but it has now dragged on for nearly eight months. Soon after the strikes began, Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, blocking oil tankers from passing through the vital passageway and causing fuel prices to skyrocket.

A total of 21 American service members have been killed and 865 wounded in operations related to the war, according to the Pentagon.

“So seven months later, how’s that war going?” Obama asked.

“Our allies and our bases have been attacked; Americans have been killed; hundreds of Americans have been wounded; we have, we’ve run down our stock of missiles,” the former president said, though Trump has denied reports of munitions shortages. “And Iran now has veto power over the Strait of Hormuz, where oil goes through to the world market.

“This is the reason why the price of oil is up. Nobody disputes that. This is reason the price diesel is up. This is why the reason the just about everything is up.”

Trump is hosting daily rallies across the country to boost Republicans’ chances of keeping control of Congress in November’s midterm elections — all while dealing with his deeply unpopular war.

Forty-seven percent of Democrat voters and 56 percent of Republican voters think ending the war in Iran should be one of the highest priorities for the next Congress, according to a new AP/NORC Center for Public Affairs Research poll.

Obama was in Cleveland Saturday to support Amy Acton, who is running for Ohio governor against Trump ally Vivek Ramaswamy, and former Senator Sherrod Brown, who is running against Republican Senator Jon Husted.

Ramaswamy saw his betting odds plummet over a controversial ad using his opponent’s mother, whom Acton has accused of abuse, to call her a “liar” and urge Ohians not to vote for her.

Acton has an 83 percent chance of winning the race compared to Ramaswamy’s 18 percent on the prediction market Polymarket as of Saturday evening.

Ramaswamy was also trailing Acton by seven points in a traditional poll. Acton had 50 percent of support among likely Ohio voters compared to Ramaswamy’s 43 percent, according to a recent survey conducted by Marist Poll before the campaign ad was released.

In that same poll, Brown was up four points with 48 percent of voter support compared to Husted’s 44 percent.

#602. Macron accused of putting France’s right to protest at risk with ‘vandal’s law’

Making innocent protesters liable for damage would be bad for rule of law and play into far-right hands, say opponents

Emmanuel Macron has been accused by trade unions of trying to curb the fundamental French right to protest as the government fast-tracks a bill to make protesters pay for damage caused by vandalism at street demonstrations even if they did not directly cause it themselves.

The French president, who leaves office next spring, has faced more than a month of protests in which high-school students have blockaded schools demanding a response to teacher absences, repairs to decrepit buildings and changes to the university applications platform.

Trade unions and gilets jaunes (yellow vest) anti-government protesters are now joining sustained street demonstrations in a growing social crisis over schools, as well as the cost of living and public services. Authorities have increased security measures and placed restrictions on demonstrating after France saw one of its worst days of violence in years earlier this month when largely peaceful demonstrations were marred by looting, fires and vandalism of state buildings.

The senate will this week debate the government’s proposed “vandals pay” law. The legislation would mean that a person protesting could be held liable to pay up to €10,000 for damage and vandalism even if they did not directly cause it themselves, if they were continuing to demonstrate nearby or as part of the same group as perpetrators. The bill also provides for greater use of drones over protests.

The prime minister, Sébastien Lecornu, said the bill, which was originally prepared in the wake of violent clashes after Paris Saint-Germain’s victory over Arsenal in the football Champions League in May, was “absolutely essential” after looting and burning during recent demonstrations across France.

The bill comes as the government struggles to contain the current crisis. Marine Le Pen, of the far-right, anti-immigration National Rally (RN), has seen her frontrunner status in next spring’s presidential election boosted as she seeks to reinforce her law-and-order credentials and back police.

Le Pen, who has lodged an appeal to France’s highest court over her conviction for embezzlement of European parliament funds, launched her presidential campaign on Sunday at a rally in Orléans, promising to “bring back order to France”.

The RN has claimed the “vandals pay” bill was originally its idea and Le Pen said she would ensure damage to property was paid for by rioters. Le Pen told a cheering crowd of more than 8,000 people at the rally: “Vandals will pay and if it takes their whole life to pay, then they will spend their whole life paying.”

She said: “We are going to restore the rule of law. Who can claim that there is rule of law when hordes come to ransack and burn our buildings?”

Éric Coquerel, from Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s radical left La France Insoumise, said on Sunday that the “vandals pay” law “would mean the end of street demonstrations” in France because people would not want to take the risk of being held responsible and made to pay up to €10,000 for vandalism they had not committed. The Communist senator Fabien Gay told the parliamentary TV channel Public Sénat: “No demonstration could take place again in our country.”

In an open letter to Le Monde, leftwing trade unions and France’s Human Rights League wrote: “This unfair law, which is a serious attack on the freedom to demonstrate, should be abandoned, or risk a major step back for the rule of law.”

At this weekend’s protests there was widespread anger at Macron for banning non-union demonstrations that his government deemed “illegitimate” and for keeping all protesters away from city centres.

Although Macron has not put in place a state of emergency, the government and local authorities have introduced limits on demonstrating, including bans on gatherings in front of schools and demonstrating in city centres, as well as curfews in some places and extended periods of police custody for the record number of people arrested.

At demonstrations this weekend, many said they had come out to defend the French right to protest in the street. Street protests in France, which are seen as a fundamental way for people to make themselves heard by a centralised state, have regularly been used to express criticism of changes to the law, from pension changes to same-sex marriage.

Serge Slama, a professor of public law at Université Grenoble Alpes, told Telerama magazine: “Although a state of emergency has been ruled out [by Emmanuel Macron] at this point, we have seen a number of decrees bringing a wide restriction on public liberties.”

He said this was “unprecedented” and would benefit the far right if they won elections next year, allowing them to put in place further crackdowns without changing the law.

Lecornu will meet representatives of protesting high-schoolers on Monday as the government comes under pressure to respond to their demands with funding and school repairs in order to avoid further school blockades.

The Government's emergency committee, Cobra, is meeting twice a week for crisis talks about water provision in the south-west of England and Sussex.

A 'reasonable worst-case scenario' drawn up in secret by officials suggested water stocks could plummet to zero per cent in weeks.

Without action, it could lead to taps running dry in homes and affect the construction of a nuclear power plant.

Wimbleball reservoir which supplies more than 400,000 people in Somerset is currently at 13 per cent capacity, while Ardingly, which serves 280,000 customers in West Sussex, is at just 17 per cent.

A summer of record-breaking heatwaves led to widespread droughts, with some parts of England still under hosepipe bans even after two autumn storms have hit the country.

Sussex and the south-west were two of the areas worst affected by the lack of rainfall, having faced their driest April to September for more than a century and are still facing critically low water supplies.

Crisis plans have been drawn up for a terrifying 'Day Zero' scenario where reservoirs run completely dry in each area.

Fears of winter droughts have led to requests from water companies for the government to impose non-essential use bans which would prevent businesses from being able to operate car washes, water sports fields and refill private pools.

Southern Water, South East Water and South West Water have all called on Environment Secretary Dame Angela Eagle to consider such bans.

South West Water has told the government privately it does not anticipate Wimbleball running dry, due to preventative measures it had put in place, while South East Water does not expect to run dry until next year, if situations don't improve.

However, concerns still remain for provisions in Devon's Venford reservoir and 'day zero' risk monitoring has begun for Stithians and Drift reservoirs in Cornwall.

Even if the reservoirs did run completely dry, water companies would likely be able to transfer supplies from elsewhere.

Despite wet weather returning across the country, experts warned that normal levels of rainfall will not be enough to counteract the effects of the drought.

Officials said rainfall would need to be 120 per cent higher than average in the next six months in order to provide a nationwide recovery.

Water companies have been told to conduct daily monitoring of reservoir and river levels and tougher water quality monitoring, and the Environment Agency will increase compliance checks on businesses that abstract water to check they are only taking permitted amounts.

Last week Dame Angela ordered the Environment Agency to prioritise public water supply and allow fewer releases of reservoir water into rivers to help clean them and keep ecosystems healthy to reduce 'supply interruption'.

However, this has led to fears about the effect such a move could have on salmon and other fish, which could perish due to a lack of clean water.

The government told The Times: 'Customer water supplies remain uninterrupted. We have instructed water companies to transfer water to support the lowest reservoirs, speed up leak detection and deliver daily monitoring of reservoir and river levels to help ease the pressure on public supplies.'

South West Water said it was not expecting any water restrictions in early November. It said: 'We are taking urgent action to protect drinking water supplies, repairing around 440 leaks a week and transferring three million litres of water a day to areas where it is needed most.

'We are also exploring additional water sources and working directly with households and high-using businesses to reduce consumption.'

South East Water said 'day zero' was not being considered for 2027 given the actions it had taken. Southern Water and EDF Energy, the company behind Hinkley Point C, declined to comment.

John Halsall, chief executive of South East Water, previously told The Mail, supplies in the region are now under 'severe pressure' and standpipes may appear on British streets for the first time in 50 years.

Mr Halsall also ended exemptions to a months-long hosepipe ban in Sussex – with the rules expected to be in place until next year.

However, he admitted his company's efforts to persuade customers to use less water had not been as successful as hoped – with water demand falling by about 5 per cent rather than around 10 per cent as expected.

Mr Halsall also recognised that outages in Tunbridge Wells and other towns and villages had undermined customer confidence.

#605. Your guide to Proposition TE: Exempting fire victims from the 'mansion tax'

Many changes were proposed this election cycle to Measure ULA — the so-called ‘mansion tax’ that levies a fee on property sales above $5.4 million to raise money for housing and homeless initiatives — but only one made it to the ballot. Proposition TE seeks to exempt Palisades fire victims from paying the tax if they sell their home within five years of the disaster.

Measure ULA, which took effect in 2023, levies a 4% transfer tax on all L.A. property sales above $5.4 million and a 5.5% tax on sales above $10.9 million. Under current rules, if a Pacific Palisades resident who lost their home in the fire sold the vacant lot for, say, $6 million, they would owe $240,000 in transfer taxes.

Proposition TE would grant a one-time exemption to that tax, offered only to homeowners in Pacific Palisades selling properties that were damaged or destroyed by the Palisades fire. The exemption would last until Jan. 6, 2030 — five years from the date of the fire — and could retroactively be applied for refunds if a resident already sold their property and paid the tax. It applies to all residential properties, whether or not their owners lived there.

Advocates argue that fire victims already have been through enough, and paying the tax as a result of selling a house that’s been destroyed is unfair.

The ballot measure was approved by 13 of the 15 L.A. City Council members. Councilmember Traci Park, who led the initiative, called it the “least we can do as the community continues to recover,” adding that many are forced to sell because of lack of insurance or rebuilding costs.

“They’re not selling to profit. They’re not selling because they want to. They’re selling because they’ve already lost everything and there’s nothing left,” Park said in a July meeting.

Critics argue that the exemption is too broad and should have included only victims whose primary residence was destroyed as opposed to landlords or investors with portfolios of properties.

Councilmember Eunisses Hernandez, the only council member who voted against putting the measure on the ballot, called it a “loophole for the ultrawealthy” and said any exemption should favor lower-income victims such as tenants who lost rentals or laborers who lost work.

“When wealthy property owners need relief, we’re willing to bend over backwards … to meet their needs and desires. But when working-class people need protections, suddenly our hands are tied,” she said at a meeting in July.

Others are hesitant to stop any funds from flowing into Measure ULA, which raises funding for housing initiatives in the midst of a housing crisis. The exemption could reduce ULA revenue by 6% per year, or $32 million annually, according to the L.A. Housing Department.

Proposition TE was spearheaded by Park, whose district includes Pacific Palisades. As fire victims navigate insurance gaps and expensive rebuilding costs, the exemption was proposed as a way to give them more flexibility should they choose to sell their homes.

#606. Ageing users and record deaths – the data behind Britain’s multibillion-pound cocaine habit

Sewage samples and crime statistics paint a picture of established users taking more cocaine, more often

Cocaine-linked crimes on the rise in Britain as consumption surges

Pubs, festivals and workplaces: how cocaine has become an everyday habit

From pub toilets and private parties to football terraces and far-right marches, cocaine has become a familiar presence across modern Britain. Deaths linked to the drug have risen to record levels, while its supply supports a multibillion-pound criminal industry.

But who is using the drug, and how has the picture changed? The figures do not necessarily show millions of new users, but highlight an established market in which some people take more cocaine, more often – and face greater harm as they grow older.

In June this year the scientists at the Home Office published a new experimental estimate of cocaine consumption. Samples taken from sewage suggested that cocaine use rose by 26% between 2021 and 2025. They estimated that people in England consumed 123,000kg of cocaine in 2024-25: the equivalent of roughly 2.5bn lines, with an estimated street value of £9.8bn.

Yet crime survey data suggests the number of people reporting cocaine use in the past year has remained flat or fallen slightly since the pandemic. One explanation is that a similar or smaller number of people are using the drug more frequently. Another is that the survey excludes people who are homeless or living in institutions, and some respondents may be reluctant to admit to illegal drug use.

Dr Josh Torrance, a specialist in drug markets, says wastewater analysis can reveal use that surveys miss. Having surveyed festivalgoers, he said: “People are often not honest about their drug use with strangers.” Although sewage estimates have substantial margins of error, and the estimated street value should be taken with a pinch of salt, the direction of travel is broadly accurate.

For several years, the market offered cocaine at falling prices. The National Crime Agency (NCA) told the Guardian that wholesale prices dropped below pre-pandemic levels in 2022 and reached “historically low levels” in 2025. However, it has seen signs that wholesale prices are rising again in 2026.

Torrance tracks advertised prices for an ounce of cocaine from a pool of vendors. He says the price fell from about £1,300 in March 2022 to about £1,000 by August 2023. When he resumed collecting data in September 2025, it was about £700. That remained steady until June this year, when it rose to roughly £800; in July it reached £1,000. By September it had eased to about £950.

“I’ve never seen such a quick increase in the price of cocaine,” he says of the summer rise. He suspects a supply disruption, but says the cause is difficult to pin down in a market with many suppliers and routes.

High purity means that cheaper supply is more dangerous. Torrance says cocaine sold as “pure” is often about 70-80% pure, and a more diluted tier (sometimes marketed as “social cocaine”) may be about 60%. The NCA says wholesale purity has remained high in recent years. In Torrance’s view, “a reduced price drives up use. With increased use and increased purity, you end up with more deaths.”

Crime survey data – while subject to caveats – shows younger people are still the most likely to use the drug, but that use is falling among the young, and rising among older age groups.

Other statistics back this up. In England, 33,612 people started treatment for cocaine problems in 2024-25 – up 57% from 2019-20 – which also suggests an increase in frequent users.

Millennials seem to be the generation with the most problematic use. People in their 30s were the largest age group seeking help. Ten years earlier, it was those in their 20s. “We’ve seen a cohort of cocaine users age and continue to use cocaine,” says Torrance.

There has been a 10% rise in recorded crime rates mentioning cocaine since 2019, according to figures collected by the Guardian from 39 police forces.

Police recorded almost 30,000 crimes mentioning the word cocaine in 2025. Most were drug offences, but 18% were violent crimes, including stalking and harassment and one murder case. About 4% were sexual offences, including 344 rapes.

A police record mentioning cocaine does not by itself demonstrate cocaine use but the figures do give an indication of the direction of travel.

Home Office figures suggest that nearly six in 10 police drug tests carried out on people arrested on suspicion of domestic abuse were positive for cocaine between July 2025 and June this year. Those tested may be more likely to have used drugs than other domestic abuse suspects, but the figures highlight cocaine’s exacerbating role.

More older people taking cocaine, increased purity on the streets, and a possible increase in frequent users have coincided with mounting harms, and not just for the victims of crime. A record number of people are now dying from taking cocaine in England and Wales. This rise has mainly been driven by older people, with more than half of cocaine deaths registered last year involving someone over 40.

Torrance says the ageing of a group that continues to use cocaine is reflected in hospital presentations and deaths. Acute overdose deaths often involve cardiac problems, he says, while the health effects of long-term use add to the risks.

An independent report last year found that the UK lacked a coordinated drug prevention strategy with dedicated funding, despite international evidence showing drug prevention programmes work.

“One reason cocaine deaths have risen so dramatically over the last five to 10 years is that we didn’t invest in upstream prevention,” Torrance says. Earlier drug education and helping people at risk engage with services would help.

“The more we can do that, the fewer of these issues we’ll see decades down the line.”

#608. Germany sit out FIFA Under-15 World Cup as Russia return

Germany is one a handful of FIFA's 211 nations to refuse an invitation to play at the inaugural Under-15 World Cup. The tournament will also mark Russia's return to the FIFA fold.

With nearly 1,000 matches and 189 teams, the Under-15 World Cup in Azerbaijan is bloated even by FIFA's usual standards. It marks a change in its attitude to children's international tournaments, which have not existed on anywhere near this scale before.

For Germany, it is too much too soon. The German FA (DFB) told DW that it had "consciously decided not to participate." The statement from the organization suggested the DFB — whose development of young players is under scrutiny after a run of poor performances by the men's national team — felt such a tournament was not appropriate for players so young.

"At this age level, our identification and development process is still underway, and we therefore do not believe it is appropriate to nominate a fixed squad for an international tournament at this stage," it read.

"More broadly, the DFB's approach to talent development is based on the principle of supporting young players in a long-term and age-appropriate manner. We carefully assess at which stage international competitions and development measures are beneficial for each age group.

"In our view, young talents should not be exposed too early to the accompanying aspects of professional football. These include increased expectations and pressure, public evaluation of their performances, and early interest from agents and the football market."

The refusal to play in the tournament, which began this week, is consistent with Germany's more cautious approach to youth development. While in some leagues, like the Premier League in England, there is no regulation about youths playing professionally, in Germany's top flight, the Bundesliga, players must be 16. In fact, before the 2020-21 season, players could not be granted a pro license before they were 17 years and six months old.

That change enabled Youssoufa Moukoko to make his Bundesliga debut for Borussia Dortmund a day after his 16th birthday in 2020, becoming the league's youngest player in the process.

Arguably the biggest change is to Area Planning Commissions, a group of seven volunteer-led boards that hear cases from homeowners, developers and businesses and make rulings on liquor licenses, hillside developments and more.

The amendment would consolidate those seven commissions into a single unified Neighborhood Appeals Commission.

The goal is efficiency. State laws passed in recent years have changed the appeals process, leading to lower caseloads for the seven boards. It’s also a financial drain on the city to train and take on so many volunteer commissioners while also paying overtime for staffers to host evening meetings for seven bodies — think audio-visual specialists, tech support, etc.

Under the amendment, the total number of commissioners would shrink from 35 to as few as seven, and the staff required to host meetings also would shrink significantly.

A unified board would eliminate the risk of commissions issuing different rulings. Picture a developer trying to build a hillside home; a commission that’s wary of such projects could reject it, while another commission that leans more pro-housing could approve it. Having a single board means that all appeals and applications would be governed by the same logic.

Could an L.A. skyscraper rise higher than those in New York City or Chicago? Could downtown L.A. claim not just the tallest building west of the Mississippi River but also in the entire country?

This amendment allows the City Council to change the existing floor area ratio (FAR) restrictions, which cap the height of certain buildings. FAR ratios vary by neighborhood and height districts, but the highest ratio is currently 13x, meaning a building’s total square footage never can be more than 13 times the size of the parcel of land that it sits on. For reference, L.A.’s tallest building, the Wilshire Grand Center, has a ratio of roughly 10.5, depending on how you calculate the square footage and acreage.

These limits were set in the 1950s, when L.A.’s population was roughly half of what it is now. This amendment would give City Council the flexibility to set higher limits to add density in order to adapt to a growing population.

Individual projects still would require zoning reviews, so no need to worry about skyscrapers coming to Silver Lake.

You just opened a taco shop and you want to serve margaritas. The City Council said it’s fine, but you’re waiting to hear back from the City Planning Commission. They’re taking too long, and your patrons are drinking water while you wait for a liquor license.

Under current rules, decisions from either the City Council or Planning Department can get kicked back and forth indefinitely. This amendment sets deadlines for both sides to speed up rulings: Planning boards have 30 days to concur or dissent with City Council decisions, and City Council gets 21 days to hold a final vote on Planning decisions — or else the Planning decision becomes final.

Here’s how it would work. Say there’s an amendment to the city’s General Plan, which guides land use decisions, or a tweak to one of the neighborhood community plans that oversee development in areas like Hollywood or Boyle Heights. The mayor has signed off on it. The City Planning Commission approves too. But the City Council just hasn’t quite gotten to it yet.

Under current rules, if the City Council doesn’t vote on an amendment within 75 days of the mayor and Planning Commission approvals, it’s automatically denied. This amendment reverses that; if the City Council doesn’t get to an amendment within 75 days, it’s automatically approved.

In recent years, homeowners, developers and businesses have complained that navigating the Planning Department can be complicating and confusing. These changes aim to streamline how permits and projects move through the department.

#631. Wikipedia’s Pyrrhic Victory

In a world where state policy is shaped by A.I. hallucination, it’s cute to remember that consulting Wikipedia was once seen as irresponsible. Schools banned its use, and journalists were shamed for credulously repeating its hoaxes. “Reads like Wikipedia” was a literary insult. As a millennial, I had teachers who spoke of the encyclopedia in terms of epistemological stranger danger: You don’t know who wrote those articles and what they want you to believe! And, indeed, we do not. But we do know, a quarter century on, that Wikipedia—a text of thirty billion words, in three hundred languages—is the greatest compendium of human knowledge ever assembled. It is also, as Richard Cooke writes, “an escapee from a parallel universe, a refugee of the internet as it should have been.”

Discover notable new fiction, nonfiction, and poetry.

Cooke’s new book, “The Last Best Place on the Internet: A Human History of Wikipedia,” takes on this colossus, a relic of early-aughts idealism on a web that tech giants long ago carved into algorithmically barbarized fiefdoms. Like some crumbling Roman library in the Dark Ages, the encyclopedia is, in many ways, “both too big to understand and somehow too ephemeral to bother trying,” he writes. (The English-language Wikipedia alone would take forty sleepless years to read.) But he’s less interested in its content than he is in those who built it. The book’s driving insight is that Wikipedia is best studied as a subculture. It was neither the first online encyclopedia—Britannica had a web presence by 1999—nor even the first that users could edit. Rather, its vitality arose from the community of obsessives whom it attracted, and its decline has coincided with the market’s capture of their creativity.

Wikipedia has lost more than a billion monthly visits in the last four years—a twenty-five-per-cent drop that, because the site recruits its editors from its audience, also threatens its growth and quality. Much of its traffic has been intercepted by A.I., as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini overtake traditional search. And these chatbots were weaned on Wikipedia, “the single most important training corpus in the history of artificial intelligence,” Cooke informs us. In short, the encyclopedia is being destroyed by its biggest beneficiaries, like a golden goose slaughtered for slop-pâté. It’s a cruelly ironic fate for a project that began as a failed startup’s castoff. “Had anyone predicted Wikipedia’s true value during its formative phase,” Cooke writes, “it would never have survived.”

Invisible hands built Wikipedia, but those of the free market hardly figured. It wasn’t for lack of trying: Jimmy Wales, one of the project’s co-founders, was an Ayn Rand fan who worked in finance. He launched Wikipedia’s for-profit predecessor, Nupedia, at a company called Bomis, which owed most of its advertising revenue to a softcore-porn directory called the Babe Engine. (Denis Diderot, too, sold smut, Cooke tells us, in the form of an erotic novel that supported his work on the Encyclopédie.) Nupedia was written and peer-reviewed by scholars, and was eventually supposed to make money from advertising. But the editorial process was painfully slow, and the business model was doomed by the dot-com crash. Wikipedia was a desperate attempt to salvage the project through crowdsourcing. It went online in January, 2001, against the objection of Nupedia’s academic advisory board.

Wikis—or modular, collectively edited websites—preceded Wikipedia. They were invented by Ward Cunningham, a software engineer in Portland, Oregon, who wanted to assemble a community history of programming ideas. His project touched off a “Jurassic wiki period” of similar experiments. It was Larry Sanger, whom Wales had hired to oversee Nupedia, who hit on the idea of building a wiki encyclopedia, though the two men dispute the exact details. (Sanger later left Wikipedia, and has criticized it in conservative media outlets.) Cooke does his best to dramatize these origins, often through dry humor, but the personalities involved don’t quite rise to the level of an Aaron Sorkin drama. Happily, Cooke devotes the bulk of his narrative to everyday Wikipedians and their collaboration’s emergent properties.

Wikipedia began life “as an unapologetic first draft, full of experiments, doodles, and marginalia,” Cooke writes. One of its decisive early advantages was a low barrier to entry. Because articles didn’t require anyone’s approval to write or edit, they accumulated quickly—and, with them, reader-editors, who flocked to the site from Google’s search engine, which at the time was still new. Much of what they wrote was junk. Early Wikipedia was full of short articles, called “stubs,” and unnecessarily detailed information about fictional universes; the Washington, D.C., of “Atlas Shrugged,” for instance, had a page before the real city. Yet the site’s pop-friendliness drew traffic, and its poor quality lured editors; then as now, people loved to correct strangers online.

The site’s breakout moment was 9/11. In an era before social media, Wikipedians’ real-time aggregation of news about the attacks established it as a serious informational resource. This also demonstrated that volunteers who’d come to the site for their own niche reasons could redirect their efforts en masse. (Cooke cites the Wikipedia researcher Brian Keegan, who compares this “migration of expertise” to the decentralized coördination of corals and insect swarms.) Bomis briefly considered trying to monetize the site’s runaway success. But the rumor of ad sales led to an insurrection on Spanish-language Wikipedia, which threatened to break away. In 2002, Wales transferred control of the site to his new nonprofit, the Wikimedia Foundation: an admission that Wikipedia wouldn’t work if it wasn’t free.

The encyclopedia became a mecca for outsiders. Cooke notes that its main period of growth, between 2001 and 2008, coincided with the coming of age of a generation “denied the hallmarks of adulthood by a conspiracy of circumstance.” This included jobless graduates, millennial “failsons,” layoff victims, hardcore fans, and the neurodivergent—just about everyone with “abilities that were hidden, overlooked, undervalued, or simply too weird to express elsewhere.” One “very senior Wikimedian,” we are told, “came to think of the project as a ‘city built by Asperger’s.’ ”

Cooke’s narrative is studded with mini-portraits of pioneering “super editors.” We meet Ruth Ifcher, an ex-coder and Ph.D. so agoraphobic that she rarely left the house, and Einar Kvaran, an unemployed and uncredentialled art historian editing from his monograph-crammed trailer home in New Mexico. Derek Ramsey, a former lay preacher from the edge of Amish country, believed that “everybody should have their own hometown” in the encyclopedia—and wrote the site’s first bot, which scraped census data, to make it so. A more controversial figure was Ryan Jordan, or Essjay, who presented himself as a Catholic theologian, and who featured in a 2006 article about Wikipedia for this magazine. Alas, it had to be corrected: Essjay was, in fact, a mere ex-legal secretary, who, according to Cooke, had been fired for using his firm’s computers to hoard porn.

His unmasking fed an emerging panic over Wikipedia’s accuracy. Cooke samples an absorbing range of hoaxes, from the college students who convinced the world that an imaginary Scotsman had invented the electric toaster to the Chinese housewife who fabricated an entire war out of boredom. Other examples are more sinister—for instance, the far-right takeover of Croatian Wikipedia, or efforts by corporations to launder their images. (An Exxon employee doctored an article about an oil spill.)

Wikipedia began tightening its policies in the wake of a 2005 scandal involving the journalist John Seigenthaler, Sr., whose page falsely implicated him in the assassinations of John and Robert F. Kennedy. Unregistered editors lost the ability to create new articles, and sensitive categories, such as “Biography of living persons,” implemented stricter sourcing requirements. The Wikimedia Foundation persuaded cultural institutions to hire “Wikipedians in residence,” and Wales became the toast of TED. The professionalization worked, but at the expense of the site’s internal culture, Cooke argues, as generous eccentrics were displaced by neurotic lovers of rules.

Wikipedia has two main factions—the “Inclusionists” and the “Deletionists”—and their schism centers on how big the encyclopedia ought to be. The former held sway in the early days, but, around 2007, a year after English-language Wikipedia hit its millionth article, “a ‘Soup Nazi’ culture” set in “almost overnight.” Even Wales—affectionately known as the WikiPope, and studiously neutral about the schism—ran afoul of these “hanging judges,” who deleted his stub about a popular Cape Town restaurant. Nowadays, articles “nominated for deletion” have a sixty-five-per-cent conviction rate. The encyclopedia continued to grow, but its number of active editors hit a ceiling in 2007, and hasn’t recovered since.

Cooke makes no effort to hide his sympathies; at one point, he compares the Deletionists to bullies knocking down sandcastles at the beach. But are they really as responsible for the encyclopedia’s decline as their opponents pretend? After all, Wikipedia’s stagnation also coincided with the rise of social media, which Cooke hardly mentions. Could it be that the site’s lost generation wasn’t pushed away by Soup Nazi editors but pulled in by posting, podcasting, and short-form video? Annie Rauwerda, arguably the encyclopedia’s No. 1 booster, started out as an editor but made her name on Instagram, where she runs the wildly popular account DepthsofWikipedia.

Cooke’s more convincing charge is that Deletionism has kept Wikipedia parochial. Its editors are overwhelmingly male and Western, and their outlook is reflected in what’s deemed “notable” enough for inclusion. For many years, Pokémon were more thoroughly covered than most of Africa; the page for Makmende, a Kenyan superhero, was deleted over and over again for years, despite being the most popular meme in one of the Anglophone world’s most populous countries. Gender bias is similarly prevalent; in 2018, the physicist Donna Strickland’s article was culled, just a few months before she won the Nobel Prize. Wikipedians use edit-a-thons and affinity-based campaigns to address these disparities; a group called WikiProject Women Scientists actually inverted the “quality gap” in its category. But there are also deeper epistemological issues: What’s to be done about oral traditions, for instance? Or minority languages? (Greenlandic Wikipedia’s sole active admin had it shut down, arguing that its poor quality made it a threat to their culture.) Wikipedia has yet to overcome the blind spots of its Enlightenment predecessors, Cooke suggests: “Not only is most human knowledge not Western, most of it has never been written down.”

A.I. promises to exacerbate these problems. Wikipedia has already banned content generated by large language models, but it can’t stop people from outsourcing their inquiries to chatbots, Cooke writes, “breaking the virtuous chain of reading and writing that has sustained it so far.” L.L.M.s amplify the encyclopedia’s biases, which are repackaged as oracular truth, and shrink the community tasked with fixing them. For some A.I. boosters, that’s precisely the point. Last year, Elon Musk launched Grokipedia, an L.L.M.-written encyclopedia billed as a Wikipedia-killer. Humans can only suggest changes to articles, and there’s no forum for debating them. Grokipedia’s fate is uncertain—until recently, it had been “paused” for five months—but similar projects are surely in the offing. Cooke speculates that in the future, encyclopedists may be reduced to tending “the frost-bitten harvests of machines.”

Meanwhile, the same academia that once deemed Wikipedia dangerous is throwing open its doors to artificial intelligence. Chatbots, we are told, will empower students to engage with difficult primary sources—for example, by letting them debate theology with a virtual St. Ignatius de Loyola—or at least give professors a break from the thankless enforcement of A.I. bans. (A recent, widely criticized report on the state of the humanities even used L.L.M.s to identify politicized scholarship.) This embrace of the “inevitable” styles itself as a kind of humility: If the ivory tower misjudged Wikipedia, couldn’t the same be true of L.L.M.s? But there’s a difference between widening the conversation and sacrificing it to a simulacrum. Wikipedia’s “crime” was to democratize the encyclopedia, allowing anyone with an internet connection to participate in an Enlightenment project previously monopolized by experts. A.I., by contrast, threatens to expel us from the writers’ room of consensus reality, leaving to oligarchs what was once hashed out on the Talk page.

For all that Cooke celebrates Wikipedia, he writes as though it were already dead. He opens with an image of the encyclopedia literally lost on the moon—as part of the Lunar Library, a nano-sized repository of human knowledge that crashed in 2019—and repeatedly suggests that it may be the last such project that humans undertake. He may be right. But there’s still time to stand athwart history and shout, “Citation needed.” ♦

McOsker, who represents the Harbor Area and parts of South L.A., said he’d like to spend less time on the 110 Freeway getting downtown and more time serving his community directly. He called the frequent council meetings the “most nonproductive part of my week.”

Fewer meetings also may encourage people to instead attend council committee hearings, McOsker said, which often discuss measures in more detail and allow for robust public comment.

To qualify a referendum for the ballot, petitioners must collect a number of signatures equal to 10% of the votes cast in the last mayoral election. Amendment EE would increase this threshold to 15% of ballots cast.

Quan speculated that this was included — at the very top of the ballot measure — as a sweetener to help sell the less palatable provision to reduce the number of required council meetings. “It’s a really marginal reform,” he said.

Amendment EE would increase civil fines — those imposed by a judge through the court system — to the same level as administrative fines levied by the ethics commission.

McOsker said the higher penalty should lead to fewer violations.

Quan said this was another marginal change, noting that under Charter Amendment ER, approved by voters in 2024, the ethics commission can impose fines of up to $15,000. Under EE, private citizens or others filing ethics suits in civil courts could seek fines of the same amount.

In addition, Amendment EE would ban ethics commissioners or the executive director from running for City Council or the LAUSD Board of Education for five years after they leave office.

Stakeholders currently are defined as anyone who lives, works or owns real property within the neighborhood boundaries, or community organizations like chambers of commerce or nonprofits.

Europe is entering a “new era of green marketing” as more cities turn their backs on climate-damaging advertising.

It all started with Amsterdam, which became the first capital city in the world this year to ban billboards promoting fossil fuels and meat on 1 May – five years after the law was first proposed.

The ban is part of the city’s targets to reach carbon neutrality by 2050 and to halve meat consumption during the same period. Other Dutch cities, such as Utrecht, the Hague, Zwolle, Delft and Nijmegen already have similar bans in place.

Many other cities including Helsinki, Stockholm and Bristol have introduced bans on promoting fossil fuels or other carbon-intensive products such as aviation, shipping, fast-fashion and construction. However, many of these don’t include meat.

While the climate impacts of burning fossil fuels are widely understood, the environmental effects of animal agriculture get less attention.

A recent report from European non-profit Changing Markets found mounting evidence that departments within the UN Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) have a “bias in favour of the livestock sector” – as lobbyists seek to undermine climate action.

This includes high-ranking members of the FAO delivering speeches at the World Meat Congress and affiliated scientists from the animal agriculture industry attempting to discredit studies that promote plant-rich diets.

But there is no hiding that food and agriculture contribute one-third of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, second only to burning coal, oil and gas.

Animal-sourced foods in the EU are also believed to make up between 81 and 86 per cent of total GHG emissions of food production. However, they only supply an estimated 21 per cent of calories and 64 per cent of protein.

Over recent years, scientists have called for countries – particularly those in the West with access to plant-based alternatives – to reduce their meat consumption to tackle this.

Most notably, the 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission report – compiled by 70 leading experts from 35 countries – found that around 15 million deaths could be avoided each year if the world shifted to predominantly plant-based diets.

Experts say this could also cut non-CO2 GHG emissions from agriculture – such as methane from livestock and nitrous oxide from fertilisers – by 15 per cent by 2050.

When factoring in the entire food lifecycle – such as reducing deforestation, transportation and refrigeration, and restoring nature – the total drop in emissions would be much higher.

Amsterdam’s meat ban proved controversial, with many people on social media branding the ruling “ridiculous” – or arguing that their meat consumption isn’t linked to what they see on billboards.

“I can’t honestly remember the last time I actually purchased something directly because of an advertisement,” one Reddit user wrote. “Probably [when I was] a teenager.”

But Linda Aspey, a climate psychology specialist and board member of Climate Psychology Alliance, tells Euronews Earth that much of advertising’s influencing power comes from “repetition and cueing”.

“One study found that seeing meat imagery (in this case, a Big Mac) increased people’s desire to eat it, and its everyday presence likely reinforces meat as an ordinary choice,” she says.

“This may be reinforced by someone’s identity, culture of livelihood where eating meat is simply a part of that, and carries little conscious tension.”

Meat consumption in the Netherlands, for example, has been on the decline. In 2024, the Dutch ate less meat (including meat products) than at any time in the past 20 years, according to Wageningen Social and Economic Research’s ‘Meat Report’.

“For others, there's an uncomfortable pull between valuing animal welfare or the environment and still eating meat, and advertising may make that pull easier to set aside,” Aspey explains.

“What the evidence does show is that advertising typically shapes what feels normal for most of us, to varying degrees, which in turn can shape behaviours.”

Amsterdam is yet to announce any data on how the ban has impacted meat consumption in the country – but experts predict there will be a correlation.

When Transport for London (TFL) in the UK removed junk food advertising from its network, weekly household purchases were estimated to fall by nearly seven per cent, confectionery by almost a fifth.

This was measured through actual shopping data, not consumer surveys.

“The effect wasn't dramatic as a ban changes the constant prompts that people see around them day to day, without necessarily changing why people eat what they eat, whether that's habit, taste, livelihood or budget,” Aspey explains.

“As with tobacco control, such restrictions tend to work best combined with other measures, not alone.”

Despite the known climate-link, the EU has historically propped up foods like red meat with generous subsidies.

A recent report from charity Foodrise found that the EU’s common agricultural policy (CAP) directed more subsidies to the production of high-emitting animal-sourced foods than plant foods in 2020.

That year, animal-sourced foods received around 77 per cent of total CAP subsidies, accounting for €39 billion out of the total €51 billion spent.

The study found that beef and lamb, which are consistently identified as the biggest culprits of climate damage, were given around 580 times more in subsidies compared to legumes such as lentils and beans in 2020.

Going from funding these foods to actively stripping them from public advertising may feel too drastic – but experts say it could become part of a “mix of strategies” to tackle climate change.

“Most research points to sustainable diets needing meaningfully less meat than the UK eats now, for both planetary and personal health, and marketing that is weighted heavily towards meat – especially mass produced and cheaper – makes that shift harder," Aspey says.

In the final days of his final campaign, supporters of the Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva turned to a British classic. The City Charter, which serves as L.A.’s governing document, requires that the parks department receive .0325% of the total assessed value of taxable properties in Los Angeles.

That translated into $303 million for the park system this year. If the formula were left unchanged, the minimum allocation gradually would grow to $493 million over the next 10 years, according to a city analysis.

If voters approve Charter Amendment PRK, the minimum allocation would double that amount — to $986 million — by 2036, the analysis said.

“This forces a ramp-up in the expenditures that are dedicated to care for our parks,” said City Councilmember Monica Rodriguez, who spearheaded the proposal.

#650. “Fatherland” Cleans Up the Rough Edges of History

Paweł Pawlikowski’s “Fatherland” offers an amiable eighty-two minutes of condensed history, centered on the septuagenarian novelist Thomas Mann’s trip, in the summer of 1949, to Germany—West and East—in order to receive each country’s Goethe Prize on the occasion of that great predecessor’s bicentenary. If Pawlikowski’s aim was to portray Mann (played by Hanns Zischler) as a terminal bore and a pompous ass, he succeeded brilliantly, even if he failed to suggest the reasons for his own animosity. Mann, who has been living in Los Angeles since 1941, is accompanied on the journey by his daughter Erika (Sandra Hüller), who serves as his assistant, even editing his speeches. And he does gives speeches. He gives an impromptu one at a press conference at their first stop, Goethe’s native Frankfurt; he gives a carefully crafted one at the formal prize ceremony there, and he gives another at the East German ceremony in Weimar, Goethe’s longtime home, overtly under Soviet dominion. What’s more, he gives speeches, terse but fusty, in private, to Erika.

For the most part, Mann struts around looking iconic, bearing an air of absurd vanity in his belief that, as an eminent anti-Nazi exile and a Nobel Prize winner who left Germany, in 1933, soon after Hitler came to power, his supra-ideological presence in both Germanies, at a time of Cold War tension, could indeed matter. But Mann most of all strikes an air of formality even in private, even when, in Frankfurt, he gets the grim news of the death of his son Klaus (August Diehl)—Erika’s brother and confidant, who is also a noted author—from a drug overdose. (His death was ruled a suicide.) Most pompous of all are the silences; the most conspicuous of them hollows out one of the major early scenes, when father and daughter, newly arrived in West Germany and travelling by car to Frankfurt, pass through streets of destruction, a country ravaged by war—and remain silent. Yes, awestruck and horrified, no doubt; still, I wondered throughout why silence served Pawlikowski as an optimal mode of expression.

Throughout my viewing, something felt off—in style and in substance—and the first concrete diagnosis came through in my colleague Anthony Lane’s recent New Yorker Profile of Pawlikowski, which I had deliberately delayed reading until after seeing the movie. (History may have no spoilers, but drama does.) That in-depth portrait mentions a fundamental change the filmmaker wrought upon Mann’s real-life 1949 journey: the author was, in fact, accompanied by his wife, Katia, who, in “Fatherland,” is said to be too weak to travel. The character of Erika, a writer and intellectual with her own range of uneasy connections to Germany—she’ll encounter an ex-husband and an ex-lover there—is a screenwriter’s convenience, a walking plot-production machine. Behind closed doors, however, the switch from a spouse to a child, from Mann the husband to Mann the father, is the switch from the intimate to the casual, from uninhibited speech to guardedness.

It took no more than minutes of curiosity to find that the fulcrum of “Fatherland,” the death of Klaus —the first character seen in the movie—occurred not during the Germany trip but some months before it. As for the trip itself, it was far more tumultuous, far more infuriating to Mann, and far more unflattering toward West Germany than the movie lets on. Upon his return to the United States, Mann wrote, for the Times, that, in West Germany, “they live by the slogan: ‘Everything was better under Hitler!’ By virtue of the experiences they claim to have had, they declare triumphantly that democracy has been tried and found wanting. Democracy to them means the Occupation Powers and all who ‘collaborate’ with them.” This isn’t the elegant stuffiness of “Fatherland”; it conjures a Fassbinder-esque film—outspoken, insolent, crude, loud, and brazen. Mann may not have shared his testimony, his vision, his voice with Erika during the tour, but he might have with Katia.

To share that voice in “Fatherland,” Pawlikowski would have to imagine it. But his economy of expression also suggests that the film, which is never at risk of spilling out beyond the confines of its scripted pointedness, depends on an economy of imagination. Its contrivances are less like conveniences or abridgments than like deceptions: the facts and the tone are in service to a style that itself has little room for variety or range. “Fatherland” is a muted, muffled quiet-box of understatement, twisting history less for dramatic effect than for affectlessness. Pawlikowski has developed a felt-lined aesthetic—of black-and-white images, in the traditional nearly square screen dimensions, that leave space around the characters—that bespeaks observational detachment and comes off as culture personified. This forced reserve and mannered dignity create a hermetic atmosphere of refinement (from behind the camera) and of elimination (in front of it). It is a cinema of prefabricated meaning and predetermined moods, in which images limit the very kinds of events that can be depicted.

Here, Pawlikowski further rarefies the style that he developed in “Ida” (2013) and advanced in “Cold War” (2018), but not to advantage. I found myself thinking of other filmmakers who rarefy: Christopher Munch, whose 1991 feature, “The Hours and Times,” about the 1963 Barcelona jaunt of John Lennon and Brian Epstein, has the decency to stick to these characters and not replace Epstein with, say, George Martin; or Hong Sangsoo, whose radical reduction of space and span in his new movie, “The Day She Returns,” creates great cinematic leeway for the uninhibited voice of its actress-protagonist; or Robert Bresson, whose severe restraint served artistically as both microscope and microphone, to experience infinitesimal gestures on a cosmic scale of moment and morality.

In “Fatherland,” such microscopy is reversed. Pawlikowski’s camera reduces the figures and voices, in order to fit them into his own rigidly molded spaces. The characters are petrified like specimens, expressing and embodying only what fits into the film’s design, with no potential for inconvenient loose ends for viewers to follow outside the frame’s borders. It’s a sieve too fine for anything as inconvenient as bodies or as coarse as discourse to get through—an aesthetic of purification, disincarnation, signification, as if the mere fact of keeping voices down and holding thoughts in were itself a mark of urbanity, conferring that cultural virtue upon a dignified audience. The movie sanitizes the past and removes its tang, its pungency, leaving a craving for contact with nature, with raw experience, with incongruities and accidents and anything but what fits, in lieu of such synthetic sophistication. ♦
