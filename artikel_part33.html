Open source in the public sector doesn’t fail because of technical issues. It fails because it’s not aligned with the political agenda

PRAGUE — At Open Source Summit Europe, Dr. Laura Dornheim, Munich’s chief digital officer, said in her keynote speech that Munich abandoned its LiMux Linux desktop deployment for Windows over a decade ago because of politics, but now it’s returning to open-source software.

It’s not returning to the Linux desktop, however — not yet, anyway. Instead, it’s switching its infrastructure programs from proprietary software to open-source software.  For example, it’s replacing VMware with Proxmox, moving from Jira and Confluence to OpenProject and XWiki, and adopting PostgreSQL as an alternative to Oracle databases.

The two infrastructure and collaboration migrations alone are expected to save roughly €1.5 million or more annually.

Taken together, these projects illustrate the difference between Munich’s first Linux campaign and its current approach. Rather than making desktop Linux its center pole, the city is replacing individual layers of proprietary infrastructure where it can make a practical case.

So, this comeback is not a wholesale resurrection of LiMux, the Debian/Kubuntu Linux desktop project that made Munich the Linux desktop standard-bearer between 2003 and 2013. Besides, Dornheim added, dealing with the elephant in the room, “So [if LiMux] was a success, why is it not alive anymore? Not because of technical issues.” So, what changed?

Dornheim explained it was all politics. “Open source in the public sector doesn’t fail because of technical issues. It mostly fails because it’s not aligned with the political agenda.” She continued that Munich had switched away from Linux because a new government got Microsoft to agree to make Munich the headquarters for Microsoft Germany.

The lesson from LiMux is that technical capability isn’t enough, she continued. Open-source projects also need durable political support.

Munich’s original move began as Windows NT 4.0 neared the end of support. Yes, that was over 20 years ago. In May 2003, its council voted to migrate to a tailor-made Linux distribution, LiMux.

The rollout began in earnest in 2006. By December 2013, Munich had completed the migration with more than 14,800 desktops running LiMux. A European Commission Open Source Observatory case study reported savings of about €11 million because the city dumped Windows.

Also: 7 open-source apps worth paying for

LiMux became the leading example that a large city could operate Linux desktops and open-source office software. It also exposed the difficulty of changing a sprawling government IT environment, particularly when Microsoft Office documents, legacy workflows, PDF use, and user expectations remained tied to proprietary systems.

The political climate changed after Munich’s 2014 municipal election. By November 2017, the council had voted to reverse course and return its computers to Windows, with a target of 2020. The office-suite transition took longer. Indeed, the migration from LibreOffice to Microsoft Office wasn’t completed until 2023.

So, despite what you may have heard, this was not a case of “Linux failed.” It didn’t. It was politics, pure and simple.

Today, Germany’s third-largest city is targeting proprietary platforms where open-source alternatives can deliver financial savings, greater control, and software that other municipalities can reuse. Digital sovereignty matters, but that’s not the primary driver in Munich.

That said, Dornheim noted that a cloud transition to Azure without sufficient assurance from Microsoft of General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliance would require the city to scrutinize who could use each tool. This management overheard didn’t help make a case for staying with Microsoft. It’s also a clear example of how digital sovereignty has become both an important technical and political issue.

Also: Inside the global push to replace US cloud giants with open-source

Dornheim added that her answer to getting politicians to support open-source software in the long run is to connect open source with priorities they understand. These include budgets, public services, and the ability to make software available across the administration.

She added that Munich’s current IT strategy is to ensure open-source software functions well without losing the political support needed to sustain it. It certainly helps that, thanks to the shift to open-source software, “We are saving roughly a million euros a year. So that’s again a great win, not just for us, for our belief in public and public code, but also for the politicians in Munich.

The new Munich IT plan also embraced the “public money, public code” principle: software developed with public funding should, where appropriate, be available for others to inspect, use, and improve. The commitment applied to newly acquired or developed software even as the earlier Windows migration continued.

Dornheim, who became Munich’s CDO in September 2022, has not treated that policy as a mandate for a third administration-wide operating-system upheaval. Supporting institutions have also changed. Munich established an Open Source Program Office in 2024. This added a dedicated structure for coordinating its open-source work. Its public software portal presents the city’s approach in three categories: Use existing open-source software, improve it, and publish software that the city develops itself.

That’s a broader commitment than just replacing Windows with a Linux distro. Munich is now an open-source software development participant, not simply a customer choosing between licenses.

There are several examples of Munich sharing its software. One such is DAVe, Munich’s application for documenting and analyzing traffic data. In use since 2021, the software has also been adopted by Wolfsburg, Germany.

Dornheim said such collaboration should become routine rather than depend on another city discovering a suitable project. “I think these matches shouldn’t just happen sort of by accident, but there needs to be a structure across the whole public sector. It’s on my wish list.”

Munich’s work also includes MUCGPT, a municipal AI application that enables employees to access large language model (LLM) capabilities. This helps with writing and proofreading emails.

The city is also investing in the open-source ecosystem. Its Open Source Sabbatical program supports developers working on open-source projects. The broader purpose is to support improvements and funding components used in open-source municipal services.

Put it all together, and Munich’s open-source approach is no longer simply about the Linux desktop. Instead, it’s all about Munich reducing its dependence on proprietary software, controlling costs, and turning publicly funded software into a reusable public asset. So far, the city’s doing well.

#1604. Designing an AI serving harness built to last

The engineering system surrounding an AI model increasingly determines whether its intelligence can be delivered reliably, economically, and at scale.

The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it.

That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?”

Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability.

For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it.

There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem.

A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures.

The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture.

That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic.

A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't.

The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost.

Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency.

Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance.

GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive.

The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat.

Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements.

For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value.

Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances.

That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy.

Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset.

EXL researchers have explored these production challenges in greater depth in the EXL Engineering the Harness series. It examines how serving architecture, memory management, scheduling, and inference optimization work together to make enterprise AI more efficient, scalable, and economically sustainable.

Increasingly, enterprises have access to many of the same models. Sustainable differentiation comes from how effectively an organization turns that intelligence into business outcomes.

The model provides the intelligence. The harness determines whether the enterprise can deliver outcomes reliably, economically, and at scale.

#1606. OpenAI checked fired researchers’ phones for Signal chats, WSJ reports

OpenAI safety researchers fired last month had disappearing Signal chats with Ryan Greenblatt, The Wall Street Journal says. He says OpenAI’s claim that he leaked its secrets is false.

“I was told that there was a breach of trust because I interacted with a third party through unsanctioned channels,” Tomek Korbak said.

Korbak is one of three OpenAI safety researchers fired last month. He spoke to The Wall Street Journal for a story by Maxwell Zeff, Keach Hagey and Berber Jin, published on Thursday.

OpenAI’s security team asked the three to open up their personal phones during a leak investigation, the Journal reported. Investigators named AI safety researcher Ryan Greenblatt as the suspected leaker. He denies it. TNW has not independently verified the account.

The leak inquiry followed a 1 September story in The Information. It said Astra, OpenAI’s latest model, used a new technique that made it more powerful but showed less of its thinking. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki called the reporting “confused”, and the claim set off a wider debate over AI reasoning.

On the phones, security officers saw that some of the researchers had turned on disappearing messages in their Signal chats with Greenblatt. A week later, OpenAI called the three into meetings at the same time, 2pm, and fired them. The Journal first reported the firings on 1 October.

The three are Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang. Balesni and Korbak say OpenAI leaders cited the disappearing messages as a reason. All three say they never shared confidential information outside the company.

OpenAI told the Journal that an internal investigation found the three had broken its policies on sensitive information.

The firings were “not for raising safety concerns or speaking out,” the company said.

A spokeswoman said using Signal was not approved, but it was not the only reason. Korbak called disappearing messages a “basic security best practice” in Silicon Valley.

Wang said she was told she was fired for repeatedly opening an executive’s email. She said she had asked OpenAI to remove that access nearly a year earlier. She opened one sensitive email by mistake last month and reported it at once, she said. OpenAI said it had no problem with the access itself.

Greenblatt was chief scientist at Redwood Research and now works at METR. METR had contracted him to help examine logs from the Hugging Face incident, when OpenAI’s agents went rogue. He told the Journal that he takes confidentiality seriously and never shares such information with reporters. After the story ran, he went further.

“Based on this article, OpenAI has made false accusations about me,” Greenblatt wrote on X.

The three researchers were told he had helped leak architecture details to The Information, he wrote, and that is false. A journalist from The Information did contact him before its story ran, he said, but he did not comment beyond one request about a blog post of his.

He wrote that he had heard OpenAI also accused him of reading an internal memo with sensitive architecture details, which he denies. He said he saw only a version without sensitive information that was approved for sharing with him. He believes OpenAI now knows it mixed up the two documents.

The Signal chats were with professional contacts, Greenblatt said, and Signal use is common in tech.

The three researchers had spent the summer pushing to keep AI reasoning visible. Their focus was the “chain-of-thought”, the written scratchpad a model uses to work through a problem. In an October letter to OpenAI’s board, they urged the company to work with outside safety groups.

“Best explanation I have for our firing: talking to auditors was too much safety, disappearing messages were too much security, and we should have paced ourselves,” Korbak wrote on X.

#1610. What Type of Mattress Is Right for You? (2026)

No two mattresses are the same, and what’s happening beneath the sheets has a lot to do with why. Memory foam contours to your body, latex has a springier feel, and hybrid mattresses combine foam or latex layers with a supportive coil system for the best of both worlds.

As a product reviewer who has been testing mattresses and writing about sleep products since 2022, I’ve spent the past four years getting up close and personal with mattress construction. I’ve learned how everything from the fabric covering the mattress to a few extra inches of foam can change the feel of an entire bed. I’ve also spent a fair amount of time lying down in the name of research, which is a pretty great job perk.

Consider this a primer on the five main types of mattresses—hybrid, innerspring, memory foam, latex, and even smart beds—so you can figure out which one best suits your sleep style and gives you the best excuse to hit snooze. To help us explain these materials even more, we asked John Merwin, CEO of 3Z Brands (parent company of Helix, Birch, Bear, Nolah, and Leesa) and founder of Brooklyn Bedding, to weigh in as well. —Nicole Kinning

Updated October 2026: We've rewritten portions of this guide, added a fresh introduction, updated FAQs on mattress buying, and ensured up-to-date links and prices.

Knowing your memory foam from your latex is a good start, but how do you actually put that knowledge into practice and choose a bed you’ll be excited to climb into every night? A few factors can help narrow down your options:

Feel preference: Do you like sinking into your mattress or feeling like you’re sleeping on top of it? Memory foam tends to closely contour to your body, and latex feels springier and makes it easier to change positions. Hybrids can go either way, depending on comfort layers and coil system.

Sleeping position: Side sleepers typically benefit from mattresses that have enough cushioning to relieve pressure around the shoulders and hips. Back sleepers usually need a balance of cushioning and support, while stomach sleepers often prefer a firmer surface that keeps their midsection from sinking into the bed.

How hot you sleep: If you’re a sweaty sleeper, consider a latex or hybrid mattress. Both offer more airflow than memory foam, which tends to trap heat. That said, cooling features can help, too; some memory foam mattresses use gel infusions or phase-change fabrics that are designed to pull heat away from the body.

Bed sharing: If you or your sleeping partner toss and turn, proper motion isolation should be at the top of your wish list. Memory foam absorbs movement well, so sleeping partners won’t disturb each other. Pocketed coils have a bit more bounce without wiggling the entire mattress. Innerspring, on the other hand, tends to be the bounciest of the bunch. And then there’s firmness: If you and your partner disagree on this crucial mattress characteristic, a split or adjustable mattress may work best.

Traditional coil mattresses have evolved into hybrids, which is what you will likely encounter on today’s mattress market (aside from latex and foam options). Hybrid mattresses are highly customizable, allowing you to pick and choose what you want in your bed, whether that’s a combination of foam and coils, latex and coils, or even a mix of the three. Many mattress reviews will use the phrase “best of both worlds” to describe hybrids, as this mattress lets you sample the design aspects of the materials involved. You can get the pressure relief you need from memory foam or latex on top, but ample support from the coil core (not to mention the aforementioned responsiveness from coil inclusion).

Speaking of coils and harking back to traditional mattress design, the type of coil used in hybrid mattresses matters too, as it improves upon the design of the Bonnell coil. The keywords to look for are “pocketed coils” or “individually wrapped coils,” which make up the main support mechanism of a mattress. Instead of being interconnected, each coil stays in its own lane (pocket, really) and will compress uninhibited by the other coils around it. In other words, where there is more weight, the bed will feel firmer.

This is especially applicable for areas where body weight is centralized when you’re lying down, particularly around the hips and shoulders. Pocketed coils are also meant to outperform traditional ones in the motion isolation arena, meaning that when you move around, the individual coil takes the heat motion-wise and stops the bed from reenacting a small earthquake. With the interconnectivity of traditional coils, it doesn’t take much for the feeling of movement to travel along the entire surface of the bed.

Fun fact: You do not need a box spring with a hybrid mattress. This is a recurring question I see, and pocketed coils in hybrids eliminate the need for one. Box springs were designed to give more support and elevate performance to traditional coil beds, which would align certain sections of coils to yield more support. Nowadays, you may actually void your warranty if you use a box spring with a hybrid mattress—be sure to check that fine print.

Our favorite hybrid we've tested is the Helix Midnight Luxe, which happens to be our top overall pick in our Best Mattresses guide. It also utilizes pressure-relieving foams and pocketed coils to provide targeted lumbar support.

Another awesome hybrid mattress, the Nolah Evolution, seamlessly puts together some superb memory foam and pocketed coils, both of which counteract tension buildup for pressure points. I love it as an option for side sleepers for this reason, but it also works for back and stomach sleepers. The coils also make it easy to move around on, which is helpful for co-sleepers.

This is as much a history lesson as a type of mattress you want to shop for. “Traditional” innerspring mattresses are the firm-feeling beds you probably have early memories of, given that the makeup is just innerspring coils wrapped in a cover. It’s just as squeaky as you probably remember, too, as the coil type used involves connecting every coil so they work as one unit.

These are Bonnell coils, and they connect both laterally and vertically. When you lie down, and your weight is pressed into the mattress, all those coils act as “one for all and all for one” and simultaneously compress. It’s not going to work wonders for pressure relief like modern hybrids will, but it is highly responsive, which means it’ll pop back into its original shape as you move around. So if you’re a sleeper who moves around between sleeping positions, coils are a good feature to have, so you’re getting near-instantaneous support.

Why aren't traditional coils in the works with mattresses nowadays? According to Merwin, upgraded pocketed coils far outperform traditional ones. “Unlike traditional coils that are wired together and move as a unit, pocketed coils are encased individually, which allows each coil to respond independently to movement and pressure," he said. "This design minimizes motion transfer, so when one person moves or gets out of bed, their partner feels far less disruption. It also provides more targeted support, contouring to the body in a way that improves spinal alignment and overall comfort.” While you can still find innerspring mattresses with traditional coils out there, they're typically getting phased out in favor of hybrids. I've only tested a handful of these over the years.

The Saatva Classic is one of the rare modern beds that rely on traditional coils in the secondary coil layer on the very bottom of the bed. This is to help with overall durability and support, which WIRED tester Nena Farrell has mentioned could be beneficial for those with back pain.

We actually have NASA to thank for this one. If you Google search images of those early space missions, it’s no wonder that something was needed to keep astronauts from being jostled around inside rockets; hence, memory foam was born. It’s evolved quite a bit from those initial iterations, especially when Tempur-Pedic came onto the scene in the ’80s with a vision for it to become the basis of sleep products. Today, memory foam stands in a league of its own when it comes to the unique, compressive feel.

Memory foam is an umbrella term for all the iterations mattress brands have come up with today, but let's refer to the mattress manufacturing expert for this one.

"Memory foam is produced by combining liquid polyurethane with various chemicals, like polyols and isocyanates, to enhance its viscosity and density," says Merwin. Without whipping out the periodic table of elements and giving you a science lesson in the chemical components of this material, here’s the need-to-know: These compounds form a malleable foam that holds teeny-tiny bubbles within it. How it works is, as you lie on it, your body imparts heat onto the surface, flowing into these air pockets, which in turn allows it to become softer to contour around your body.

“The key ingredient is a polymer that creates an open-cell structure, which allows the material to respond to heat and pressure. This is what gives memory foam its signature contouring or ‘hugging’ feel," Merwin says. "During the manufacturing process, the liquid foam mixture is poured onto a moving line, where it expands and solidifies into large foam blocks, which we call ‘buns.' At a high level, it’s a blend of chemistry and precision that transforms raw materials into a high-quality product.”

To say it's a natural fit for those who need pressure relief from their mattress may be a bit too on-the-nose, but it's the standout feature of this material. It also lessens the feeling of movement for the most restless of sleepers, which also makes it an attractive option for couples. Arguably, the biggest issue that plagues memory foam is heat retention, since once the heat enters these air chambers, it turns into something akin to microscopic saunas that can make the overall mattress into a nightmare for those who sleep warm. This is where the various iterations of foam come into play, as some brands infuse elements like graphite, copper, gel, or more advanced cooling technology like phase-change materials into the foams that they use.

The same stuff that goes into tires, balloons, and sports equipment can also be used for mattresses—and it’s the good stuff, too. Latex acts similarly to memory foam when it comes to pressure relief, but is far more reactive to you moving around. Think of a rubber band snapping back into shape when released; it’s pretty dang close to that. Many brands rely on organic rubber sap to create latex as we know it, and there are two categories of latex foam you’ll usually find with mattresses: Dunlop and Talalay. These names refer to the production process and resulting product, as liquid rubber is poured into molds, torched at high temperatures (called “vulcanization” and yes, I have to resist doing Spock’s “live long and prosper” hand signal every time), and flash-frozen to retain the feel. Dunlop is a firmer latex foam, and hence, has a firmer feel. Talalay yields a more plush feel. Latex is generally pretty even-keeled when it comes to temperature, too, keeping heat from accumulating within your mattress to maintain a nice, room-temperature feel.

WIRED reviewer Scott Gilbertson has applauded the organic latex construction of the Birch Luxe Natural, citing great support for the lower back region and good temperature regulation.

Smart mattresses offer the ability to adjust their firmness level with the press of a button. They do this via internal air chambers or sensors that adjust coil firmness. To make your bed softer or firmer, you use a remote control or the brand's accompanying app.

That's not the only thing that sets this mattress type apart. Some are also outfitted with tech features such as sleep trackers and massage settings, as in the case of the Bryte Balance Pro. Smart beds are becoming more refined as sleep tech evolves.

Smart beds often employ adjustable bases, which allow the head and foot of the bed to move up and down. This is an important consideration in the purchase—you should budget for the cost of the base, as this type of bed can't inherently achieve this level of flexibility on its own.

Is It Better to Get a Boxed Mattress or a Fully Set-Up Mattress?

One of the biggest arguments I see in the mattress world isn't what the best type of mattress material is—it's how the mattress is delivered to you. Some will argue that mattresses compressed into boxes compromise the materials' long-term durability and performance. The thought is that you won't get as much support because of this, which is why some brands insist on bringing a fully inflated, set-up mattress to you via white-glove delivery.

On the other hand, manufacturers that specialize in boxed mattresses will tell you that these materials are resilient and compressing them doesn't damage them. As long as you set the bed up within the prescribed delivery window (usually within 30 days of delivery), the mattress itself inflates and operates just fine.

My take is that one is not better than the other; it simply comes down to the manufacturer. Some brands, like Saatva or certain Tempur-Pedic models, shouldn't be compressed because of how the materials were developed. Hence, they will be delivered ready to go upon arrival. 3Z's brands, including Nolah, Leesa, and Helix, can be compressed because their foam and coil components can withstand this pressure. The beds are compressed, boxed, and promptly shipped out within a few days. Upon arrival, they should be unboxed immediately, but it's not the end of the world if you don't get to it right away. Make sure not to exceed the maximum time range (usually a month), and let the bed inflate for at least 24 hours after unboxing. This will allow the materials time to “breathe” as they inflate, ensuring they reach their full height and support potential.

When a boxed mattress is first opened, there may be a noticeable chemical odor. What you’re smelling are volatile organic compounds (VOCs) coming from the layers of foam, especially memory foam, and from the adhesives used to glue the layers together. You’ll want to look for certifications like CertiPUR-US and GreenGuard Gold to ensure that emission levels are low and that they don’t contain noxious chemicals like formaldehyde. Even then, it’s a good idea to air out the mattress for a day or two in a well-ventilated room before you sleep on it. If a bed contains memory foam and is not CertiPur-US certified, you don’t want it in your home.

How Long Does It Take a Mattress to Expand After Unboxing?

This depends on the mattress’s construction and unboxing instructions. Many mattresses can be used shortly after unboxing, while others can take up to a few hours or days to fully expand.

Most modern mattresses, including hybrid and memory foam mattresses, are designed to sit on a solid platform bed, slatted bed frame, or adjustable base. A traditional box spring is meant for older innerspring mattresses and has actual springs inside it. Always check your mattress manufacturer’s requirements, because using the wrong foundation can affect the mattress’s performance or warranty.

With the right care, a mattress typically lasts around seven to 10 years. Lifespan can depend on its construction, materials, and how much use it gets.

How Do I Know When It’s Time to Replace My Mattress?

If you notice visible sagging, lumps, or permanent indentations in a mattress, it may be time for a new one. Or if you find that you’re waking up with new aches and pains, these could be signs your mattress is wearing down and it’s time to get a replacement.

What’s the Best Type of Mattress for Couples With Different Preferences?

Some mattresses offer split firmness, allowing each side of the bed to have a different feel, while adjustable smart beds allow sleepers to customize their firmness independently. Mattresses with good movement isolation are also great if one partner moves around throughout the night.

What’s a Mattress Topper, and When Should I Use One?

A mattress topper is a thick layer of cushioning you can put on top of a mattress to make it softer or firmer. It's worth considering if your mattress is still in good condition but needs a little adjustment—check out our guide for recommendations.

How Often Should I Flip or Rotate My Mattress?

It depends on your mattress. Many modern mattresses actually shouldn’t be flipped because they’re designed with specific layers that need to stay in a particular order. Rotating a mattress 180 degrees from head to foot can help distribute wear, but not every mattress needs it. Check the manufacturer’s instructions for a suggested rotation and flipping schedule.

Do All Mattresses Work With an Adjustable Bed Frame?

No. A mattress needs to be flexible enough to bend safely with an adjustable base, and not every model is designed for that. Check the manufacturer’s guidelines before using a mattress on an adjustable base.

#1629. 8 Things to Know About the iPhone Duo

The iPhone Duo is finally here. Apple's first folding smartphone arrives seven years after Samsung pioneered the category with the Galaxy Fold. That gave Apple plenty of time to refine the hardware behind closed doors rather than in public view—and the results show: The Duo is a pretty dang good foldable.

Whether your Duo just landed on your doorstep or you're still weighing that $1,999 price, here's what you need to know before unfolding it.

The iPhone Duo is a new shape and size for Apple, so many of your apps likely aren't optimized to show up properly on the unique 5.4-inch outer screen or the 7.6-inch inner display. Apple also changed how navigational elements appear on the Duo, aligning them on the right rail instead of centering them on the bottom like every other iPhone.

Apple apps, redesigned for the Duo, look great. Many third-party apps look like the Google Home app in the picture above: a strip down the middle with thick, black bars on the sides. Several major app developers have already optimized their apps for iOS 27.1—Slack, Instagram, DoorDash, and WhatsApp, for example—but I've noticed that even some optimized apps like Instagram can still have broken user-interface elements. It will take time for every app in your library to work perfectly across these new screens.

It might be a good thing that the Duo is back-ordered for several weeks. By the time you snag yours, app developers will likely have caught up to the new device, and you might enjoy a richer folding experience.

Nearly a decade ago, Apple introduced Face ID as the future of the iPhone, eventually replacing the fingerprint-based Touch ID. So it might be confusing to hear that Apple's first folding phone doesn't have Face ID at all and instead relies on the older biometric authentication method. (Likely because Face ID tech takes up too much space in the thin iPhone Duo.) Touch ID never disappeared—Apple has used it on the iPhone SE and still features it on iPads and MacBooks—but this is the first time it has returned on a flagship iPhone.

Using your thumb again will take some getting used to, but it's not a deal-breaker. Some folks will miss the convenience and speed of Face ID, where all you needed to do was stare at the screen, instead of now reaching for the Touch ID sensor on the right side. Others who hated facial authentication might actually like the return of the fingerprint sensor.

Apple also introduced the Dynamic Island a few years ago—the pill-shaped island around the Face ID camera sensors that shows live app activities, like the next turn on your navigation or music currently playing, giving a second job to the camera cutout. Without Face ID, Dynamic Island hasn't disappeared from the iPhone Duo. Instead, Apple moved it to the top-right edge around the outer selfie camera; on the inner display, it appears at the top-right edge when a live activity is in progress.

The iPhone Duo supports MagSafe just like the rest of the iPhone lineup, so you can magnetically stick your favorite phone grip on the back, or attach the phone to a magnetic car dock, bike mount, or wireless charger. One thing to note: If you unfold the Duo and attach it to a car dock or tripod, the phone will tip to one side because the magnet can’t keep it held straight given the uneven weight distribution. Third-party accessory makers could address this with anti-rotation magnets, but it might mean upgrading your current accessories.

Wallets, unfortunately, are the one accessory that won't really play well with the Duo. Yes, you can still attach a MagSafe wallet, but because the Duo is shorter than other iPhones, the wallet sticks out awkwardly past the lower edge. Apple doesn't have an official solution, so if you want to use a magnetic wallet with your iPhone, you'll have to look at third-party accessories.

Everyone knows the rear cameras on your smartphone are better than the selfie camera on the front. One of the best features on a folding phone is the ability to take a selfie with those superior rear cameras and still be able to see a preview with the outer screen.

Don't confuse this with “Duo Preview” as I did, though. That's a feature that lets you show the person you're photographing the camera's viewfinder, so they can make sure they look picture-perfect before you tap the shutter. The setting to enable this is on the left edge of the camera app, where you can also turn on “Kids Cue” to have Snoopy animations play on the outer screen to get the attention of a kid for the perfect photo.

But if you're taking a selfie yourself and want the camera controls on the front screen, all you need to do is launch the camera and flip the phone around—you'll see a slider that says, “Slide to show camera.” Slide it, and the full camera app will show up on the outer screen, so you can switch modes and access Photographic Styles right there and enjoy the higher-quality results from the superior cameras. Doing this also crucially disables the inner screen, so your fingers won't accidentally switch modes.

Filling the inner 7.6-inch screen with two different apps running side by side is incredibly handy and one of the cornerstone features of any folding phone. Whether you're on a phone call and want to pull up a confirmation email, or want to browse two different Safari webpages, you can drag an app from the bottom and pull it to the left or right side to enter the split-screen state.

One trick that isn't immediately obvious is that you can create app pairs. Just tap the middle bar between the two apps once, and it'll glow white, meaning that those two apps are now locked and can be accessed at any moment from the Recent apps menu. You can't save that as an app icon combo, though. This is still very handy if you know you'll always want to open two apps simultaneously on the big screen.

I get it, you're going to want to protect the fragile screens of your $1,999 iPhone. A case is smart—folding phone cases aren't like normal cases, as they usually come in two shells that attach to each side of the phone. You can also totally add a screen protector to the outer 5.4-inch screen.

But for the 7.6-inch matte inner screen, exercise some caution. You might see chatter online that you can “remove” the Duo's nano-texture finish, but it's an “integral” component of the inner display—as Apple says on this support page. “Don't attempt to remove it,” Apple writes. (Apple also has a page on cleaning the nano-texture display properly here.) Attempting to remove the finish is likely a surefire way to void your warranty. Apple doesn't say anything about using third-party screen protectors, but using one could compromise the display performance.

If you scratch or damage the nano-texture finish, Apple says you can take it to any Apple Store or an Apple Authorized Service Provider and have it replaced for $19 (or $49 if the phone is out of warranty). That's not the same as if there's an actual crack in the inner display—that'll run you a cool $999 if you don't have AppleCare+.

The iPhone Duo is a wide phone. If you've complained about the size of modern smartphones and how it's hard to reach the top of the screen, well, you might still have some issues reaching the sides of the Duo. There's some good news, though. Apple lets you split the keyboard to make typing on the inner display a little easier.

Later this year, Apple will also issue a software update bringing Reachability to the Duo. On other iPhones, this brings the top of an app down, making it easier to reach. On the Duo, it will move an app to the left or right so you won't have to stretch those fingers as much.

Have you always wanted to use a stylus on an iPhone? Soon! Steve Jobs is probably rolling in his grave.

Apple will bring support for the USB-C Apple Pencil (and only that Apple Pencil model, not others) in a software update later this year. Paired with the 7.6-inch screen's nano-texture finish, it should make writing and drawing on the iPhone Duo feel paper-like. Sadly, there's no place to store the stylus on the iPhone like you can on an iPad. I'm sure someone will make an accessory for it.

But many of their often-overlooked cousins have a surprisingly impressive nutritional profile.

From bright orange butternut to pasta-like spaghetti squash, winter squashes offer a wide variety of vitamins, minerals, fiber and healthy plant compounds.

And as flu season gets underway, they may even have protective antioxidants that could ward off pesky winter bugs.

As a registered dietitian, I'm constantly recommending my patients add more squash to their sheet-pan dinners, curries, bowls and pastas.

While there is no single healthiest squash for everyone, certain varieties stand out for my clients and on my grocery list.

Below, I have ranked six of my favorites based on their nutritional strengths, versatility and benefits for different health goals.

Squash is packed with vital nutrients that immune, heart and digestive health

Delicata squash earns its place on this list because sometimes the healthiest vegetable is the one you'll actually make and eat.

Unlike butternut squash, which usually needs peeling, delicata has thin, edible skin that softens when cooked. That means less prep time and less food waste.

Delicata also contains fiber and potassium, which support digestion, fullness and healthy muscle function. Its slightly sweet, nutty flavor also makes it particularly appealing to people who aren't usually excited about eating their vegetables.

My favorite way to eat it is simple: slice it into half moons, toss with olive oil and roast until golden.

Add to salads or serve alongside salmon or chicken - no peeling required.

Spaghetti squash can serve as a replacement for traditional pasta. Consider pairing it with lean protein for added balance

Spaghetti squash has a unique advantage: When cooked, its flesh separates into thin strands resembling noodles.

That makes it a practical option for people looking to cut calories or carbohydrates - or simply add more vegetables to comforting fall meals.

One cup of plain cooked spaghetti squash has about 42 calories, 10g of carbohydrates and 2g of fiber. For comparison, a cup of cooked traditional pasta generally contains around 200 calories and more than 40g of carbs.

Despite the name, spaghetti squash isn't nutritionally equivalent to pasta, so what you serve with it matters when rounding out a meal. Adding chicken, turkey, shrimp, tofu or beans can make it more balanced.

And you don’t have to give up pasta altogether. Mixing spaghetti squash with regular spaghetti is another easy way to increase your vegetable intake and get used to the differences in texture and taste.

I like to roast the squash halves until tender and then use a fork to separate the strands. Toss with marinara, sautéed vegetables and your favorite protein for a lighter take on spaghetti.

Sometimes called Japanese pumpkin, kabocha has a dense, creamy texture and a naturally sweet flavor similar to a sweet potato.

Its standout nutritional feature is its carotenoid content. These plant pigments, also found in carrots and sweet potatoes, include beta-carotene, which the body converts into vitamin A, lutein and zeaxanthin.

These nutrients are concentrated in the retina and help support low-light vision and protect against degeneration.

A recent study published in HortScience found that certain kabocha varieties contained substantial amounts of carotenoids, although exact amounts varied depending on the variety, maturity and storage conditions.

Kabocha also provides fiber and carbohydrates, making it a satisfying side dish, particularly when paired with a source of protein.

Consider cutting it into wedges and roasting with olive oil and a little salt. Like delicata, the skin also softens when cooked and can be eaten.

When shopping for canned pumpkin, choose plain varieties to avoid excess amounts of added sugar

Pumpkin might be the most recognizable winter squash, and it isn’t just good for making pies.

Like butternut squash, pumpkin gets its orange color from beta-carotene, which the body converts into vitamin A to support vision, as well as immune and skin health.

Vitamin A helps maintain protective mucosal barriers in the respiratory and digestive tracks, the body's first line of defense against the flu and common cold.

It also comes in a can, which can be far cheaper and more shelf stable than fresh produce.

One cup of plain canned pumpkin contains about 83 calories, 7g of fiber and 500mg of potassium. It also provides immune-boosting iron and vitamin C.

Unlike many winter squashes that require peeling, chopping and roasting, canned pumpkin is ready to use straight from the pantry and is incredibly versatile, making it great for both sweet and savory recipes.

Stir plain pumpkin puree into oatmeal, blend it into smoothies or add it to soups, chilis and sauces for extra fiber and nutrients. Just make sure to choose 100 percent pumpkin puree and not pumpkin pie filling to cut down on added sugar.

Butternut squash can easily be blended into a soup or cubed with olive oil, cinnamon and rosemary

Butternut squash is particularly rich in beta-carotene, the pigment responsible for its bright orange color.

One cup of baked butternut squash contains about 82 calories, nearly 7g of fiber and about 580mg of potassium.

Butternut squash also provides about 34 percent of the daily recommended value of vitamin C, which helps produce disease-fighting white blood cells and reduces inflammation during infections.

That's an impressive nutritional package for relatively few calories. The fiber supports digestion and fullness, and potassium plays an important role in maintaining healthy blood pressure.

Butternut squash has a naturally sweet, slightly nutty flavor, making it an appealing way to add more vegetables to meals, even for picky eaters.

Consider roasting cubed butternut squash with olive oil, cinnamon and rosemary, then add it to grain bowls or salads. It also blends nicely into creamy soups without needing much cream.

One cup of baked acorn squash contains more than twice the potassium of a medium banana, which supports heart health

Acorn squash takes my top spot thanks to its impressive fiber and potassium content, making it a standout choice for digestive and heart health.

One cup of baked acorn squash contains around 9g of fiber and nearly 900mg of potassium. For perspective, that's about one-third of the recommended 28g daily fiber intake and more than twice the amount of potassium in a medium banana.

Fiber supports digestion and regular bowel movements, helps lower cholesterol and promotes fullness, while potassium helps counteract sodium's effects on blood pressure.

Acorn squash also provides about 21 percent of the daily value for magnesium and 24 percent for vitamin B6 in just one cup. Both support important functions such as muscle health and energy metabolism.

Acorn squash contains more carbs than some other winter varieties, but those carbs come packaged with fiber and beneficial nutrients.

I like to slice acorn squash in half, scoop out the seeds and roast it until tender. Then, stuff the center with quinoa, lentils, vegetables or ground turkey for a balanced and delicious meal.

The answer depends on your nutritional goals and preferences.

Acorn squash takes the top spot for its high fiber and potassium content, while butternut stands out for vitamin A and other essential nutrients.

Kabocha offers an impressive range of carotenoids, and canned pumpkin is an affordable, convenient source of beta-carotene. For lighter, lower-carbohydrate meals, spaghetti squash is a great option, and delicata makes prep quick and easy.

Some winter squash varieties are particularly rich in potassium, so people with kidney disease who need to limit potassium should pay attention to portions.

Ultimately, I wouldn't choose just one favorite. Diversifying the winter squashes you eat is a great way to enjoy a wider range of nutrients, flavors and textures while making healthy eating more interesting and enjoyable.

#1638. Three things you should know about permafrost

A primer on this crucial and frankly mysterious piece of climate science.

Maybe, like me, you’ve been aware that this layer of frozen soil is thawing, that it’s important in the global puzzle of climate change … and that’s about the extent of it.

The great news for both of us is that my colleague James Temple just published two in-depth stories about permafrost. His reporting took him to the boreal forests of Alaska, where he helicoptered into the wilderness to learn more about how this material could affect our climate both now and in the future.

I’d recommend you give both of James’s stories a read. There’s this feature about efforts to tackle the intensifying fires that are threatening permafrost, as well as this look at how researchers are trying to understand the complicated tangle of potential climate effects. Here are three things I took away.

Permafrost is the frozen layer of soil that’s under about 15% of land around the Northern Hemisphere. While technically material needs to be frozen for only two consecutive years to count as permafrost, most of our current permafrost formed during the last ice age.

That means there’s organic material that’s been frozen, and essentially locked away, for thousands of years. But as global temperatures tick up and this layer thaws, microbes are able to break down that organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide and methane—powerful greenhouse gases that drive climate change. Those emissions form a feedback loop, accelerating warming further.

The steady rise in global temperatures has already shrunk the footprint of the Arctic permafrost by about 12% since 1850, and the pace has been picking up in the last 50 years.

The wild thing is, only a minority of the models that have been used in UN climate reports actually take into account the gradual loss of permafrost. And none of them include the abrupt thaw and collapse that can happen after events like heat waves and fires (more on those fires in a moment). There are efforts to make sure future UN reports reflect the growing science, but for now, all this means that we’re probably underestimating how much and how fast the planet is going to warm.

Fire is a natural part of boreal forests’ life cycle, but they’re getting more intense because of climate change. And crucially, fires can accelerate the thawing of permafrost. By the end of the century, this effect could release the equivalent of 80 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide.

Today, the policy across remote parts of Canada and Alaska is generally to let wildfires burn as long as they’re not threatening human habitats. But some researchers say we should explore fighting these fires to save the carbon trapped in the forests and the permafrost beneath them.

Researchers at the Woodwell Climate Research Center are developing a map of burn risk and carbon vulnerability across Alaska, essentially highlighting areas where fires are most likely to spread and which areas are home to certain types of permafrost that would release the most emissions. (Yes, apparently there are different types of permafrost. Yedoma is a particularly deep, carbon-dense form from the late Pleistocene era.)

That’s all just the beginning—once we figure out potential targets, the question is what to do about it. Some suggest setting more prescribed fires, something Indigenous groups have done for thousands of years, partly to reduce the danger of massive blazes. Or resources could go to early detection and efforts to put out fires before they grow.

There’s about 1.5 trillion tons of carbon locked away in permafrost, about twice the amount in the atmosphere. We know that permafrost thawing can release greenhouse-gas emissions and drive climate change. But there are complications.

One particularly big wrinkle: Other effects from permafrost thawing and fire recovery might counteract the carbon lost from those events. For example, as temperatures warm, plants can gradually move north and take hold where they couldn’t have before. Those plants soak up carbon from the atmosphere as they grow, at least partly locking away what was emitted.

Researchers are working to better understand this ecosystem. And the more we learn, the more complicated the picture looks. How much carbon is lost from permafrost, and how much a given area will recover its carbon, depend on a huge array of factors. How severe are the fires that have accelerated local thawing? What’s the local climate and environment? And what kind of permafrost is at stake?

The higher we push the planet’s temperature, the more we mess with complicated natural phenomena that we’re only beginning to understand.

#1646. AI’s hidden storage bill: What inference leaves behind

Enterprises need economically scalable capacity to keep pace with the data created by AI inference.

Ask most executives to picture the cost of AI and they’ll likely see GPUs—racks of accelerators with gigawatts of power—and a waitlist for silicon. But that picture leaves something out: Data storage costs.

What happens to data after a model responds? Much of the back-end data that’s generated—from billions of daily prompts, images, and videos—needs to be stored. But data storage is one of the harder infrastructure costs to plan for, and it’s often overlooked in initial AI infrastructure planning.

Traditionally, AI infrastructure conversations have centered on the compute-intensive process of training a model. Training is finite: a run starts, burns through compute, and ends. But inference runs continuously, every time a model answers a prompt. For infrastructure planning, that difference matters: A training run is a project you can budget for and retire. Inference doesn’t retire; every inference request leaves something behind.

Take AI-generated video. In this workload, the back-end spend can be significant. From internal measurements, WD found that an 8-second clip (returned to a user as a 3.97MB 720p file) left more than 500MB of associated data behind on the back end, including logs, cached context, immediate renders, backups, and safety records. That’s a 130x increase over the output file size the user saw. Scale this to an enterprise generating longer, higher resolution video and the back-end footprint grows into the hundreds of gigabytes per clip.

And that back-end footprint is growing fast. McKinsey projects AI inference demand will grow at a 35% CAGR through 2030, versus 22% for training, making up more than 40% of total data center demand by the end of the decade.

A recent WD-sponsored IDC global white paper that surveyed 763 IT and business decision makers found that nearly 95% of respondents reported an increase in data volume due to generative AI over the past 12 months, with roughly 61% reporting growth of 25% or more.

“You can think of having more storage as an optimization for keeping the GPUs busy,” said a senior product manager of a hyperscale cloud service provider quoted in report. “The biggest challenge is to ensure that the GPUs are not idle, waiting for data to show up—hours of GPU idle time becomes very expensive, very fast.” Put in dollar terms: if your storage spend is growing 25% year over year while your compute budget grows in the more predictable cyclical way, storage ends up outpacing the budget line that receives the most attention.

Compute demand rises and falls with training cycles, product launches, and hardware refreshes. But the volume of retained data doesn't fall back down with it when compute demand slows. Model logs, synthetic outputs, inference results, and more keep accumulating, and organizations are holding onto more of it for longer.

This changes the role of retained data. Information that once moved into an archive and rarely surfaced can now become an input to future AI workloads. As more data is retained, retrieved, and reused, storage must support not only growing capacity, but increasingly dynamic access to it. The same WD-IDC survey found 96% of respondents anticipate needing faster archive retrieval to support inference or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).

AI workloads require both performance and capacity, with different technologies serving different needs across the data lifecycle. And for the vast volumes of data that must be retained and revisited, HDDs provide a cost-effective, high-capacity foundation, with a significant cost-per-terabyte advantage

For a CFO or CIO, this means “AI infrastructure spend” may be scoped too narrowly on compute. A model that idles while data loads costs about as much as one idling for lack of a GPU. But the storage bottleneck stays hidden until it’s already a problem, surfacing in operating costs and retrieval delays rather than a CapEx line next to the accelerators.

Consider a retailer generating AI product videos for its catalog, or an insurer running claims documents through a RAG pipeline for faster underwriting. In both cases, the compute cost is a known, budgeted quantity: a per-generation or per-query rate that finance can forecast. The storage cost is different: It compounds with every video generated and every claim processed, because none of that back-end data disappears once the task is finished.

A team that budgets AI as “compute plus a rounding error for storage” may find, in 12 to 18 months, that the rounding error has become a significant fraction of the program’s total cost—right around the time leadership is deciding whether to expand.

Addressing this challenge starts with architecture. Storage strategy tends to be designed for expensive, hot-tier performance (for data accessed instantly and constantly), while most AI data by volume accumulates in the warm, cool, and cold tiers behind it.

What enterprises need is economically scalable capacity: Infrastructure designed for the full AI data life cycle, balancing performance with capacity, power, reliability, and total cost of ownership as data persists, compounds, and is increasingly reused. That means treating archives that once sat untouched as active assets for RAG and inference, and setting retention policies for data’s growing strategic value rather than its original storage cost. It also means recognizing that decisions about storage play out across fleets, not individual drives.

Such infrastructure requires storage tiers, where individualized power consumption, reliability, density, and operational efficiency all determine whether the system remains economically sustainable as capacity scales.

Every morning during the Martian spring and summer, a thin and extremely long cloud emerges, only to disappear again. The wispy formation appears near the volcano Arsia Mons and gradually elongates until it reaches over 1,000 miles—about the distance from New York to Kansas City as the crow flies—before dissipating. Since it was first recorded by a probe in 2018, scientists have been unable to find a plausible explanation for the existence of this cloud, often referred to as the Arsia Mons Elongated Cloud (AMEC). Recently, researchers successfully modeled the cloud’s formation for the first time, their results suggesting that it may be driven by a physical process that has never been directly observed in any planet's atmosphere.

"We found that we needed to include some exotic physics … physics that, while included in textbooks, is treated as theoretical and usually thought not to happen in nature. It certainly hasn’t been seen in action before," Jorge Hernández-Bernal, a planetary scientist at Paris’ Sorbonne Université and lead author of the study, said in a press release. "Once we included this physics in our simulations, the AMEC emerged just as we hoped.”

On Earth, clouds usually form when water vapor creates droplets or ice crystals in the presence of tiny particles, such as dust or salt. The new study, published in Nature Geoscience, instead suggests AMEC could be forming through so-called homogeneous nucleation, where vapor changes directly into ice crystals on its own without any help from debris.

“It’s akin to droplets of condensation appearing in the middle of a room, rather than on a window," Hernández-Bernal explained. “It’s wholly unexpected.”

This phenomenon requires extreme conditions—a sharp temperature drop of 30 degrees Celsius within 10 minutes and high relative humidity. The Arsia Mons volcano also plays a key role. When wind current hits the 65,000-foot-tall volcano, it creates a powerful wave that launches moist air high in the atmosphere in a matter of minutes.

Scientists have assumed a similar phenomenon could occur in the upper atmospheres of other planets, including Venus and even Earth, but it has never been observed. That’s not too surprising given the extreme air humidity required for this phenomenon, which is more than 100,000 times greater than any value measured on Earth.

Before this modeling, it was not known that these humidity levels existed on Mars either. “We don’t have nearly as much information about Mars’ atmosphere as we do about Earth's, so reproducing the AMEC to this degree is a big success,” said Hernández-Bernal, adding, “the result is remarkable.”

#1650. Is US President Donald Trump right to rename AI as 'Super Intelligence'?

Donald Trump thinks the name artificial intelligence sounds "fake" and has ordered US agencies to use "Super Intelligence" instead. Yet, tech firms and researchers have little reason to play along.

What's with Donald Trump and renaming things? First, the Gulf of Mexico became the Gulf of America — in US federal documents, if not globally. Then Lake Ontario became Lake America. Now he wants to rebrand the defining technology of our time: artificial intelligence (AI).

Earlier this month, the US president claimed on his Truth Social platform that the term artificial intelligence is "inaccurate" and "ineloquent." He said "superior," "extreme," or "supreme" intelligence were better alternatives.

In a three-day social media poll, Trump fans chose "Super Intelligence" (SI) by about 65% to 35% (for Superior Intelligence), allowing him to announce at the United Nations General Assembly last week that US federal documents would use his new term going forward.

The tech industry has yet to adopt the phrase, however, while academics warn that superintelligence is a future term for when technology can beat humans across the board. They say the current AI models are years away from that milestone.

"Most AI is not superintelligence — it's just different intelligence," Pedro Domingos, a computer science professor from the University of Washington, told DW. "AI is doing different things from what humans do, so calling it superintelligence is misleading."

Computer scientists and mathematicians coined the name "artificial intelligence" in 1956 at a summer workshop at Dartmouth College in New Hampshire. "Artificial" simply meant made by humans, as opposed to natural human intelligence.

Not all experts agreed with the name. In the book "Machines Who Think," historian Pamela McCorduck cited AI pioneer Arthur Samuel, who said the term "makes you think there's something kind of phony about this."

Despite this, the name artificial intelligence has stuck for 70 years. Critics say changing it now will not quieten the many fears around AI, including job losses, human obsolescence or machines slipping out of our control.

"The name change is fostering these kinds of fears: 'Oh, this is superintelligence and it's going to make humans obsolete, turn on us and take our jobs'," warned Domingos. "If you want to maximize the backlash against AI, rename it Superior Intelligence."

Those public fears are growing fast. In a Public First poll for Politico earlier this month, about half of respondents in six countries — the United States, Canada, France, Spain, Germany and the United Kingdom — backed a pause in further AI development to reduce the risk that the new tech could destroy humanity.

A separate CNN/SSRS poll found nearly nine in 10 Americans are more concerned than excited about AI surpassing human intelligence.

Those concerns have already reached the US Congress. Last week, Vermont's independent Senator Bernie Sanders co-introduced a bill to "permanently ban the development of artificial superintelligence" that humans cannot control.

The legislation also calls for a pause on advanced AI development until federal testing and oversight are in place.

Trump's new term could, however, suit the major US AI players, who are keen to play up their advanced models. Those systems already outperform humans on many specialized tasks and until recently were far ahead of China.

Yet both Anthropic, the firm behind the Claude model, and OpenAI, which created ChatGPT, have pushed back plans to go public. Some market watchers blamed the delays on recent scares where AI models hacked into computer systems or ran tasks they were not meant to.

With Chinese models closing much of the quality gap with much less computing power, US labs may still appreciate the branding that helps confirm they are still leading the AI race. Global spending on AI is expected to surpass $1 trillion (€880 billion), according to Goldman Sachs research.

"The [US] labs have every interest in calling it super, hyper, superior and whatnot intelligence, because it's playing up their capabilities. It's like just marketing," said Domingos, the author of the 2015 book "The Master Algorithm."

While the current AI models keep improving and can already run many tasks on their own, the next step that many labs discuss is AI systems that improve themselves. That's called recursive self-improvement or RSI.

On that journey, Elon Musk has predicted that AI learning could be automated, with limited human input, by the end of this year or 2027. OpenAI thinks it could take a year longer. But even then, AI systems won't be fully recursive.

Jürgen Schmidhuber, a German computer scientist based in Switzerland, argued in a blog post earlier this month that "full RSI, however, will require not just self-improving [AI] software but self-improving hardware in the physical world."

In other words, to reach superintelligence or RSI, tech companies would have to produce chips, humanoid robots and even replace entire factories without human input.

While AI is already speeding up chip design, it cannot yet run the foundry alone. Self-improving machines, with no human input, are likely still decades away.

Beyond the science of machine learning, politics will likely play a bigger role in whether Super Intelligence sticks. While Trump claimed Chinese President Xi Jinping liked the name, China has yet to show any signs of changing from AI.

At a tech summit on Tuesday with key industry leaders, Trump signed an executive order instructing US government bodies and agencies to start using the term SI. While some Big Tech leaders have adopted it, experts say the new term is unlikely to catch on.

"Knowing the political orientation of most people in the [AI] field, they're more likely to have the opposite reaction, which is: 'To hell with you. We're going to call it artificial intelligence even more than before'," Domingos said.
