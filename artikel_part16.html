#751. US south-west braces for more storms after enduring days of rain and flooding

More inclement weather forecast in rain-soaked Four Corners region after evacuations and rescues last week

Another round of heavy showers and thunderstorms were expected to hit the south-west US on Sunday after the region endured days of rain that forced evacuations, water rescues and multi-million-dollar damage.

The National Weather Service issued a flood watch for much of the Four Corners – the meeting point of Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico and Utah – even as local officials forecast further receding of swollen rivers that surged since rains picked up on Wednesday.

About 4ft (1 meter) of water flooded the home of Shane Ferrari, the sheriff of San Juan county, New Mexico. He said the area’s situation was stable on Sunday, but he and other residents were trying to clean up and deal with damage.

The New Mexico governor, Michelle Lujan Grisham, said in a statement on Saturday that law enforcement had reported hundreds of water rescues between the cities of Farmington and Bloomfield.

“Families in Bloomfield and Farmington spent the night on rooftops watching the river flood their homes, and our national guard and state police crews have been bringing them to safety,” said Lujan Grisham. “Right now, our focus is saving lives. If you’re told to evacuate, go. Once the water recedes, the state will do everything in its power to help San Juan county families rebuild.”

“No deaths have been reported,” she added.

The San Juan River in Farmington on Friday ran 2 ft above its previous record set in 1909.

San Juan county spokesperson Devin Neeley said the county did not have official numbers on damage to homes or the number of people evacuated, but he was not aware of any loss of life.

Neeley said the flood waters were receding but left mud and silt, much of which has been contaminated with sewage because of failures at waste plants. There were still power outages affecting multiple areas, and it would probably be a few days until residents got their power back on.

“It’s a pretty active disaster zone,” he said. “We’re not on the downswing by any means.”

The flooding is just the latest from two months of on-and-off heavy rain caused by tropical moisture coming ashore from the Pacific. After months of drought, other recent deadly flooding struck the Grand Canyon and areas of New Mexico.

New Mexico has been hit with back-to-back weather events this weekend. A tornado struck Santa Fe on Saturday, damaging at least eight houses, including to roof collapses, according to a statement by the city of Santa Fe. No injuries were reported.

#752. ‘You can’t bring in robots’: the leather firm handmaking everything from wallets to cigar cases

In the third article in a series on high-end British manufacturing, we visit the Ettinger factory in Walsall

‘Our town is making jeans again’: reviving jobs and skills in Wales

From feathers to pet portraits, Rolls-Royce rides high on custom craze

“It is all down to attention to detail and everything we do is based around that.”

Gary Billingham is demonstrating how the 28 pieces which make up an Ettinger wallet are cut from leather using a hydraulic press by the small team at one of Walsall’s last remaining leather goods factories. The edges of the pieces are thinned down so they are ready to be turned, glued and stitched together by hand.

Billingham has been at the factory near Birmingham since the 1980s, when “there were three pages in the Yellow Pages” business directory listing leather factories in the West Midlands town. “They were everywhere. It has gone from that to about five factories. It is more like a cottage industry.”

About 20 people, mostly women, work carefully and precisely in Ettinger’s factory – a site of leather goods production since the 1890s – with a handful more doing work from home.

Robert Ettinger, the company’s chief executive, says the vast majority of its manufacturing process has to be done by hand. “You can’t bring in robots.”

He still owns the British brand founded in 1934 by his father, who turned to selling and manufacturing leather goods in the UK after fleeing Nazi Germany via Paris and a stint in the film industry.

Ettinger began making wallets and sourcing leather items, and today the company supplies a range of luxury goods to wealthy customers, including King Charles and Queen Camilla whose royal warrants they hold.

The company’s bestseller is still a wallet, but they also sell leather pill containers, cigar cases, picture frames and various accoutrements for dog owners, from treat cases to dog-waste bag holders, all of which can be personalised with initials.

The brand sits in a niche between the big label luxury and high street with a British hand-crafted leather tote bag for just under £800 compared with more than £2,000 charged by a big swinger such as Louis Vuitton.

An array of machines resembling pastry-cutters are used to punch out leather shapes that are then glued by hand with a brush to build up each item with trims and linings.

The tiny seams around the leather edges are thinned down with a shaving machine, known as a skiver, and turned inward before being stitched with a sewing machine. Each piece is then carefully checked and trimmed using a scalpel and sharp scissors before being polished and wrapped in tissue paper.

Gerry Ettinger, the founder, began by using an array of manufacturers before buying his original factory at the heart of London’s leather-working trade in Clerkenwell near the Smithfield meat market in the 1950s.

Robert Ettinger says: “When I was a kid, there were dozens and dozens of small factories, making leather goods, and they all specialised. One made suitcases, and one made handbags, and one made wallets.”

As the area gentrified in the 1990s, the Ettingers decided to move and bought out one of their suppliers, James Homer in Walsall, established in 1890. It was then mostly making corporate promotional leather goods. Staff were trained up and new machinery brought in to make the high standard of goods expected by Ettinger’s new international clientele led by the Japanese.

Walsall has been a centre of leather-working from the middle ages. It was focused on saddle-making with more than 100 firms operating in the town by the end of the 19th century.

As the use of horses in everyday life declined, the factories began expanding into making other leather goods, ranging from bicycle saddles, car upholstery and furnishings to bags, gloves and purses.

The industry thrived during the first world war when there was high demand for military saddle wear but began to suffer in the 1950s as it faced competition from abroad and from human-made alternatives to leather.

Still, by 1970 there were about 50 leather goods manufacturers in Walsall, and the town produced around half of the British-made fancy leather goods, according to the Wolverhampton Heritage and History Society.

Tracy, expertly glueing and building Ettinger’s elaborate cigar cases before they are sewn, has been at the company for 15 years and working in the leather industry since she left school 40 years ago.

“When we were that age it was all leather work. You could walk out of one job and into another on the same day,” she says.

That history is recognised in street names and the local Saddlers Centre shopping mall and there is a leather skills training centre which offers courses in bridle making.

Launer, whose customers included the late Queen Elizabeth II, J&E Sedgwick, the bridle maker, and small leather goods firm E Hulme, founded in the 1920s, continue to make leather goods near Ettinger’s factory. However, all employ only small numbers of people and the local industry is a shadow of its former self.

Even Walsall’s leather museum, housed in a former factory that dates back to the 1880s, is now closed amid disputed plans to move it to a modern city centre site.

That reflects a rapid decline in the leather goods manufacturing industry across the UK with just 7,000 people employed in it today compared with 37,000 30 years ago.

Ettinger itself had to cut its staff numbers by about seven after the Covid pandemic hit trade.

Robert Ettinger says he never really considered shifting production overseas, despite most of his customers coming from outside the UK.

“We might have made more profit, but then we would also have much more competition because when you’ve got 50 companies bringing in a purse from the far east, then it’s all about price.”

He says the gambit is now paying off as a younger generation increasingly appreciates British craft, with online sales up 30% on last year and UK sales up by a fifth, with shoppers aged from their early 20s.

“A lot of younger people are now thinking that they want something that will last, that won’t go out of fashion in three months’ time. Yes, it costs more, but it lasts for 15 or 20 years, and can be repaired. I’ve just repaired my wallet which is 17 years old.

“Our products are affordable luxury. A purse or wallet for £200 or £300, it’s the price of some trainers and they’re not made in the UK. So it’s not out of reach of most people.”

Still, expanding production in the UK might be tricky. Billingham says it is hard to find and train new staff. Even those who attend Walsall’s Leather Skills Centre are more likely to go into making car upholstery or saddles.

“We are all getting old now, that’s a problem, we are from 40s to 60s. If you find someone working for somebody else you take them,” says Billingham.

“To take five to 10 people on would be great but the reality is they are not there.”

Robert Ettinger says the UK should look at how young people are trained into the trade in mainland Europe, where he was sent by his father to serve an apprenticeship.

“They spend a few years really learning a trade, everything from making leather goods, to being a chimney sweep, or a butcher, or baker. When you pass all the apprenticeship exams, you then go on to do more and become a master, a Meister. We don’t have that here. So, it’s harder for us to take on younger people.”

He is optimistic things could change. “People might go into jobs which they don’t really enjoy or get bored of doing, and then they join us, and, you know, it’s like a family in our factory.”

#754. Football: German coach Hürzeler flying high in Premier League

Fabian Hürzeler is younger than his star player, a fellow Premier League German. But, contrary to his reputation in his homeland, Hürzeler has become a popular figure both on and off the pitch in England.

"We are absolutely in love with him. He's been an incredible manager. The results are extraordinary and we're flying."

Those words from Ben Parker will be familiar to fans of any club who have been on the kind of winning run Brighton and Hove Albion are currently on.

But all the signs on and off the pitch suggest that the man in question — Brighton's German coach Fabian Hürzeler — is more than just a flash in the Premier League pan.

Hürzeler has never coached in Germany's Bundesliga. After winning promotion to Germany's top flight with St. Pauli in 2024, he left to become the youngest man ever to take charge in England's Premier League at 31. His team currently sit third, having won six games in a row including a 5-0 thrashing of bitter rivals Crystal Palace last weekend.

It was the repeat of a fixture last season that marked Hürzeler's lowest Brighton ebb, with a 1-0 home loss making it one win in 12. "There are only two options," the German coach said at the time. "Give up or keep pushing harder, and that's always the option I choose in my life."

Parker, the co-editor of Brighton and Hove Albion fanzine, Dogma, admitted to DW that there were plenty who weren't sure if persisting was the answer. "There was some disquiet at the end of that game, there were people shouting: 'You're getting sacked in the morning.'"

While such sentiment may be difficult to swallow for many coaches, Parker said that Hürzeler told him in an interview soon after joining the club that he welcomed it when needed.

"He talked about wanting a fan relationship that had a two-way aspect. The club and him are really ambitious, so if the performances aren't up to scratch, we should be telling him that. And that's what happened on that day. I see the whole thing as being quite healthy."

Hürzeler never played at the top level. He represented Germany's youth teams and turned out for Bayern Munich's reserves but soon grew frustrated with second-team football. He then accepted a player-coach role at German fifth-tier side Pipinsried at the age of 23. From 2018, he was also an assistant coach for Germany's U20s.

He performed this alongside a job at an art dealership in the German state of Bavaria, where he moved with his family at the age of two having been born in the United States. After starting as an assistant coach at St. Pauli in 2020, he took over after head coach Timo Schultz was fired in late 2022. Since then, he hasn't looked back.

That unusual path to top-flight management and some innovative tactics at St. Pauli led to a perception in Germany that Hürzeler was a little aloof and something of a 'laptop manager' in the vein of former Bayern Munich and Germany boss, Julian Nagelsmann who had a similarly nondescript playing career. This, so the charge goes, meant Hürzeler wasn't able to forge the relationships and togetherness needed to make a team more than the sum of its parts.

That was rarely what was reported by his players and staff at St. Pauli, nor is it Hürzeler's belief. "I want to be myself, I don't want to play a role, and my age definitely helps regarding understanding the players," he said.

"I always say I speak their language and I understand their thoughts, because it wasn't that long ago when I played."

His antics on the sideline also earned him a reputation as a bit of a hothead. Hürzeler was booked seven times during St. Pauli's promotion push and since joining Brighton, the German has been booked nine times and been sent off once. When asked about this, he said: "Emotions are part of the game."

Parker said this authenticity has also been reflected in his relationship with Brighton and Hove, on the south coast of England.

"For most Brighton fans he comes across as quite a quirky, interesting, left-field figure. The mantra he set out when he first arrived was quite counter-culturalist, to challenge the establishment, and that fits well here," Parker said.

"You will see him walking along on the seafront, going up for coffees around town, and he travels back and forth on an ebike, not in a chauffer-driven car as a lot of managers might. He's also hosted fan events in pubs, which I believe he paid for. So I'd say he's very approachable."

But Hürzeler isn't the only 30-something German beloved in Brighton.

Pascal Gross, who rejoined the club from Borussia Dortmund in January, is also a firm favorite. The midfielder, who retired from Germany duty after the World Cup earlier this year, has scored three and made four in the club's opening seven league games. The 35-year-old's connection with Hürzeler is also key, with Groß admitting he stayed in touch with Hürzeler even before he resigned. The return is proving to look a great decision for both sides.

"I was here for seven years, got married here and both my kids were born in England. The most important milestones in my life happened here. I missed my friends and the feeling of being deeply connected to the club."

The feeling is mutual, said Parker. "Pascal Gross is everybody's favorite player. He's Brighton's first best signing, from Ingolstadt, and second-best signing, from Dortmund. He's a sensational player, and it's probably true to say there is a lot of warmth towards German football and Germany because of these hugely important characters at the football club."

The Premier League club's success has been built on a smart coach and player recruitment strategy guided by local owner, Tony Bloom. Part of the model is that those who shine brightest will often be snatched by the bigger fish.

"I think he's said he's a supporter of Bayern Munich, and he uses them as an example of the standards he wants," said Parker of Hürzeler. "I can see that he could be a good fit in a couple of years time. He won't go to a Liverpool or a Chelsea, I think there's a strong pull to Bayern in particular."

Bayern's current boss, Vincent Kompany, is in little danger at the moment, though Bayern coaches are only ever a few losses from crisis. For the meantime, Hürzeler is also happy where he is, having extended his contract to 2029. Again, the feeling is mutual.

Next up in the Premier League is a trip to Liverpool, where Jürgen Klopp became the first German coach to make a real mark on the Premier League. But before that, Hürzeler's team have an assignment in the Europa Conference League, a competition Brighton are among the favorites to win. Parker hopes a first major trophy is in sight.

"If he can be the person that wins us that first trophy, then he will go down as the greatest."

#757. The overlooked geography of American poverty

Data: U.S. Census; Map: Erin Davis/Axios

California and Louisiana have something surprising in common: Both have among the nation's highest poverty rates when the cost of living is factored in, according to Census Bureau data.

Why it matters: Southern states dominate America's traditional poverty rankings. But that familiar geographic divide starts to disappear when more of a family's financial realities are taken into account.

  • Axios analyzed 2023-2025 state poverty estimates from the Census Bureau to see what happens when housing, taxes, medical expenses and government benefits enter the picture.

What we found: Under the Census' traditional-style official measure, Louisiana had a 19.6% poverty rate and California was at 10.7%. New Hampshire was the lowest at 5.4%.

  • The South is home to 10 of the 11 states where the traditional poverty rate significantly outpaced the national average.
  • California's poverty rate jumped by nearly two-thirds to 17.8% under the broader Supplemental Poverty Measure, or SPM. Louisiana remained near the top at 19%.
  • Maine posted the nation's lowest SPM rate at 6.4%, followed by Nebraska at 7.5% and Minnesota at 7.9%.

How it works: The traditional measure compares pretax cash income with a family-size threshold that doesn't change by geography.

  • The SPM adds benefits such as SNAP and housing assistance, factors in taxes and tax credits, subtracts work and medical expenses, and adjusts for geographic differences in housing costs.

Zoom out: The official poverty rate fell for a third straight year to 10.2%, while child poverty reached a record-low 13.4%.

  • Yet the SPM remained statistically unchanged at 13.1% — nearly three percentage points above the official rate.
  • Real median household income hit a record $87,460 in 2025.

Yes, but: The SPM doesn't replace the official poverty rate.

  • Census designed it as another lens — one that captures resources and unavoidable expenses the traditional measure misses.
  • The state figures also aren't single-year 2025 estimates. Census recommends three-year averages because state samples in the Current Population Survey are smaller.

The bottom line: Income alone doesn't tell you whether a family can make ends meet.

Theme parks attractions are never really finished, especially those intended to be built to last. And so it is with Disneyland’s Indiana Jones Adventure, which officially reopens Friday after a six-week refurbishment. The ride is looking as fresh as it has in years. There were minor changes — more large spiders and other creepy critters were added to one foreboding tunnel — but also some substantial ones designed to increase the attraction’s capacity and reliability.

While some ride refurbishment closures result in very noticeable changes to the guest — for instance, the recent addition of a face-shifting buccaneer on Pirates of the Caribbean, or the temple-shattering projections that were added to Indiana Jones Adventure back in 2023 — other updates are less visible yet enhance the experience, such as helping to eliminate downtime.

That is the case for Indiana Jones Adventure. Behind-the-scenes engineering modifications over the last year have boosted not only the attraction’s reliability, but allowed its capacity to increase by about 125 people per hour. Ultimately, that allows for about 1,000 more guests per day, says Nicholas DiBernardo, an area manager for Attractions Engineering Services and eight-year veteran of the resort.

A yearly rehab of the ride is required, says DiBernardo, in part because it is one of the resort’s most complex, with many moving show elements. To ensure that it continues to meet the theatrical and safety standards required, it may appear to Disneyland regulars that Indiana Jones Adventure receives tune-ups with more regularity than other rides at the resort.

When asked whether that’s the reality, DiBernardo answers: “Yes and no.”

“Every attraction is different,” he says. “Indiana Jones typically has a refurbishment every year just because of the size of the attraction and the amount of work that goes into keeping this attraction up. It all depends on the needs for improving show quality or maintenance reliability.”

Two key changes were made to the attraction in 2026. The most recent — and the reason for the ride’s downtime this fall — was something of a wishlist item, and that was changing the machinations that drive its first major show scene, the doors that lead guests face-to-face with Mara, the deity within the Temple of the Forbidden Eye that drives the ride’s action. Prior to the refurbishment, the doors were controlled by hydraulic actuators. Now they’re fully electric. In simple terms, DiBernardo says the doors are now easier to maintain, there’s no longer a risk of a hydraulic spill, and they should properly open and close with significantly more dependability.

That may not sound like a sexy ride enhancement, but it’s one that DiBernardo says will ensure Indiana Jones Adventure runs more efficiently. “In the past, when the old actuators would fail, the doors would have to remain open, ruining the guest’s show experience,” he explains. Not just that, but it slowed down the ride, as fewer cars could be dispatched so riders weren’t seeing the vehicle in front of them.

Elsewhere, DiBernardo says capacity was able to be increased by improving the loading and unloading process, again a change that will be invisible to guests. Essentially, the vehicles park more accurately.

DiBernardo, who works Disneyland’s third shift from 2 a.m. to 11 a.m., says this adjustment, a mix of hardware and software changes, was made over the course of the past year. The problem, oversimplified: Vehicles did not always park in their precise loading and unloading position. Because a theme park attraction needs to operate with extreme exactness to ensure guest safety, DiBernardo says that before this fix, operators would have to send vehicles through the temple sans guests to reset them.

“That was a major win for us,” says the lifelong San Pedro resident. The parking issue was completed in May, and it’s changed the ride’s capacity.

Of course, a huge cause of downtime for a Disneyland attraction is often largely out of the park’s control. It’s us, the guests, who are the problem.

“Our No. 1 downtime driver is guest items,” DiBernardo says. “Guests will be on the attraction, and they’ll have a large water bottle or a backpack. All the vehicles have a pouch for them to store everything, but if they don’t do that, there’s many twists and turns throughout the ride, and those articles can fly out. We’ll have to stop the ride. Not only are we working on ways we can improve maintenance reliability, we’re also working with our operations to help notify guests to store their items.”

To that end, each Indiana Jones Adventure vehicle has been outfitted with new, bigger pouches that should make it easier for riders to dump in their items. All of that should ensure more guests throughout the day can enjoy the attraction, and right now they’ll want to, as all the figures have been dusted, blacklight paint has been reapplied, and the Indiana Jones audio-animatronics have been disassembled and rebuilt.

Walking through the attraction on Thursday, DiBernardo pointed out how all the figures are currently moving with full mobility. The first time we see Indy he’s attempting to force shut a door, one that moves with the figure in front of vibrant animations. Later, he’s hanging over a guest in one of the ride’s grandest illusions, one designed to trick guests into believing the vehicle is reversing (it doesn’t move backward, but rather the walls shift) as a crater of a ball closes in on us.

Walking the track, I noticed many a detail I hadn’t seen on my hundreds of rides on the attraction over the decades. As Indy hovers above us, for instance, his legs and hips subtly shift. Elsewhere in the ride, when a vehicle dips down a hill, it’s actually going down steps. The latter are painted on the track, but DiBernardo notes that over time the cars eventually strip away the paint. I had never seen the stairs. Of course, it’s easy to be distracted by the skull in front of us hovering over a pit of lava, where many of the lights have now been refreshed.

Indiana Jones Adventure has changed over the years. Some opening day effects are long gone, such as the illusion created by actual shifting doors at the start of the attraction, or visions of tumbling rubble near its beginning. But Indiana Jones Adventure remains one of the park’s most detailed attractions, one in which I’m still discovering artistic minutiae more than 30 years after it opened in 1995. And as the recent refurbishment proves, it’s an attraction that remains an ever-evolving work of art.

“The sky is the limit on what we can also add to this attraction,” DiBernardo teases. But for now, it looks good as new.

#797. Blue Planet III features cloudier footage to prove authenticity amid AI boom

BBC series deliberately uses ‘more organic’ shots to assure viewers animals are not AI-generated, producers say

The David Attenborough-narrated series Blue Planet III deliberately includes cloudier, less polished footage to counter AI fakery and assure viewers it is “authentic”, its producers have said.

Nearly a decade after Blue Planet II shone a light on plastic pollution, and 25 years since the first Blue Planet aired, the hit BBC series is returning with more animal behaviour and cutting-edge technology, including the world’s first underwater CCTV camera – dubbed “stonefish” cam.

Since the first Blue Planet, TVs and cameras have evolved to give viewers sharper, higher definition pictures. But the combination of the rise in AI and the extraordinary sequences and fantastical creatures captured by BBC Studios’ natural history unit for this series led producers to include what they called more “organic” footage and less polished shots, to show that the animals were not AI-generated.

The executive producer, Elizabeth White, said AI, “makes us want to be even more authentic”, adding that the creatures filmed in the deep ocean “are probably more remarkable than AI could imagine and it’s completely real”.

She said that in the past “the amount of plankton and things that are in the water in the sequences” would usually mean they might be excluded, and previously, “we might have framed out a boat or a buoy or gone: ‘Oh, actually … the water conditions are not really good enough for this.’”

But now such things are being left in as “we’re really leaning into that authenticity … As AI booms we will probably lean more into the more organic”, murkier and less polished footage.

For the first time, the BBC’s specialist factual commissioning head, Jack Bootle, assured journalists at a press screening: “No gen AI was used in this series. Not a single frame. It’s all real photography.”

Bootle added that as we live in a “golden age of natural history-making … sometimes people feel like they’ve seen it all before [so] this series is bursting with novel behaviour”.

That includes never-before-seen action of dolphins using sea sponges to block holes in coral to trap fish, captured using underwater CCTV – developed by the programme’s producer and director, Yoland Bosiger.

Over the four years of filming across five oceans, BBC Studios “continually innovated”, said the series producer, Lara Bickerton, creating a “mini-dome” camera to follow an octopus, a “deep-sea selfie stick”, camera operators wearing leg-mounted scooters to create immersive TikTok-style diving footage and using lasers to film deep-sea creatures.

Other remarkable animal behaviour captured in the programme includes grouper communicating with another species of fish; 200,000 crabs lining up in formation to feed; sea urchins using shells as sun hats; and footage involving penguins that echoes the Planet Earth II, ‘iguana v snakes’ sequence.

Threats to the ocean as well as its resilience are addressed in the series. One episode shows how warmer seas caused by the climate crisis are killing off sea anemone where clown fish live and how they try to fix their homes, and the issue of plastics raised in Blue Planet II continues with a story about pollutants [and] forever chemicals, said White.

Bosiger said Attenborough, who is 100 years old, was “very much involved” and made changes to scripts.

White added: “What’s wonderful is he hasn’t lost his curiosity … he’s still that little boy … who gets excited … when you show him a new piece of footage.”

Asked if the BBC’s £500m cost-cutting drive would affect BBC Studios’ renowned natural history reputation, White said she could not comment on that but pointed out that Blue Planet III made savings by using more local crews on location than in previous series.
