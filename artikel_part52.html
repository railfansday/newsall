#2551. Moderna mRNA vaccine almost halves the risk of skin cancer returning

Severe sunburn increases the risk of developing melanoma, the most serious type of skin cancer.Credit: Olga Pankova/Getty

A personalized mRNA vaccine reduced the risk of skin cancer returning or the recipient dying by 42% when used alongside an immunotherapy drug, according to long-awaited results for what could become the first approved mRNA vaccine for cancer.

Oncologists regard a risk reduction of 10% as substantial, says Kristen Radford, an immunotherapist at Mater Research in Brisbane. A risk reduction of more than 40% “would send many researchers over the Moon”, adds Seth Cheetham, a geneticist at the University of Queensland in Brisbane, Australia.

The personalized vaccine, called intismeran, is not designed to prevent skin cancer, or melanoma, from developing in the first place, but to stop it returning in people who have already undergone surgery to remove tumours.

Anticipation for the trial results has been building since August, when the vaccine’s developers — Merck, based in Rahway, New Jersey, and Moderna, which is headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts — announced early positive results. Data from the phase III trial will be presented at the European Society for Medical Oncology meeting in Madrid on 24 October. But when data were leaked online on 22 October, the companies released the key results.

The stock price for Moderna, a pioneer in mRNA vaccine technology, had been rising since the August announcement, but dropped by around 25% after the leak. Health-care financial analyst Evan Seigerman at BMO Capital Markets in New York City told media company Forbes that such reactions in the stock market probably reflect “inflated expectation into the full data release, rather than a true indictment on the efficacy of the combo therapy”.

The intismeran trial enrolled 1,137 people with moderate to advanced melanoma across 157 centres. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a tailor-made vaccine (intismeran) alongside the immunotherapy drug pembrolizumab — which is already used to treat melanoma — or pembrolizumab plus an inactive placebo. Trial leader Georgina Long, an oncologist at Melanoma Institute Australia in Sydney, says participants have so far been followed for around 25 months and will continue to be monitored for several years to determine whether the vaccine improves overall survival. She adds that the therapy did not result in any major harmful side effects.

Roughly 15 years ago, people with advanced melanoma typically survived for only months. Today, nearly 55% are effectively cured with targeted drugs or immunotherapies, says Long. The combination of intismeran and pembrolizumab is designed for people whose cancer could return after surgery and who do not respond to immunotherapy alone, she says.

Jessamy Tiffen, a cancer geneticist at the Centenary Institute in Sydney, says that the trial results are exciting, but that only time will tell how effective the treatment is beyond two years. “Cancer cells also have an insidious ability to adapt and evade whatever treatment we throw at them,” she says. Furthermore, some people did not respond to the vaccine, she says.

Long says that her team will conduct a statistical analysis to identify who is most likely to benefit from the treatment.

To develop intismeran, Long and her colleagues sequenced DNA from a sample of each participant’s tumour to identify mutations in the cancer cells. These mutations can give rise to proteins called neoantigens, which help to distinguish cancer cells from healthy ones.

#2554. What night is van Gogh’s ‘The Starry Night’? New analysis says it’s in July.

Published Oct 22, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

The swirling bluish-white mistrals, towering cypress trees, and glittery stars in Vincent van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” (1889) have captivated our imaginations for generations. Despite never selling a painting during his lifetime, van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” has since inspired a hit song in 1972, draws visitors to the Museum of Modern Art in New York City, and adorns everything from journals to scarves and tote bags.

But what particular night above Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, France, did the troubled post-impressionist painter actually put on the canvas? A new analysis points to July 22, 1889. During the early morning hours at that time of year, a waning crescent moon, the planet Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in Taurus appeared close together—all of which are seen in the painting. A study published today in the journal Scientific Reports details the findings.

According to the Metropolitan Museum of Art, van Gogh was inspired by the view from his window at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in southern France. He spent twelve months there 1889–1890, seeking reprieve from his mental illnesses and was also diagnosed with a form of epilepsy.

Like many of his paintings that year, “The Starry Night” was created in several sessions during the day, under different weather conditions. The village nestled below the hills was also based on other views, and could not be seen from his window. Although van Gogh reconstructed some of the features in the night sky as he saw them, he also altered the celestial shapes and added a sense of glow.

“Van Gogh assigned an emotional language to night and nature that took them far from their actual appearances,” the museum writes. “Dominated by vivid blues and yellows applied with gestural verve and immediacy, ‘The Starry Night’ also demonstrates how inseparable van Gogh’s vision was from the new procedures of painting he had devised, in which color and paint describe a world outside the artwork even as they telegraph their own status as, merely, color and paint.”

Using astronomy to pinpoint which day inspired the painting began with UCLA art historian Albert Boime in 1984 and Harvard University astronomer and astrophysicist Charles Whitney in 1986. The two scholars reached conflicting conclusions, and neither of them displaced the prevailing religious and symbolic interpretations of the painting.

Historically, the painting has been dated based on a letter van Gogh wrote to his brother Theo around June 18, 1889. However, the waning gibbous moon from mid-June does not account for the slender crescent seen in the painting. By July 22, 1889, the Hyades star cluster rose in the eastern sky alongside Venus, and the moon’s shape looked closer to the crescent on the canvas. This new study uses observational geometry to find an answer.

“To an observational astronomer, the cluster of stars in ‘The Starry Night’ is the Hyades in Taurus,” said study co-author Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim.  “But the moment I read the earlier studies explaining it as Aries, I heard the painting calling to me.” Kim is the CEO at South Korean robotic telescope company SLLAB and a PhD candidate at Kyung Hee University.

For this research, the team combined astronomical simulations and measurements of star positions, with historical weather records, and van Gogh’s own letters. They also used earlier sketches of the landscape to reconstruct the narrow field of view represented in “The Starry Night.”

According to the team, the crescent moon, Venus, and the Hyades star cluster in the constellation Taurus would have been visible around dawn on July 22, 1889. This configuration generally corresponds to what is portrayed in the painting. Weather records suggest that the night sky around July 22 would have been clear enough for this astronomical arrangement to be visible. Additionally, the frequent mentions of cicadas in van Gogh’s letters suggest that the region was seeing a period of hot weather, which matches the recorded temperatures in July 1889 better than in June.

The team also verified that the painter’s field of view in another painting titled “Starry Night over the Rhône” (1888) with photographs taken on site was painted when the sky was in Aries. Using the same method, the team also believes that the stars in “Café Terrace at Night” (1888) may be the Summer Triangle.

“I hope this work prompts a renewed, multidisciplinary look at the sequence and working methods of the Saint-Rémy paintings, and at Van Gogh’s movements that summer,” Kim said. “Our conversation with Van Gogh’s other skies is still ongoing. We plan to tell those stories one by one.”

#2555. AI Can Spot Signs of Impending Death Among Cancer Patients in a Single Face Photo

A new facial-analysis system predicts cancer mortality risk, but its clinical usefulness remains unproven.

Doctors have long studied their patients’ faces for signs of illness. But a new AI system has taken it a step further.

In a remarkable new study, researchers developed an artificial intelligence system that could identify cancer patients at unusually high risk of dying. Of the 122 patients assigned the highest risk scores, more than half died within just 90 days of having their picture taken. Among 975 patients in the lowest-risk group, only 20 died during the same period.

Our faces may carry subtle signs of deteriorating health that even experienced doctors struggle to quantify. Trained on more than 40 million facial photographs, the AI learned to recognize patterns associated with biological aging and cancer survival.

However, it’s not like you can upload a selfie to this system and find out your life expectancy. It’s more complicated than that, and, for now, the higher-than-chance prediction rate is validated in quite ill cancer patients.

“We’re trained in medical school to walk into a room, look at the patient and make an assessment — an impression of how healthy, frail or sick they are,” Raymond Mak, a radiation oncologist at Mass General Brigham and a senior author of the study, told Scientific American. The problem, he said, is “that’s really a subjective assessment.”

An AI could do a face check more reliably than a human doctor, helping medical staff evaluate whether a patient can withstand difficult treatments.

The researchers began by training an AI model called FAHR-Face on more than 40 million facial photographs. During training, the system repeatedly hid random sections of each face and learned to reconstruct the missing details, teaching itself to recognize minute facial features and patterns.

They then developed two specialized versions. One learned to estimate a person’s biological age using more than 740,000 photographs with known ages. In an independent public-photo test, its age estimates missed the actual age by an average of 5.1 years. The other studied photographs and survival records from 34,363 cancer patients, learning which facial characteristics were associated with living longer or dying sooner.

Among the patients treated with radiotherapy, looking older than one’s actual age was associated with poorer survival. After accounting for other clinical factors, patients whose facial age exceeded their real age by at least five years had a 21 percent higher rate of death over follow-up compared with those below that threshold.

In a separate test of FAHR-FaceSurvival involving 3,820 patients, 64 of the 122 people with the highest scores (52.5 percent) died within 90 days of their photographs. Among 975 patients with the lowest scores, 20 (2.1 percent) died in that period. Even after accounting for age, cancer diagnosis and treatment, patients in the highest-risk quarter based on their facial features had more than three times the death rate of those in the lowest-risk quarter.

To check whether their findings held up beyond the original patients, the researchers tested both AI tools on a separate group of 4,892 cancer patients in the Netherlands. The results followed the same pattern: patients whose faces looked older than their actual age tended to die sooner. The same was true for those flagged as high-risk by the survival-prediction AI.

What’s interesting here is that the two tools picked up on different warning signs. So, by using both together, doctors may offer a slightly better picture of a patient’s survival prospects than using either tool alone.

To estimate biological age, the first AI focused on the nasolabial folds — the creases running from either side of the nose toward the corners of the mouth — as well as the forehead just above the eyes and the temples. These are areas where wrinkles, changes in skin elasticity and loss of facial volume can become more noticeable as we age.

The survival-prediction AI, however, paid particular attention to the area beneath the eyes and around the bridge of the nose. But what exactly was it detecting? Was it dark circles, puffiness, hollowing beneath the eyes or something far subtler? The odd thing is that the researchers don’t yet know. Their analysis revealed which facial regions influenced the predictions, but not which specific physical changes signaled a greater risk of death. So, this black box AI won’t give us any hints that one might use by looking in the mirror.

The research extends the same team’s 2025 FaceAge study, which linked older-looking faces to shorter cancer survival. Another study published in April 2026 found that patients whose facial appearance aged faster across successive photographs also tended to have poorer outcomes.

The researchers also found links between older-looking facial age and smoking, heavy drinking and drug use. Estimates were reasonably consistent across makeup, facial expressions and lighting, though image conditions can still affect them. As we’re all painfully aware, a selfie taken during a certain time of the day or after a rough night can make you look a bit older and beaten up than you normally would.

The American cancer-patient test group was about 85 percent White, and both clinical cohorts consisted of people receiving radiotherapy. This means, these tools cannot be used confidently with the general population. The study did not attempt to assess whether AI score improves treatment decisions or outperforms doctors’ assessments of frailty. That would be a study for another time.

“Predicting an outcome is not the same as explaining it,” Miroslaw Bober, an AI researcher at the University of Surrey who was not involved, said in comments published by the Science Media Centre.

And while such AIs could be useful, there’s also the ethical risk that a wrong statistical forecast can change a patient’s fate. Matthew Liao, a New York University bioethicist, warned Scientific American that errors could result in patients being denied care. He also raised concerns about insurers or employers exploiting facial health predictions.

For now, the face offers a provocative clue in some patients — but thankfully not a medical verdict.

The findings appeared in The Lancet Digital Health.

#2556. Physicists Use Light To Unlock a Crystal’s Hidden Electrical Powers

A crystal’s laser-induced electrical order lasts just a trillionth of a second, giving researchers a way to explore ultrafast approaches to processing and storing information.

Inside a crystal, tiny electrical regions can point in different directions or line up in an organized pattern. Cooling helps bring them into alignment, but physicists at Auburn University have now shown that brief flashes of laser light can also produce order at warmer temperatures, where those regions would otherwise remain disorganized.

The team worked with single crystals of a modified form of strontium titanate. The material allows electrons to move through it while also containing tiny regions with distinct positive and negative sides, an unusual combination of electrical properties. These regions are known as polar nanoregions.

For decades, physicists have debated whether atoms shift together to create the material’s polarized state or whether its polar regions already exist and become organized as it cools. Telling those explanations apart has been difficult because the regions are so small and their signals so faint.

Wencan Jin, the Thomas and Jean Walter Endowed Associate Professor in Auburn’s Department of Physics, led the research with graduate student Mohamed Kandil. The project began at the 2023 American Physical Society March Meeting, where Jin connected with researchers in Japan who could grow the crystals. Collaborators at Japan’s National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology grew the samples used in the experiments.

The Auburn researchers built a highly sensitive laser-based measurement system, then spent nearly a year improving the equipment before detecting the regions’ faint optical signal. By measuring how the crystal changed incoming light, they could track the regions’ alignment through repeated rounds of warming and cooling. The measurements showed disordered directions at higher temperatures giving way to preferred orientations as the crystal cooled. This provided strong evidence that the polar regions were already present before they lined up.

The team still had to capture the fleeting moment when laser illumination brought the regions into alignment. “The breakthrough came in 2025, when Mohamed finally observed the disordered polar regions becoming aligned under laser illumination,” Jin said. “Seeing that signal emerge was a thrilling moment for the team.”

The light-induced alignment lasted only a picosecond, or one trillionth of a second (10⁻¹² second), but an optical measurement could still detect the resulting pattern. Being able to create and read that brief arrangement with light gives researchers a way to investigate extremely fast approaches to processing and storing information. It also provides a tool for studying electrical behavior that is otherwise difficult to observe.

“This experiment has truly been Mohamed’s odyssey,” Jin said. “It took him three years of dedication to complete the work and achieve his first publication.”

Reference: “Optical Poling of a Quantum Ferroelectric Metal across the Order-Disorder Phase Transition” by Mohamed Kandil, Yasuhide Tomioka, Yafei Ren, Ryan Comes, Isao H. Inoue and Wencan Jin, 14 October 2026, Physical Review Letters. DOI: 10.1103/nx3d-1mqf

#2557. Could lowering high blood pressure be as simple as stimulating your ear?

A small device that delivers gentle electrical pulses through the ear could offer a new approach to treating stubborn high blood pressure. In a clinical trial led by researchers at Queen Mary University of London, the technology proved safe, well tolerated, and practical for patients to use at home.

Although the treatment did not significantly lower blood pressure compared with a placebo device across all participants, researchers observed encouraging results in patients with more severe hypertension. The findings suggest that stimulating nerves in the ear could eventually help certain people whose blood pressure remains elevated despite taking medication.

The results come from the SCRATCH-HTN trial, published in Hypertension, a journal of the American Heart Association. Researchers emphasize that larger studies are needed to determine whether the approach can provide meaningful benefits and which patients might respond best.

How Ear Nerve Stimulation Could Lower Blood Pressure

The experimental treatment uses a technique called transcutaneous autonomic neuromodulation (tAN), which aims to influence the autonomic nervous system. This network of nerves automatically controls essential bodily functions, including heart rate, digestion, and blood pressure.

By delivering mild electrical signals to nerves in the ear, researchers hope to influence the body's natural mechanisms for regulating blood pressure without requiring an invasive procedure.

The handheld device, developed by Afferent Medical Solutions, uses two small clips positioned on the tragus, the small flap of cartilage located just in front of the ear canal. These clips deliver gentle electrical stimulation, allowing patients to administer the treatment themselves without visiting a clinic.

Clinical Trial Tests the Device in 63 Patients

The clinical trial, funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR), enrolled 63 people with uncontrolled hypertension. All participants were already taking at least one medication to lower their blood pressure, but their readings remained above recommended levels.

Researchers randomly divided the participants into two groups. One received active nerve stimulation, while the other used a sham device designed to serve as a comparison. This approach helped investigators assess whether the electrical stimulation produced benefits beyond those associated with using the device itself.

Participants used their assigned devices for 30 minutes daily during the first two weeks. For the following 10 weeks, they reduced their sessions to once per week.

The results showed that the treatment was safe and generally comfortable for participants. Most also followed the prescribed schedule, suggesting that the technology could be practical for regular use at home.

However, when researchers compared blood pressure changes across the entire study population, they found no statistically significant difference between patients receiving active stimulation and those using the sham device.

Promising Results in Patients With Severe Hypertension

Despite the lack of a significant overall benefit, the researchers identified a potentially important finding among participants who entered the trial with more severe hypertension.

A prespecified subgroup analysis, meaning the researchers had planned this comparison before examining the results, revealed a greater reduction in blood pressure among these patients when they received active nerve stimulation rather than the sham treatment.

This finding raises the possibility that the technique may work better for people with particularly difficult-to-control blood pressure. However, the trial was relatively small, and the subgroup results require confirmation in larger studies before any conclusions can be drawn about its effectiveness.

Dr. Ajay Gupta, Chief Investigator, Clinical Reader at Queen Mary University of London and Consultant Physician at Barts Health NHS Trust, said:

"This is an exciting, highly patient-acceptable technology that could ultimately provide a cheap, scalable solution for millions. Although the trial did not achieve significance across the broad unselected group, those with more severe hypertension exhibited a clear blood pressure reduction in the active group compared to sham.

"As the first and most thorough study of its kind, this points towards important new research directions that could help us understand whether non-invasive neuromodulation can benefit particular groups of patients with uncontrolled hypertension."

A Potential New Option for Hard-to-Control Blood Pressure

High blood pressure, also known as hypertension, is a major contributor to cardiovascular disease and increases the risk of heart attacks and strokes. It places additional strain on blood vessels and the heart, potentially causing serious damage over time.

Although medications help many patients manage their condition, a substantial number continue to have blood pressure readings above recommended targets even while receiving treatment.

This ongoing challenge has encouraged scientists to explore additional approaches that could complement existing therapies.

Ear nerve stimulation is one such possibility. Because the device is small, does not require surgery, and can be used at home, it could offer a convenient option if future research establishes that it reliably lowers blood pressure in certain patients.

For now, the findings represent an early step rather than proof of an effective new treatment. The researchers say larger clinical trials will be essential to confirm the encouraging results in patients with severe hypertension and identify which groups, if any, could benefit most from this approach.
