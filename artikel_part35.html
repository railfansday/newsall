#1702. Pluralistic: The death of history's most successful publisher (20 Oct 2026)

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

I published my first story in 1988, when I was 17 years old. Today, at 55, I've published more than 30 books, with three more coming in the next year or so. In the intervening years, I've spent a lot of time thinking and talking about publishing.

I've had plenty of opportunity to reflect on publishing: I've been a bookseller and worked in libraries, I wrote a column for Publishers Weekly, self-published a few books of my own, published the first-ever book licensed under Creative Commons, and broke several Kickstarter records for audiobook projects. But most of all, I've worked with publishing professionals: publicists, marketing directors, operations people, copyeditors, and, above all, editors.

My first novel editor was the magnificent Patrick Nielsen Hayden, who supported my publishing career from the very start, donating to help send me to the Clarion science fiction writers' workshop and inviting me to come read slush at Tor Books whenever I was in New York. Patrick didn't teach me everything I know about publishing, but he sure taught me a lot about publishing, and hardly a day goes by without my having cause to reflect on one of our many discussions over our decades-long professional and personal association.

Out of all the things I learned about publishing from Patrick, one truth stands out above all: publishing is "the act of making something public." Publishing may or may not involve editing, layout, printing, binding, marketing or other activities that we associate with publishing houses, but publishing always involves identifying a work, identifying an audience, and finding ways to connect that work to that audience.

This definition does a lot of useful work. For one thing, it locates publishing as part of the hard problem of human society, namely, "getting people to care about something that matters to you." Beyond publishing, this is the crux of politics, religion and commerce:

https://pluralistic.net/2021/07/04/self-publishing/

For another, this definition lets us understand that (for most of its existence) Google was the most successful publisher in human history. No other institution of any kind connected more works with more audiences. Lots of people have heard of Google's wildly unsuccessful motto "Don't be evil," but far fewer are familiar with Google's wildly successful mission statement, "organize the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful."

But all of that is in the past tense. With its AI projects – AI summaries, the Gemini chatbot – Google has abandoned the practice of "connecting works with audiences." Today, Google's function is to plunder all the works on the internet and chop them up and arrange them in an unimaginably vast, multidimensional array for the explicit purpose of preventing audiences from connecting with those works.

For most of its history, Google operated in a virtuous cycle with publishers whereby they allowed Google to crawl their sites and Google located audiences and connected them with the pages on those sites. Today, that cycle has turned vicious, with Google acting as a parasite that extracts value from the web's publishers and offers nothing in return.

This is entirely in keeping with the enshittification hypothesis: that once a platform is liberated from the disciplining forces of competition, regulation, labor and interoperability, it will squeeze its suppliers and users and harvest all the value for itself:

https://pluralistic.net/2023/01/21/potemkin-ai/#hey-guys

But AI represents a new phase in enshittification. Google has assayed enshittificatory gambits in the past, like deliberately worsening its search results in order to force you to search repeatedly, which gave the company more chances to show you ads:

https://pluralistic.net/2024/04/24/naming-names/#prabhakar-raghavan

But as sleazy as those gambits were, they were profitable. In some important sense, they were sustainable. Assuming Google could carry on indefinitely scamming its users in this way, they'd continue to bring in more cash than they spent, and continue to be the world's most successful publisher, connecting works and audiences (albeit in a deliberately degraded way).

Contrast this with AI, the money-losingest endeavor in the history of human civilization. Google is losing hundreds of billions of dollars on AI. The more AI Google does, the worse the company's financial footing becomes. If Google does this for long enough, it may cease to exist altogether. This collapse can only be prolonged by Google's ongoing extraction of value from web publishers, who no longer have their works connected with audiences via Google search results. Instead, Google usurps those audiences, shows them as many ads as it can, and still loses money in the bargain.

In Tim Wu's Age of Extraction, Wu lays out a thesis about how intermediaries – middlemen who perform a useful function connecting different groups, firms and individuals – are prone to developing "main character syndrome," abandoning their role as facilitators for others and abusing their position to make themselves the prime beneficiary of the transactions they facilitate:

https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/691177/the-age-of-extraction-by-tim-wu/

Google has a scorching case of main character syndrome, and nowhere is that more obvious than in its AI-fueled transformation into web-scale parasite. The fact that this gambit might well destroy Google and the publishers the company once served – rather than merely enshittifying search with worse results and more ads – makes the whole thing look more like a Mafia bust-out than the enshittification of the first half of the 2020s:

It's certainly an ignominious end for history's most successful publisher (as was).

#1705. Microsoft Patches More Than 1,400 Security Flaws

Microsoft today issued updates to fix more than 1,400 security vulnerabilities in its Windows operating systems and other software. Today’s epic patch batch dwarfs September’s record of nearly 1,000 fixes, which was already about ten times the normal monthly patch load from Redmond.

Just one of the weaknesses patched this week is considered a “zero day,” or already exploited in the wild. CVE-2026-96951 is a security feature bypass in the Windows Shell that affects Windows 10 and 11 systems, as well as some Server versions.

Windows 10 users might be wondering if they can still expect security updates after this month. Microsoft had been slated to stop shipping updates for Windows 10 systems in October 2025, but ended up adding another year of free updates after considerable pushback. A few months ago, Microsoft quietly extended that support for another year (until October 2027).

At least 78 of the flaws fixed this month are rated “critical,” meaning they could be exploited with little or no action by the user, and most of those critical bugs allow an attacker to execute code of their choice on the vulnerable system.

While Microsoft said there are no signs anyone is exploiting it yet, a security feature bypass in the .NET Framework (CVE-2026-108398) was publicly disclosed prior to today, potentially giving attackers advance time to work out how to exploit the bug.

Satnam Narang at Tenable said the number of flaws fixed this month far surpasses the previous record-setting year 2020, when Microsoft patched 1,261 CVEs.

“We’ve now crossed the 4,000 CVE mark with two months left to go,” he said.

Microsoft credits large language models for the high volume of bug discovery, and presumably AI gets credit for many of the patches used to fix these flaw. Meanwhile, security experts have been warning that these unusually large patch volleys from Microsoft are placing extra strain on corporate security teams, which often have to test individual updates before deploying them across a network to ensure the fixes don’t cause problems for other software.

Tyler Reguly, associate director of security research and development at Fortra, notes that just 26 Microsoft vulnerabilities appeared in the “Known Exploited Vulnerabilities” (KEV) catalog maintained by the U.S. Cybersecurity & Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) so far this year, and security teams would do well to prioritize patching those 26 vulnerabilities first.

“Security leaders should focus on exploitable risk, and ask whether their teams have the clarity, capacity and support to act,” Reguly said.

#1706. Spain grows fruit and vegetables for Europe, but it's running out of water

Spain's southern coast produces up to 25% of Europe's fruit and vegetables. But amid intensive farming, climate change and illegal wells, the water it takes to grow them is drying up.

Donana National Park in southwestern Spain — one of Europe's most important wetlands, home to millions of migratory birds and threatened species like the Iberian lynx — is running dry. But rising temperatures and drought caused by climate change are only part of the problem.

The UNESCO-protected region on the Atlantic coast relies on an underground aquifer that's been tapped by hundreds of illegal wells. The reason: Europe's fruit and vegetable supply. Spain provides one-quarter of Europe's fresh produce, supplying colder parts of the continent with fruit and vegetables year-round.

The scale of water extraction in Spain is staggering: In 2016, WWF Spain estimated that the Donana wetland was home to over 1,000 illegal wells and more than 1,700 ponds illegally irrigating an area as big as 4,200 football fields.

"It's gotten hotter every year. And the water dries up so quickly," one local resident in the town of El Rocio, just north of the wetlands, told DW.

"For me, day to day, the main issue is my animals," said another, hitching his horse up to a tourist carriage. "I have to draw water for them, but the well doesn't have enough water to provide it all at once."

The Donana area used to be royal hunting grounds, consisting of marshes and ponds. In the early 20th century, parts began to be used for dryland farming, agriculture in arid regions that relies heavily on rainwater.

But in 1971, Spain — still under the dictatorship of General Francisco Franco — declared the region's irrigation to be of national interest. The Almonte-Marismas Plan, named after the aquifer it tapped into, was part of a massive, state-sponsored effort to increase food production.

Authorities decided to bet on strawberries, which they called "red gold." According to calculations based on FAO data, strawberries can yield up to €150,000 (about $170,000) in gross revenue per hectare per year, compared to roughly €20,000 for potatoes or €15,000 for grapes.

But growing strawberries at scale also meant using a lot more water. Authorities handed out generous quotas and allotments, seemingly without considering how much extraction the aquifer could handle. Even those legal quotas weren't enough for the ever-expanding fields.

Farmers turned to illegal wells and reservoirs to make up the difference. They built them to stockpile water, draining supplies from local residents and the UNESCO World Heritage Site next door.

"There was extreme drought and lack of attention from the administration," said Pedro Banez, of the Condado Water Users' Association. "And we found ourselves with a number of farms — or parts of farms — that fell outside the land classified as irrigable agricultural land."

The turning point came in 2019 when, following numerous reports of unlawful wells, a 2-year-old fell into deep, reportedly illegal borehole on farmland near Malaga and died. In the aftermath, Spain's Civil Guard police force created dedicated units focused on water theft.

Two years after that, in 2021, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that Spain had failed to protect the Donana aquifer, finding the country in violation of EU law.

Today, police and NGOs like WWF Spain track illegal extraction — near Donana, and other overtaxed agricultural regions across the country — using a mix of old and new tools. They start with satellite images of the region, comparing them against land rights maps to flag areas that appear greener than their legal status allows. Drones follow, giving investigators a closer look at where the water is actually coming from.

Some wells are completely illegal. Others are legal but extract more than they're allowed, which requires closer, on-site inspection to prove.

Investigators check the flow meters that measure how much water a well is pulling. Tampering is common, often using a powerful magnet which disrupts the internal mechanism of the flow meter, allowing water to pass through unrecorded.

Farmers caught extracting illegally can be charged with crimes against the environment and fraud. Most face fines, but sentences can run up to five years in prison.

In a bid to undo decades of overuse, local and national authorities have negotiated a €1.4 billion joint plan. The government has continued shutting down illegal wells, sealing almost 300 in the Donana region alone.

Officials have also started buying up farmland, rewilding it and turning it into protected areas, while setting up funds to create jobs in other sectors like tourism and renewable energy.

Farmers, meanwhile, have had to change how they water their crops.

Many have switched to drip irrigation systems, where each plant gets its own individual dripper, paired with moisture sensors that report exactly how much water a plant needs. That cuts down on the overuse of both water and fertilizer.

Farmers have also begun planting less water-intensive crops and shifting their growing season to colder months.

Spain isn't alone in confronting this problem. As economies mature, fruit and vegetable production starts being outsourced to places with cheaper labor, fewer restrictions and more sun.

Mexico, Brazil and Colombia grow food for parts of the US, while countries in southeast Asia export a lot of produce to China or Japan. Some countries in northern Africa, including Morocco and Egypt, also supply fresh fruits and vegetables to their European neighbors to the north. But, as in Spain, intensive agriculture has strained local water supplies.

Morocco has tried to turn the tide by using satellite comparisons to monitor irrigation and offering subsidies for farmers who switch to drip irrigation. In India, the government is trying to move farmers toward less water-intensive crops.

And in California, the US state has paid farmers to halt certain types of irrigation and introduced legislation to monitor and allocate water more sustainably.

Securing water, no longer seen as an unlimited resource, will increasingly be a challenge as the effects of climate change intensify. Addressing overextraction will take a coordinated effort from governments, farmers and the industries that depend on their water.

Burning fossil fuels may have stopped the Earth from entering another ice age, a new study suggests. The research, published Thursday in the journal Science, uses samples of ancient vegetation and rock records to analyze the growth—and sudden retreat—of an ice cap that is the last vestige of an ice sheet that once covered much of North America.

The findings shows the Barnes Ice Cap, which covers parts of Canada’s Baffin Island, started growing a few thousand years ago before rapidly reversing course beginning in the 1960s as carbon pollution ramped up.

“A question that’s in the popular consciousness is, have we caused enough global warming to stop the next ice age from happening?” says Andrew Wickert, an associate professor of environmental science at the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, who was not involved in the research. The new research shows that “even as we were just starting to ramp up emissions, we’ve already done enough damage to cause the major North American ice sheet to already start retreating. That’s significant.”

The planet regularly goes through phases where ice extends down from the high latitudes—what we colloquially call "ice ages"—due to natural shifts in the Earth's rotation and orbit, known as Milankovitch Cycles. Those are followed by interglacial periods where ice retreats toward the poles.

The last ice age ended 12,000 years ago. At its peak, what's known as the Laurentide Ice Sheet covered all of Canada and extended as far south as New York and the Midwest. It retreated to form the Barnes Ice Cap, which covers an area the size of Delaware on Baffin Island.

Unlike glaciers, which can move, the Barnes Ice Cap is frozen to the bedrock, preserving the vegetal record of what grew around it under the ice. That quirk allowed the scientists leading the new study to glean information about how much it has grown—and shrank.

To determine when it first reached its modern size, they used an exposure dating technique to calculate roughly how long rocks several meters from the glacier’s edge had been exposed to the sky. Using measurements of Beryllium-10, an element that forms on the surface of exposed rock, the authors calculated that the ice sheet first reached the size it is today around 3,600 years ago.

Next, they wanted to see how much the ice cap had regrown from that point. To do this, they turned to the humble denizens of the tundra in front of the glacier: mosses and lichen.

“After the ice cap retreated, mosses eventually colonized this newly exposed land,” says Nicolás Young, a paleoclimatologist at the Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory at Columbia University and lead author of the new paper. “These mosses are living there, and things are normal—then, eventually, the ice cap starts to re-expand. The ice covers the moss, and kills it.” The traces of the dead moss are preserved in newly reexposed land, allowing Young and his team to piece together a timeline of how the ice has moved across the landscape.

Thanks to these records, Young and his coauthors estimate that the glacier started growing again around 2,000 years ago. This may have signaled the start of another glaciation period.

But starting in the 1960s, the ice cap has done something that wouldn’t correspond with another ice age: It started getting smaller again. The team used historic aerial photos that showed that the ice has reversed course, retreating significantly back from its new margins. The Barnes Ice Cap’s edges have retreated an average of 4 meters per year since the 1980s. That coincides with a period of rapidly increasing carbon emissions, which has caused ice to go into retreat worldwide. The ice cap offers a particularly stark example, though, given the growth prior to the 1960s.

“Modern retreat of this glacier has reversed 2,000 years of net expansion—that’s a pretty decent chunk of time,” Young says.

Meredith Kelly, a professor of earth sciences at Dartmouth, says the data gathered in the study is “awesome” and that the study shows a “clear pattern of what the ice cap was doing.” However, Kelly says that she would like to see a comparison of some of the data to other sites around the world to more fully grasp how, exactly, the Earth was moving into its glaciation period before the effects of human-caused warming kicked in. While we have a broad understanding of what kicks off periods where ice can grow, the exact mechanisms are still something that require more research.

The evidence presented in this paper, Young says, could also validate a decades-old hypothesis that has long been held up by climate deniers as “evidence” climate change is a hoax. In the early 1970s, scientists gathered for a meeting at Brown University to discuss hypotheses of when the next ice age might happen. (Young says that two of his coauthors on this paper were at that meeting.) The scientists concluded that a glaciation period was so imminent that they sent a warning letter to President Richard Nixon.

#1711. Pluralistic: The technopolitics of ad-tech (19 Oct 2026)

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

"Technopolitics" are the politics embedded in technology. The Ur-text of technopolitics is "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" by the rock critic turned scholar Langdon Winner:

https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~beki/cs4001/Winner.pdf

With "Do Artifacts," Winner demands that we move beyond the idea of technologies having "intended consequences" and "unintended consequences" and towards a political economy of tech. In service to this, he divides the politics of artifacts into three categories:

I. Artifacts that have politics embedded in them by their creators, like Robert Moses's infamous bridges over the Long Island parkways. According to Moses biographer Robert Caro, Moses deliberately specified that these bridges must be too low for a city bus to pass under, in a bid to block Black people from traveling from New York City to the beaches he'd developed on Long Island.

Today those bridges are more than a century old, and they are still too low for city buses to pass under them, and this continues to mean that people who don't own private vehicles find it more difficult to get to the beach, and as it's still the case that race is a predictor of low incomes, those are still racist bridges.

II. Artifacts whose politics are accidental but unavoidable. Winner cites a kind of tomato harvester that vastly improved the efficiency of tomato pickers. These harvesters became very popular, which allowed tomato farmers to lay off large numbers of agricultural workers, and also allowed them to price the mechanically harvested tomatoes so cheaply that they crowded out other kinds of tomatoes.

Because these machines created a large pool of unemployed workers, they shifted power from labor to capital, and because these picking machines were too rough for all but the hardest varietals of tomato, they changed the flavor profile of tomatoes in America for the worse, towards a bland, tough tomato.

These picking machines were not designed to make workers poorer, nor were they designed to make tomatoes taste worse, but because of their technical characteristics, this was the unavoidable outcome, the inextricable politics of these machines.

III. Artifacts whose politics are intrinsic to their design. Here, Winner contrasts solar panels with nuclear reactors. Solar panels may require highly centralized industrial systems to produce, but once they are in the field, solar panels are compatible with looser, less coordinated political arrangements. A world of small anarchist communes is fully compatible with solar energy.

Contrast this with nuclear reactors: the prudent ongoing operation of a reactor requires a highly centralized state that is capable of fielding reliable, trustworthy independent inspectors. This state must also be capable of exercising the coercive power to shut down badly maintained or incompetently operated reactors.

The politics of nukes are the politics of a centralized, powerful state; the politics of solar are less determined. Solar is compatible with a wide variety of political systems, including (but not limited to) weak, decentralized states.

(For a further exploration of "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" I highly recommend Ginger Coons' reading guide:)

https://www.not-so-obvious.net/reading-do-artifacts-have-politics/

I really like Winner's framework for exploring technopolitics, but I have my own gloss on it. Winner's framework doesn't explicitly contemplate the idea of technological countermeasures. For example, what if someone were to design a city bus that was low enough to fit under Robert Moses's bridges? At that point, they'd cease to be "racist bridges," and merely become "bridges built by a racist":

https://locusmag.com/feature/commentary-cory-doctorow-technopolitics/

Which brings me to the technopolitics of ad-tech. Lots of people think that advertising is "the original sin of the internet," setting in motion the surveillance dystopia that is closing in on us from all sides today:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/17/mercenary-spyware/#discover-tech

I think this is wrong. I think that finding ways to communicate ideas and pitches to the general public is fine. Without some channel for reaching people with ideas and propositions they may not be familiar with, it would be infinitely harder to reach people for political causes. It would also be harder for new books, movies and music to find new audiences. It would be much harder to get people out to your independent craft fair, your zine show, or your No Kings march.

For me, the internet's original sin isn't advertising, it's surveillance. Contrary to what you have been told – and pax Winner's framework – the internet is fully compatible with privacy:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/10/31/losing-the-crypto-wars/#surveillance-monopolism

It wasn't the internet's technopolitics that made it a surveillance nightmare. It was just plain politics. Specifically, our politicians decided to stop enacting and enforcing privacy laws. The last consumer privacy law we got in America was a bill that made it illegal for video store clerks to disclose your VHS rentals:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_Privacy_Protection_Act

(As I type these words, the Supreme Court is poised to eliminate even that dried-up old crumb of privacy protection:)

https://slate.com/news-and-politics/2026/10/supreme-court-privacy-rights-case-bork-kavanaugh.html

The widespread phenomenon of ad-tech companies spying on us is the result of the decision to allow them to do so. That decision has had innumerable negative downstream effects, such as skyrocketing "surveillance prices":

https://pluralistic.net/2025/12/11/nothing-personal/#instacartography

https://pluralistic.net/2024/12/18/loose-flapping-ends/#luigi-has-a-point

It's created vast surveillance databases that are critical to Trump's racist pogroms, providing dossiers and precise location data on the people ICE kidnaps to Salvadoran slave-labor camps:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/03/10/ice-tech/#foreseeable-outcomes

Surveillance also promotes oligarchy. Without surveillance, oligarchs must give up more of their fortunes to keep the public happy, because the alternative is spending even more money on guards to keep the public from building guillotines on their lawns. Surveillance lets oligarchs make targeted strikes on restive radicals, which is much cheaper than broad-spectrum policies that make us all better off and neutralize the case for guillotines:

https://pluralistic.net/2025/06/26/autostabilizer/#slicey-bois

These are the technopolitics of surveillance, not advertising. There is nothing about advertising that demands surveillance. For centuries, advertising was not meaningfully linked to surveillance. The decision to fuse surveillance with advertising was just that – a decision, not an inevitability.

Any time we forget this, we let spying tech bosses off the hook. After all, if advertising demands surveillance, then tech bosses are just the avatars of the great forces of history, prisoners of the imperative to spy in order to advertise. By remembering that surveillance advertising is a choice, we put the blame squarely where it belongs: on the guillotineable oligarchs who made that choice, and amassed spectacular fortunes thereby.

Surveillance is political, but surveillance advertising has its own distinct technopolitics, and to understand those technopolitics, we must contrast them with the technopolitics of contextual advertising. Up until a decade or two ago, contextual advertising was simply called "advertising." Contextual advertising is the practice of running refrigerator ads next to articles on cooking and kitchen remodeling; ads for roadside restaurants on highway billboards; ads for Disney vacations in parenting magazines.

Your intuition probably insists that context advertising is less effective than surveillance advertising. After all, surveillance advertising allows a running shoe company to target users who are training for a marathon no matter where they are and no matter what they're doing. Surely this translates into increased "conversions" (clicks and/or purchases, depending on who you're talking to)?

It does, but not by much. Advertisers get about 5% more value from surveillance ads than they do from context ads:

https://weis2019.econinfosec.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/2019/05/WEIS_2019_paper_38.pdf

Partly, that's because surveillance ad companies grossly overrate the efficacy of surveillance advertising:

https://peoples-things.ghost.io/youre-doing-it-wrong-notes-on-criticism-and-technology-hype/

But surveillance advertising's anemic returns to advertisers are down to another aspect of the system: its tendency towards monopolization:

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Google_LLC_(2023)

Surveillance is a capital-intensive activity: spying on billions of people as they move through the digital and physical world is incredibly expensive. That is a good part of the reason that online advertising has collapsed into the Meta/Google duopoly, who control about 80% of display advertising.

The harms of surveillance monopolies have a severely distorting effect on policy debates. For example, it's led some anti-monopolists to call for government-issued surveillance identifiers that follow us from birth to death in order to facilitate commercial spying to foster more competition in surveillance:

https://www.thesling.org/what-to-do-about-googles-dominance-of-online-advertising/

Here's the thing about monopolists: they cheat. Google and Meta have an illegal, collusive arrangement to rig the advertising market, overcharging advertisers and underpaying publishers:

The ad-tech giants have taken in hundreds of billions that they simply can't account for. It's disappeared down a fraud-hole:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/05/25/lying-spies/#spying-liars

All of this has driven the majority of web users to install a privacy-blocker (like EFF's Privacybadger.org), meaning that surveillance ads are disproportionately likely to not even show up in a reader's browser:

https://doc.searls.com/2023/11/11/how-is-the-worlds-biggest-boycott-doing/

So while it's possible that, in a state of nature, a surveillance ad would outperform a context ad, delivering more sales for advertisers and more revenue for publishers, the monopolistic tendency of ad-tech means that publishers earn less from surveillance ads (because more users block them, and because monopolistic ad-tech platforms extract higher commissions), and advertisers see fewer sales from them (because more users block them, and because monopolistic ad-tech platforms extract higher prices).

Context ads, meanwhile, are always going to give power to publishers, because publishers will always understand the materials they publish better than any ad-tech platform could. Commissioning, editing and publishing an article, podcast or video gives a publisher unique and inalienable insight into that material.

Surveillance data about audiences is a kind of ambient vapor floating around websites and places and payments that has to be condensed through a heroic and expensive effort that gives power to whichever company can muster the capital and infrastructure to do so. Meanwhile, information about the content of publishers' offerings arrives pre-refined into ingots that are already in publishers' own possession.

It's possible to run "programmatic ads" without surveillance. Rather than building an ad-tech stack through which publishers transmit sensitive information about their audiences ("Who will pay me to advertise to this 18-34 year old man-child from the outer boroughs who owns an Xbox and has recently researched gonorrhea symptoms?"), we can build systems through which publishers transmit information about their own materials ("Who will advertise to a user with a New York IP address who is reading an article comparing running shoe brands?").

Not only is this possible, but it actually generates more revenues for publishers than the surveillance-based ad-tech stack:

https://techcrunch.com/2020/07/24/data-from-dutch-public-broadcaster-shows-the-value-of-ditching-creepy-ads/

On top of that, publishers who don't gather personal information about users also don't need to show their users those awful pop-up ads asking their permission to gather their data. Everyone hates those notices, and they are yet another negative aspect of surveillance advertising that disappears with the switch to context ads.

An ad-tech stack for context ads is far less prone to monopolization. Such a stack can be "federated," with multiple "demand-side platforms" (the part of the system that advertisers use to place bids for the right to show their ads) all communicating with the same "supply-side platform" (the part of the system publishers use to transmit opportunities to place ads).

This is also in the technopolitics of context ads: they do not tend toward the "efficiencies of scale" that lead to abusive monopolistic middlemen who steal from publishers and advertisers and sell out the public to fascists who want to arrest them and deport them to Salvadoran slave-labor camps.

When the ad-tech sector is composed of smaller companies that are more competitive with one another, it is much harder for any of them to collude to rig the market. That fierce competition also means it's harder to create a cozy conspiracy to lobby together to block privacy laws, and it leaves the companies with lower margins and less retained earnings to spend on those lobbying efforts.

Surveillance ads also promote all kinds of parasitic businesses. The fact that a publisher can serve ads related to users rather than the subject of their media means that sensationalistic and deceptive websites can sell ads that run against content that's totally unrelated to advertisers' products and services. And because it's cheaper to produce lies and garbage, these publishers can undercut the ad prices demanded by high quality outlets and let advertisers reach the same users. This is the whole reason that "chumboxes" exist:

Just as nuclear power favors a centralized state, surveillance advertising:

favors low-quality media over high-quality production;

provides authoritarian states with an off-the-books, warrantless source of surveillance data.

And just as solar allows for decentralized, anti-authoritarian societies, context advertising:

tends towards pluralism and diversity of voices;

favors high-quality media by reserving the lion's share of the rewards to the producers;

does not rely on a weak privacy environment;

does not militate for "democratizing spying" by issuing every person with a permanent advertising identifier.

There's nothing (necessarily) wrong with building systems to tell people about things you want them to know. There's nothing (necessarily) wrong with paying for media with ads. It's possible for both of these things to go awry, but they are not in and of themselves bad. They have contingent technopolitics.

But surveillance ads have completely unavoidable, absolutely ghastly technopolitics. Surveillance ads are bad for publishers, for advertisers, and for democracy. We should ban surveillance ads:

#1715. Replacing the old battery on rechargeable bike lights

Hello! Recently I needed bike lights for my bike. And I remembered that I already had rechargeable bike lights that I bought ten years ago, that I hadn’t tried in a long time. I tried to recharge them, but after fully charging them, they only worked for maybe 5 minutes before they turned off again.

I don’t know much about electronics, but I’ve been curious about whether it’s possible to fix old electronics for a long time, and this seemed like the perfect repair project because I might just need to replace the battery.

So I went to the local queer makerspace where I’m a member to use the soldering iron and try to do it! I don’t know much about electronics and this post does not contain any safety advice because I don’t know much about safety. I think it’s nice to do projects in a community space where you can get help.

step 1: cut it open

The bike light felt like it was made of silicone, so I cut open the silicone in a haphazard way along something that vaguely looked like a seam.

I definitely ripped some silicone in the process and it was pretty messy but I got it open and found the circuit board.

I don’t know the model number of the bike lights but there’s a photo of them at the end of the post.

step 2: remove the screws

There were some screws attaching things together so I removed them so I could get the circuit board out.

Mostly I tried to remove as few screws as possible because I was worried about losing them or not being able to put them back after. I probably put the screws in a bag or something.

step 3: get the circuit board out

I took out the circuit board. Here’s what it looked like:

You can see where the battery is attached, I think it’s left of RI3 and above Q2.

Here’s what the battery looked like:

step 4: desolder the battery

I’d never desoldered anything before, so I found the iFixit guide to desoldering and read it. Also I asked my friends Lee and Lauria for advice.

Here were the steps I ended up following based on the guide & the advice I got:

  1. Use a desoldering pump to remove most of the solder
  2. Once most of it is gone, kind of pull them apart to try to separate them
  3. Also try to avoid getting the battery too hot in the process by taking breaks to let it cool down. I’m not very good with a soldering iron so it took a while.
  4. The battery has an attachment that is welded to the top. For a while I thought I needed to remove this and it seemed impossible, but it turned out the replacement battery comes with that part so actually I was supposed to leave it alone.

step 5: identify the battery

In the picture of the battery in Step 3, you can see it says something like “3” and “LI???77”. There’s a piece of metal that I think is welded or something to the top of the battery. It seemed impossible and also maybe not smart to try to remove so I wasn’t sure how to find out what an “LI????77” was or how to order another one.

I’ve been trying to avoid using LLMs (though I will not get into that because I am exhausted by LLM discourse and I’m sure you are too), but I really had no idea how to figure out what the battery was so I asked an LLM. It gave the response “LIR2477”, which (when I looked it up) looked exactly the same as my battery so I figured that was plausible.

I would be interested to learn non-LLM ways to figure this out though. There must be a way. Lauria showed me how to use DigiKey’s search which was very cool though DigiKey didn’t have that part.

(edit: someone in the replies told me that this kind of coin cell battery is named according to its dimensions, and you can use plastic calipers to measure the dimensions of the battery. So I guess a non-LLM way would be to measure the battery with calipers and try to match it to something on the List of battery sizes Wikipedia page, though that page only mentions CR 2477 and not LIR 2477. It’s a good example of what’s fun for me about trying to avoid LLMs, this “List of battery sizes” page is super interesting and if I use an LLM I might never find it)

step 6: buy the battery

I went to AliExpress and ordered:

  1. 2 batteries (I had 2 bike lights and I wanted to fix them both)
  2. some silicone glue to glue things back together

I think the batteries were $3 each and the glue was $8.

step 7: solder the new batteries in and glue it back together

The parts took maybe 2 weeks to arive, and once they arrived, I went back to the makerspace and:

  • soldered in the new batteries
  • put the screws back in. The screws were very small and hard to hold, so at this point I dropped some screws on the ground and couldn’t find them because they were too small. So I just used fewer screws and hoped for the best.
  • used the glue to try to put everything back together.
  • Make a somewhat halfhearted attempt to clamp the parts I was gluing together

Then after waiting some amount of time for the glue to dry I took it home and waited 24 hours for the glue to cure.

Also I took the old batteries to somewhere nearby that accepts old batteries.

it works!

I still haven’t needed to recharge them (and tragically I had to order a new Mini USB cable because I got rid of all my Mini USB cables, so I’m still waiting for that), so I still don’t know for sure how long the lifetime of the new battery will be.

I thought it was really cool that I was able to do this with extremely minimal electronics skills! It cost about $20 CAD to buy the parts, and (whether or not the repair holds up, I’ll try to update this post in the future!), it was fun to try to repair something and learn something new.

#1723. ShinyHunters Extorted Boeing Spin-off Prior to Arrests

A teenager from Amman, Jordan suspected of leading the prolific data theft and extortion group ShinyHunters has been detained and is reportedly cooperating with the FBI to identify other members of the hacking gang. KrebsOnSecurity has learned that the suspect, who uses the hacker handle “Rey,” was detained as ShinyHunters was in the process of extorting a business unit recently divested by the global aerospace company Boeing, which manufactures the fleet of planes used by the employer of Rey’s father — Royal Jordanian Airlines.

The logo for Jeppesen ForeFlight, a business unit divested last year by the aerospace firm Boeing.

On October 3, Reuters cited three unnamed sources saying a suspected ShinyHunters member in Amman named Saif Al-din Khader was detained by Jordanian authorities and was cooperating with the FBI. KrebsOnSecurity identified Rey as Khader in a November 2025 profile, in which the young man admitted working with multiple ransomware groups.

Rey was featured again in a September 28 exclusive about the Dutch police arresting 24-year-old convicted cybercriminal Pepijn van der Stap on suspicion of aiding in data thefts and extortions by ShinyHunters. The story noted that immediately following the Dutchman’s arrest on the evening of September 15, Rey assumed control over the ShinyHunters brand and boasted publicly about stealing highly sensitive data from the FBI and extorting the ransomware group Cl0p.

Rey taunted both the FBI and Cl0p with memes posted to his longtime account on Twitter/X, while simultaneously including images of the avatar used by Van Der Stap’s former hacker alias “Umbreon” in an apparent attempt to frame the Dutchman for both hacks.

A taunting meme uploaded to Twitter/X by Rey on Sept. 22. A giant sized version of the Pokemon character Umbreon can be seen in the bottom left.

As noted in our September 28 report, ShinyHunters gained access to the FBI site and other victims by exploiting a vulnerability (CVE-2026-35273) in PeopleSoft, a software-as-a-service platform from the tech giant Oracle that is broadly used by companies to manage hiring and human resources, benefits and payroll. Oracle quickly issued a fix for CVE-2026-35273, which ShinyHunters first began exploiting as a zero-day in June, and at the time Mandiant released web application firewall rules intended for organizations that couldn’t apply the security update quickly enough.

ShinyHunters told BleepingComputer in June that the original goal behind exploiting the PeopleSoft vulnerability was to breach the FBI’s own PeopleSoft database, but the hackers said those attacks were unsuccessful for some reason. In recent weeks, however, ShinyHunters turned to a well-known URL-encoding trick to bypass Mandiant’s suggested web application firewall rules.

In a report released Sept. 25, security experts at Mandiant and the Google Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) confirmed that ShinyHunters had mass-exploited the PeopleSoft vulnerability to steal data from dozens of systems across a range of industries, including higher education, technology, healthcare, agriculture, transportation and government.

Reuters reported October 5 that the FBI has removed a contractor at Accenture over their failure to patch the FBI recruitment website hacked by ShinyHunters, which exposed sensitive data on more than 5,000 FBI personnel, including each’s person’s unit and specialization, as well as medical and psychiatric records.

Those sources said the FBI’s investigation into ShinyHunters gained renewed urgency with the group’s attempted extortion of the former Boeing unit, which allegedly included the theft of sensitive information that sources said could pose operational safety and security risks.

In a brief statement shared with KrebsOnSecurity, Boeing acknowledged the extortion attempts by ShinyHunters, and said the incident concerned data stolen from Jeppesen ForeFlight, a subsidiary that Boeing sold in November 2025 to the private equity firm Thoma Bravo for $10.55 billion.

“We are aware of claims by a threat actor regarding data allegedly associated with Boeing and our former subsidiary Jeppesen ForeFlight,” a Boeing spokesperson shared. “We are actively reviewing the matter with the Jeppesen ForeFlight team.”

A spokesperson for Jeppesen ForeFlight shared a written statement in response to questions, saying the company has seen no impact on their end. “Based on our investigation to date into this claim and proactive security posture, there was no impact to our operations or products.”

And since I’m using SQLite, any slow database query problem will show up on the CPU profile anyway.

Anyway I don’t want to get too far into site performance. Like I said it’s easy for me to get interested in profiling, but actually I know a lot about profiling and it’s not the most important thing for me to learn about.

#1728. Diesel prices are surging putting further pressure on Europe

Trucks, farms and industry are powered with diesel. Now prices are soaring due to refinery disruptions, supply shortages and geopolitical tensions. A potential US export ban could make Europe's diesel crunch even worse.

Even if you don't fill your own vehicle with diesel, its price has a big impact on your life.

While gasoline is mainly a consumer fuel for cars, diesel powers much of the wider economy, from freight transport and farming equipment to construction and industry.

That is why diesel shortages or price shocks can have a bigger economic impact than higher gasoline prices by raising the cost of moving goods and operating heavy machinery.

As businesses become strained, these higher energy costs will be passed on to consumers and add to a growing cost-of-living crisis and inflation. So what's driving global diesel prices higher?

The United States and China are among the world's largest diesel producers. China keeps much of it for domestic use, leaving the US as the world's biggest diesel exporter.

Still, the weekly average price of a gallon of diesel is now $6.52 (€5.73) in the country, according to the US Energy Information Administration, a big increase from a year ago when it averaged $3.74 per gallon or two years ago when it averaged $3.53 a gallon.

President Donald Trump recently called on Ukraine's president to stop targeting oil refineries in Russia "because he's causing a shortage of diesel."

Russia is one of the world's other biggest diesel suppliers. But since 2022, some of its oil refineries have been attacked as many as 15 times, according to the International Energy Agency.

The agency estimates that diesel production in Russia is down by nearly 30% from 2025 levels. Moscow has now restricted diesel exports to protect domestic supplies.

As Russian production declined, the US went into high gear to help make up the difference, but even this was not enough. And on September 22, Trump announced that his administration was also considering a diesel export ban.

Such a move is likely an attempt to bring down domestic prices ahead of the November midterm elections since energy prices and affordability have become important campaign issues.

Leading energy lobby groups have come out against such a plan, and many industry experts think a ban would be slow to bring down prices or could backfire by pushing US refiners to reduce production, push up prices, or both.

For Europe, more expensive diesel could have dramatic consequences.

"Decades of policy incentives, such as tax advantages, left Europe's vehicle fleet far more diesel-heavy than regions like the US," said Georg Zachmann, a senior fellow at Brussels-based Bruegel think tank.

"As a result, the EU is structurally long on gasoline, which it exports, and short on diesel, which it must import," added Zachmann, who specializes in energy and climate policies.

In the EU, just over 38% of passenger cars are diesel and road transport, including freight, makes up 77% of total diesel and gas oil consumption, according to a briefing published by Transport & Environment, a Brussels-based advocacy group.

This reliance on diesel-powered transport "leaves drivers and businesses particularly exposed to oil and fuel price shocks," wrote Juliette Egal, the briefing's lead author.

The NGO calculated that EU drivers are paying €30 more for a 50-liter tank of diesel on average since the start of the war in Iran. An average German long-haul truck driver is paying an extra €236 a week.

Increased fuel costs for EU road transport add up to €270 million a day — of that, €203 million is for diesel, according to the briefing. Overall, the EU economy has seen additional costs of €40 billion from road diesel since the beginning of the war.

In the near term, Russia's crude oil processing volumes are the single fastest-moving lever, said Skip York, a nonresident fellow at Rice University's Center for Energy Studies in Houston, Texas.

"Each new wave of Ukrainian strikes shows up in diesel prices within days, unlike Hormuz, which has been running at a depressed but relatively stable flow rate for months," he added.

#1732. Learning a few things about running SQLite

Hello! I’ve been working on a Django site recently, and I decided to use SQLite as the database. When I was getting started with using SQLite as database for a website I read a bunch of blog posts about how it is totally fine to use SQLite in production for a small site and I think it is totally fine, but what I did not fully appreciate is that SQLite is still a database, databases are complicated, and I do not know a lot about operating databases.

So here are a couple of small things I’ve been learning about running SQLite. This is the 4th website I’ve used SQLite for, and I think this one is harder because with the power of the Django ORM I’ve been making the database do more work than I was previously without Django.

I started by turning on WAL mode like all the blog posts said to do and hoping for the best.

ANALYZE is apparently important

Today I was running a query (using SQLite’s FTS5 for full-text search) on a table with 4000 rows and it took 5 seconds. That seemed wrong to me: computers are fast!

It turned out that what I needed to do was to run ANALYZE! Immediately the problem query went from taking 5 seconds to like 0.05 seconds (or some other number small enough that I didn’t care to investigate further). I still don’t know exactly what went wrong in the query plan, but my best guess is that it was some sort of accidentally quadratic thing.

ANALYZE generates “statistics” (I guess about the number of rows in each table? and presumably other things?) so that the query planner can make better choices.

Maybe one day I’ll learn to read a query plan.

cleaning up the database is tricky

Occasionally I’ve run into situations where I accidentally put a bunch of rows in my database that I don’t want to be there (for example completed tasks from django-tasks-db), and I want to clean them up.

What’s happened to me a few times in this case is:

  1. I run some kind of command to clean up the rows
  2. The command takes more than 5 seconds, since there are a lot of rows (though I still have some questions about why these DELETE statements are so slow honestly, maybe there’s a bunch of Python code running inside a transaction, I’m not sure)
  3. One of the other workers tries to write the database while this is happening, and times out after 5 seconds (I have a timeout of 5 seconds set)
  4. The worker crashes because it couldn’t write to the database and the VM shuts down

My approach so far has been to just do these cleanup operations in small batches so that I don’t need to do database queries that take more than 5 seconds to run. This whole experience has given me more of an appreciation for why someone might want to use a “real” database like Postgres which can have more than one writer at the same time though.

Maybe in the future I’ll just take the site down for scheduled maintenance instead when I need to do this kind of thing, but I haven’t figured out a workflow for that yet.

no notes on performance of ORM queries yet

So far I’ve been using Django’s ORM to make any query I want without paying any attention at all to query performance and it’s mostly been going okay other than the ANALYZE thing. The database is pretty small (maybe 10000 rows?) and I expect it to stay pretty small forever, so I’m hoping that that plan will keep working.

backing up sqlite

I’ve done SQLite backups a couple of ways. I don’t think I’ve actually tested restoring from my backups but I do usually try to monitor them with a dead man’s switch.

way 1: restic

sqlite3 /data/calendar.db "VACUUM INTO '/tmp/calendar.sqlite'"
gzip /tmp/calendar.sqlite

# Upload backup to S3
# Sometimes the backup gets OOM killed and so it stays locked, do an unlock
restic -r s3://s3.amazonaws.com/some_bucket/ unlock
# Do the backup & prune old backups
restic -r s3://s3.amazonaws.com/some_bucket/ backup /tmp/calendar.sqlite.gz
restic -r s3://s3.amazonaws.com/some_bucket/ snapshots
restic -r s3://s3.amazonaws.com/some_bucket/ forget -l 1 -H 6 -d 2 -w 2 -m 2 -y 2
restic -r s3://s3.amazonaws.com/some_bucket/ prune

way 2: litestream

I started trying out Litestream recently because I felt like doing incremental backups might be more efficient: my restic backups were sometimes getting OOM killed, and I was a bit tired of it. Basically I just write a config file and run:

litestream replicate -config litestream.yml

I set retention: 400h in my config file in an attempt to retain some amount of history of the database but I have no idea if it works.

I’ve been backing up to AWS, which is always a pain because it’s annoying to navigate the AWS console to generate credentials. Maybe one day I’ll move away to some other S3-compatible alternative.

you can use multiple databases

My current project only has one database, but one trick I used with Mess with DNS was to split the tables into three separate database files because I didn’t actually need my tables to be in the same db. I think it was helpful.

Mess with DNS has been running on SQLite for 4 years now (since 2022) and it’s been great, I think the move from Postgres was a great choice for that project.

that’s all!

It’s always kind of fun to see how long it takes me to learn sort of basic things about the technologies I’m using. I think I used SQLite for a web project for the first time in 2022 and I only learned that ANALYZE existed today! I imagine in a year or two I’ll be learning about some other very basic feature.

#1740. Moving away from Tailwind, and learning to structure my CSS

Hello! 8 years ago, I wrote excitedly about discovering Tailwind.

At that time I really had no idea how to structure my CSS code and given the choice between a pile of complete chaos and Tailwind, I was really happy to choose Tailwind. It helped me make a lot of tiny sites!

I spent the last week or so migrating a couple of sites away from Tailwind and towards more semantic HTML + vanilla CSS, and it was SO fun and SO interesting, so here are some things I learned!

As usual I’m not a full-time frontend developer and so all of my CSS learning has happened in fits and starts over many years.

it turns out Tailwind taught me a lot

When I started thinking about structuring CSS, I was intimidated at first: I’m not very good at structuring my CSS! But then I started reading blog posts talking about how to structure CSS (like A whole cascade of layers or How I write CSS in 2024) and I realized a couple of things:

  1. Every CSS code base has a bunch of different things going on (layouts! fonts! colours! common components!)
  2. It’s extremely useful to have systems or guidelines to manage each of those things, otherwise things descend into chaos
  3. Tailwind has systems for some of these, and I already know those systems! Maybe I can imitate the systems I like!

For example, Tailwind has:

  • a reset stylesheet
  • a colour palette
  • a font scale

the systems I’m going to talk about

I’m going to talk about a few aspects of my CSS codebase and my thoughts so far what kind of rules I want to impose on the codebase for each one. Some of them are copied from Tailwind and some aren’t.

  1. reset
  2. components
  3. colours
  4. font sizes
  5. utility classes
  6. the base
  7. spacing
  8. responsive design
  9. the build system

1. reset

I just copied Tailwind’s “preflight styles” by going into tailwind.css and copying the first 200 lines or so.

I noticed that I’ve developed a relationship with Tailwind’s CSS reset over time, for example Tailwind sets box-sizing: border-box on every element (which means that an element’s width includes its padding and border):

* { box-sizing: border-box; }

I think it would be a real adjustment for me to switch to writing CSS without these, and I’m sure there are lots of other things in the Tailwind reset (like html {line-height: 1.5;}) that I’m subconsciously used to and don’t even realize are there.

2. components

This next part is the bulk of the CSS!

The idea here is to organize CSS by “components”, in a way that’s spiritually related to Vue or React components. (though there might not actually be any Javascript at all in the site)

Basically the idea is that:

  1. Each “component” has a unique class
  2. The CSS for one component never overrides the CSS for any other component
  3. Each component has its own CSS file

So editing the CSS for one component won’t mysteriously break something in another component. And probably like 80% of the CSS that I would actually want to change is in various component files, so if I’m editing a 100-line component, I just have to think about those 100 lines. It’s way easier for me to think about.

For example, this HTML might be the .zine “component”.

<figure class="zine horizontal">
    <img src="whatever.jpg">
</figure>

And the CSS looks something like this, using nested selectors:

.zine {
  ...
  &.horizontal {
    ...
  }
  &.vertical {
    ...
  }
  &:hover {
    ...
  }
}

I haven’t done anything programmatic (like web components or @scope) that ensures that components won’t interfere with each other, but just having a convention and trying my best already feels like a big improvement.

Next: conventions to maintain some consistency across the site and keep these components in line with each other!

3. colours

colours.css has a bunch of variables like this which I can use as necessary. Colour is really hard and I didn’t want to revisit my use of colour in this refactor, so I left this alone.

The only guideline I’m trying to enforce here is that all colours used in the site are listed in this file.

:root {
  --pink: #fea0c2;
  --pink-light: #F9B9B9;
  --red: #f91a55;
  --orange: rgb(222, 117, 31);
  ...
}

4. font sizes

One thing I appreciated about Tailwind was that if I wanted to set a font size, I could just think “hm, I want the text to be big”, write text-lg, and be done with it! And maybe if it’s not big enough I’d use xl or 2xl instead. No trying to remember whether I’m using em or px or rem.

So I defined a bunch of variables, taken from Tailwind, like this:

  --size-xs: 0.75rem;
  --line-height-xs: 1rem;

  --size-sm: 0.875rem;
  --line-height-sm: 1.25rem;

Then if I want to set a font size, I can do it like this. It’s a little more verbose than Tailwind but I’m happy with it for now.

h3 {
  font-size: var(--size-lg);
  line-height: var(--line-height-lg);
}

5. utilities

There are some things like buttons that appear in many different components. I’m calling these “utilities”.

I copied some utility classes from Tailwind (like .sr-only for things that should only appear for screenreader users).

This section is pretty small and I try to be careful about making changes here.

6. the base

“base” styles are styles that apply across the whole site that I chose myself. I have to keep this section really small because I’m not confident enough to enforce a lot of styles across the whole site. These are the only two I feel okay about right now, and I might change the <section> one:

/* put a 950px column in the middle of each <section> */
section {
  --inner-width: 950px;
  padding: 3rem max(1rem, (100% - var(--inner-width))/2);
}

a {
  color: var(--orange);
}

I think for the base styles it’s going to be easiest for me to work kind of bottom up – first start with almost nothing in the base styles, and then move some styles from the components into base styles as I identify common things I want.

7. spacing

I haven’t completely worked out an approach to managing padding and margins yet. I’m definitely trying to be more principled than how I was doing it in Tailwind though, where I would just haphazardly put padding and margins everywhere until it looked the way I wanted.

Right now I’m working towards making the outer layout components in charge of spacing as much as possible. For example if I have a <section> with a bunch of children that I want to have space between them, I might use this to space the children evenly:

section > *+* {
  margin-top: 1rem;
}

Some inspiration blog posts:

  • the owl selector
  • “no outer margin”

8. responsive design: use more grid!

The way I was doing responsive design in Tailwind was to use a lot of media queries. Tailwind has this md:text-xl syntax that means “apply the text-xl style at sizes md or larger”.

I’m trying something pretty different now, which is to make more flexible CSS grid layouts that don’t need as many breakpoints. This is hard but it’s really interesting to learn about what’s possible with grid, and it’s a good example of something that I don’t think is possible with Tailwind.

For example, I’ve been learning about how to use auto-fit to automatically use 2 columns on a big screen and 1 column on a small screen like this:

  display: grid;
  grid-template-columns: repeat(auto-fit, minmax(min(100%, 400px), max-content));
  justify-content: center;

I also used grid-template-areas a lot which is an amazing feature that I don’t think you can use with Tailwind.

Some inspiration:

  • A responsive grid layout with no media queries from CSS Tricks

9. the build system: esbuild

In development, I don’t need a build system: CSS now has both built in import statements, like this:

@import "reset.css";
@import "typography.css";
@import "colors.css";

and built in nested selectors, like this:

.page {
  h2 { ...}
}

If I want, I can use esbuild to bundle the CSS file for production. That looks something like this.

esbuild style.css --bundle --loader:.svg=dataurl  --loader:.woff2=file --outfile=/tmp/out.css

Even though I usually avoid using CSS and JS build systems, I don’t mind using esbuild (which I wrote about in 2021 here) because it’s based on web standards and because it’s a static Go binary.

why migrate away from Tailwind?

A few people asked why I was migrating away from Tailwind. A few factors that contributed are:

  • Tailwind has become much more reliant on a build system since 2018, I think it’s impossible (?) to use newer versions of Tailwind without using a build system. So I’ve been using Tailwind v2 for years. (there’s also litewind apparently)
  • It’s always been true that you’re supposed to use Tailwind with a build system, but I’ve never really done that, so I have 2.8MB tailwind.min.css files (270K gzipped) in a lot of my projects and it feels a little silly.
  • I’m a lot better at CSS than I was when I started using Tailwind
  • Ultimately Tailwind is limiting: if you want to do Weird Stuff in your CSS, it’s not always possible with Tailwind. Those limits can be extremely useful (a lot of this post is about me reimplementing some of Tailwind’s limits!) but at this point I’d like to be able to pick and choose.
  • I ended up with sites that mixed both vanilla CSS and Tailwind in the same project and that was not fun to maintain
  • I got curious about what writing more semantic HTML would feel like.

CSS features I’m curious about

While doing this I learned about a lot of CSS features that I didn’t use but am curious about learning about one day:

  • @layer (from A Whole Cascade of Layers)
  • @scope) (especially this example of how to use @scope in a “component” CSS design from the specification!)
  • container queries
  • subgrid

one last reason I moved away from Tailwind

I’ve been talking a lot in this post about what I learned from using Tailwind, and that’s all true.

But I read this post 3 years ago called Tailwind and the Femininity of CSS that really stuck with me. I honestly probably started out with an attitude towards CSS a little like that post describes:

They’ve heard it’s simple, so they assume it’s easy. But then when they try to use it, it doesn’t work. It must be the fault of the language, because they know that they are smart, and this is supposed to be easy.

But in the last 10 years I’ve learned to really love and respect CSS as a technology.

So I decided years ago that I wanted to react to “CSS is hard” by getting better at CSS and taking it seriously as a technology, instead of devaluing it. Doing that changed everything for me: I learned that so many of my frustrations (“centering is impossible”) had been addressed in CSS a long time ago, and that also what “centering” means is not always straightforward and it makes sense that there are many ways to do it. CSS is hard because it’s solving a hard problem!

I’ve been so impressed by the new CSS features that have been built in the last 10-15 years (some of which I’ve talked about in this post!) and how they make it easier to use CSS, and spending the time to improve my CSS skills has been a really cool experience.

And that post made me feel like Tailwind contributes to the devaluing of CSS expertise, and like that’s not something I want to be a part of, even if Tailwind has been a useful tool for me personally. Especially in this time of LLMs where it feels more important than ever to value humans’ expertise.

  • Classic rock, Mario Kart, and why we can’t agree on Tailwind

#1745. Pluralistic: Expedia amazoned hotel rooms (15 Oct 2026)

Pluralistic: Daily links from Cory Doctorow

No matter where you shop, you're getting screwed by Amazon. Amazon has found a way to make everything you buy – at Target, at a mom-and-pop shop, even direct from the manufacturer – more expensive. And they're not the only one: Expedia's done the same thing, but for hotel rooms.

America's affluent top decile – the 10% of richest Americans – do the majority of America's shopping, a phenomenon that has only been accelerated by America's precipitous slide into oligarchy, which has exhausted the savings and the credit-card limits and the home equity of the majority of Americans, who increasingly can't afford to buy anything:

If you want to sell things in America, you must sell them in the place where the top decile shops, and that's Amazon. For merchants, that is an incredibly expensive proposition: 51-60% of every dollar you spend with an independent merchant on Amazon is claimed by Amazon through junk fees, listing fees, commissions, processing fees and other scams:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/04/cheating-at-fraud/#absentee-rentier

Merchants don't have 51-60% margins they can afford to turn over to Amazon. To remain viable while selling on Amazon, they have to raise prices. Amazon doesn't mind if prices go up, so long as Amazon's price remains competitive. So Amazon imposes a requirement on its suppliers called "most favored nation," which requires sellers to offer their best price on Amazon:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/02/25/most-favored-nation/#price-fixing

Practically speaking, that means that when you raise your price on Amazon, you must raise your prices everywhere by the same amount, which means that no matter where you shop (Target, Walmart, the corner store or direct from the factory), you pay the Amazon Tax.

The Amazon Tax is a prime (you should forgive the expression) example of enshittification, in which an intermediary (a business that sits between buyers and sellers, AKA, "a platform") screws both customers and sellers to benefit itself.

But Amazon isn't the only example of a powerful digital intermediary that has used its market power to impose an economy-wide tax. Wherever you look online, you find digital giants who grew the way Amazon grew (buying all of their competitors) and then did what Amazon did (squeezing buyers and sellers, making prices higher everywhere).

That's what happened with Expedia. Almost every travel site you can find online has been purchased by Expedia (Hotels.com, Travelocity, and Orbitz), and the ones that aren't Expedia subsidiaries were acquired by its main rival, Priceline (Booking.com, Kayak, Agoda and even Opentable). Nearly all the hotel rooms are booked by a tiny minority of travelers – largely that same top decile that Amazon has so thoroughly captured and locked in – and hoteliers can't live without their business, and they're almost all Expedia and/or Priceline customers. Expedia accounts for 80% of all hotel bookings by dollar value.

Expedia's position, in other words, is basically Amazon's position, so Expedia is doing exactly what Amazon is doing: they charge huge fees to hotels, and then force the hotels to raise their prices everywhere through a "most favored nation" deal:

https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.wawd.369601/gov.uscourts.wawd.369601.1.0.pdf

But – as Lee Hepner writes for the BIG newsletter – Expedia has figured out ways to out-Amazon Amazon. They've copied one of Amazon's sleaziest businesses, and made it worse:

https://www.thebignewsletter.com/p/how-expedia-rigs-travel-markets-and

Here's Amazon's sleaziest commercial practice: when you search Amazon, the top results for your search aren't the best matches for your queries; they're products sold by the companies willing to pay the most for search placement, in a "real-time auction" that runs every time you search. The cost of winning that auction comes out of your pocket through higher prices: the first result for an Amazon query is, on average, 29% more expensive than the best match. The top row is 25% more expensive. On average, the best match for your query is 17 positions down the screen, on the second page, where almost no one ever bothers to scroll:

https://pluralistic.net/2023/11/03/subprime-attention-rent-crisis/#euthanize-rentiers

This rip-off is Amazon's most profitable line of business, more profitable than AWS or Prime. It makes the company more than $80b/year. And – incredibly – Expedia's found a way to make it even worse.

Like Amazon, Expedia auctions off its search results. They call this their "VIP listing" program. Practically speaking, hotels have to pay for this, because it boosts booking rates by 42% (the first ten results on an Expedia search-results page get 62% of the clicks). VIP listing is expensive for the merchant – but it's even more expensive for you and me.

That's because Expedia requires hotels in its VIP listings programs to raise their prices on non-Expedia sites by 10-25%…INDEFINITELY.

Let me repeat that: to stay in business, hotels need to be on Expedia. To stay in business on Expedia, hotels have to raise their prices to pay Expedia's junk fees, but they have to raise their prices everywhere else. But to really stay in business on Expedia, hotels have to use the VIP program, which is even more expensive. And – one more time – hotels in the VIP program have to jack up their prices everywhere else, INDEFINITELY. This means that, throughout the economy, hotel prices are rising at a superinflationary rate, because Expedia – who command 80% of the market – are pushing those prices up.

This goes a long way to explaining why US hotel rates rose 58% from a 2020 average of $103/night to a 2025 average of $162/night. It explains why hotel prices rose by 5% last year, which is double the rate of inflation. And this is why Expedia stock is up 65% since the start of the Iran war, a fact that Expedia CEO Ariane Gorin attributes to "higher margins" from "higher prices":

https://s202.q4cdn.com/757635260/files/doc_financials/2026/q2/Q2-2026-Earnings-Presentation-Final.pdf

In his coverage of the Expedia lawsuit – which brought many of these details to light – Hepner notes that hotels themselves have undergone near-total cartelization, with Hilton, Marriott, and Choice International owning the majority of all hotels after a string of acquisitions that went largely unchallenged by slumbering antitrust regulators around the world. These consolidated chains have also jacked up prices and enshittified their service, slashing housekeeping and check-in staff and allowing their facilities to slip into disrepair.

This, too, is characteristic of monopolization: whenever a monopoly takes over part of its supply chain, the rest of the supply chain consolidates to prevent itself from being ripped off by the new giant. This is the story of publishers consolidating to defend themselves from Borders/B&N, Walmart and Costco, then Amazon; and it's the story of literary agencies consolidating to resist the power of those overgrown publishers.

And it's the story of health-care, which saw consolidation in pharmaceuticals leading to mergers among hospitals, who screwed the insurers, who also consolidated.

In every case, it's the workers and the people who rely on them that get the shitty end of the stick. Hotel guests, readers and patients pay more for inferior products. Writers, editors, doctors, nurses, hotel staff and housekeepers get lower pay and worse working-conditions:

https://pluralistic.net/2024/12/18/loose-flapping-ends/#luigi-has-a-point

There is nothing wrong with the idea of an intermediary. Most writers can't publish and sell their own books, and few travelers can navigate finding hotels in strange cities without help. The problem of intermediaries is that when intermediaries gain monopoly power – by buying their rivals and capturing buyers and sellers – they are able to squeeze both. As Tim Wu says, this is the moment when a company that's supposed to be helping becomes a predator, acquires "main character syndrome" and makes every transaction all about the middleman, at the expense of the people buying and selling:

https://pluralistic.net/2026/08/05/absentee-landlord/

There are so many reasons for inflation and the cost of living crisis: wage stagnation, wars of choice in Iran, environmental cataclysm after environmental cataclysm. But under cover of these real factors is a cohort of what Matt Stoller calls "economic termites," parasites that have infested the walls of our economy and are devouring it from within.

You can't solve this by shopping. To fix this, we need systemic action: the corporate death penalty for the companies that are consuming the structural members of our society.

#1746. Training AIs to Solve CAPTCHAs

Interesting research:

Abstract: Vision-language models (VLMs) can solve visual CAPTCHAs without task-specific training, but the agents built on them approach every challenge from scratch. For such an agent, the hundredth instance of a familiar puzzle costs as much time and compute as the first. Specialized detectors invert the trade-off, answering in milliseconds but only for categories they were trained on. Neither improves with exposure. We study what changes when a solver improves with use. Our system pairs a fine-tuned YOLOv8 detector with an open-weight VLM behind a confidence-based router, and runs entirely from screenshots and operating-system input events, with no browser automation or DOM access. It reaches 85.4% overall and 84.2% macro accuracy across 16 classes, exceeding either component alone. Every answer VLM produces also serves as a training label, so the detector absorbs categories it was never trained for, typically after one or two encounters and without human annotation. The same loop also repairs it. A CAPTCHA operator can perturb images against the publicly released detector and drive its accuracy to 0%, but the perturbations leave VLM untouched, and its labels let the detector recover. Under a year-long simulated arms race in which the CAPTCHA operator re-crafts its perturbations each month, the solver recovers every round, and a cheap ~70%-accurate open-weight teacher hardens it as effectively as a perfect oracle. Visual CAPTCHA defenses that assume a failing bot stays failing therefore understate how quickly an adaptive solver returns...

#1747. Best Microsoft Surface Laptop (2026): Which Models to Buy and Avoid

Microsoft’s Surface Line has a product to fit a variety of needs and budgets, including flexible 2-in-1 tablet-laptops. Some very popular multiplayer games still aren't compatible at all (such as PUBG and League of Legends), though Nvidia says it's working on it. The Surface Laptop Ultra is for someone who wants to dabble in content creation, gaming, and AI on their work laptop. And yes, if that sounds like the M5 Pro MacBook Pro, that's the point.

Like every Surface tablet, you also have to pay extra for the corresponding keyboard or Surface Pen.

Another wrinkle: Microsoft isn’t running a typical media review program for these new devices. Unlike nearly every high-end laptop a major company releases, Microsoft isn’t sending review units to independent media for testing, according to a Microsoft spokesperson. That doesn’t necessarily make these devices inherently bad, but it’s a very uncommon choice and, frankly, not a good sign.

It has a more basic display, and frustratingly, it's not even in a 3:2 aspect ratio. And $950 for a laptop with only 8 GB of RAM is absurd. Microsoft Surface Laptop 13 (2025): I haven’t tested this model yet, but it’s hard to recommend given the price alone. The 256-GB base configuration is sold out on Amazon, while Microsoft sells it for a whopping $950. That's way too expensive to even consider.

Microsoft Surface Go 3: These days, there isn’t a good reason to pick up any Surface device older than the 2024 models. Shockingly, though, you can still buy the Surface Go 3 new. It costs $700, and it comes with only 8 GB of RAM and 128 GB of storage. Even in 2026, that’s insanely overpriced for the specs.

Microsoft Surface Laptop for Business: I haven’t tested this model yet, either, but it’s currently the only way to get an Intel-powered Surface Laptop. It’s been two years since the Snapdragon X platform launched alongside the Surface Laptop 7th Edition and Surface Pro 11th Edition. #1748. Data Broker Radaris Loses Domains in Privacy Fight

The consumer data broker Radaris.com has long had a reputation for ignoring requests to remove personal information from its vast empire of people-search services online. That reputation caught up with the company recently in a lawsuit alleging Radaris violated a New Jersey privacy law that provides for hefty fines against data brokers that publish personal information on state law enforcement officials. In the face of repeated stonewalling and prevarication by attorneys for Radaris, the judge in the case ordered that radaris.com and more than a dozen other data broker domains be transferred to the plaintiffs.

The radaris.com website, prior to the domain transfer to Atlas.

In February 2024, Radaris was sued by Atlas Data Privacy Corp, a company that has been pursuing data brokers alleged to be violating a New Jersey statute called Daniel’s Law. The statute allows state law enforcement officials, government personnel, judges and their families to have their information completely removed from commercial data brokers and people-search services, and provides for fines of $1,000 per violation against companies that ignore removal requests.

Less than a month after Atlas sued Radaris, KrebsOnSecurity published a deep dive into the Radaris co-founders — Igor and Dmitry Lubarsky (also spelled Lybarsky) — Russian-born brothers living in Massachusetts who operate a dizzying array of people-search companies as well as a number of Russian language dating services and affiliate programs.

Attorneys for the Lubarsky brothers threatened to sue for defamation if the story wasn’t removed and an apology issued. Their attorney asserted that our reporting was wildly inaccurate, and that the true owners of the company were Ukrainians living in Ukraine.

The Lubarsky brothers Dmitry or “Dan” (left) and Gary/Igor.

KrebsOnSecurity doubled down and showed how the Lubarsky brothers built and operated Radaris and other data broker companies using a fictitious CEO’s name. Our follow-up story noted that Radaris’s attorney — a lawyer with the Boston Law Group named Val Gurvits — admitted his clients had invented the CEO pseudonym “Gary Norden,” and that Radaris also had issued multiple press releases over the years that quoted the fake CEO while seeking money from potential investors.

Attorneys for Radaris waited until the last minute to appear in court and contest what was all but certain to be a default judgment in favor of the plaintiffs, and then told the court that Atlas had failed to serve the real owners and operators of Radaris and several of its sister data broker companies.

Atlas re-filed the lawsuit in June 2025, this time dramatically expanding the number of Radaris family data brokers accused of violating Daniel’s Law. Matt Adkisson, president and CEO of Atlas, said Radaris turned to a tried-and-true playbook: Delaying in court until the last possible minute, and playing shell games with Radaris’s true country of origin and the individuals listed as owners and operators of these sites.

“We refer to this period as their island-hopping phase. Privacy policies changed constantly, and new entities kept appearing from places like the Marshall Islands, the British Virgin Islands, and Seychelles,” Adkisson told KrebsOnSecurity. “Behind the scenes, it felt like a shell game. Defense lawyers told the court that certain entities merely operated the domains and were the proper parties to sue. But by the time a judgment neared, those entities would be discarded and new entities would appear. Meanwhile, the lawyers claimed the other entities that actually owned the domains should not be held responsible.”

Adkisson said when the defendants updated their terms of service to state that Radaris was suddenly managed by a company in the Marshall Islands, Atlas hired an investigator in that country and soon learned the brand new entity that Radaris claimed was managing the company didn’t even exist yet.

Mr. Gurvits stepped forward as Radaris’s attorney in a class action lawsuit the company temporarily lost in 2017 because it never contested the claim in court. When the plaintiffs told the judge they couldn’t collect on the $7.5 million default judgment, the court ordered the domain registry Verisign to transfer the radaris.com domain name to the plaintiffs.

Mr. Gurvits appealed that verdict, arguing the lawsuit hadn’t named the actual owners of the Radaris domain name — a Cyprus company called Bitseller Expert Limited — and thus taking the domain away would be a violation of their due process rights.

The judge in the 2017 case ruled in Radaris’ favor — halting the domain transfer — and told the plaintiffs they could refile their complaint. Soon after, the operator of Radaris changed from Bitseller to Andtop Company, an entity formed (PDF) in the Marshall Islands in Oct. 2020. The plaintiffs never re-filed their lawsuit.

A mind map of various entities tied to Radaris and the company’s co-founders. Click to enlarge.

“That seemed to be their modus operandi,” said Raj Parikh, a partner at PEM Law in New Jersey who handles most of the Daniel’s Law litigation for Atlas. “In the past, they won by attrition. Plaintiffs’ attorneys tired of the procedural games and just gave up. That strategy worked for a decade, and it probably would have worked in this case too, since any financial recovery from foreign actors will be difficult. But we were acutely aware of the threat this website posed to law enforcement officers and other public officials in New Jersey, and decided early on to commit whatever time and resources were necessary to remove that threat.”

On August 26, the judge in the New Jersey case found the defendants were given multiple chances to appear and defend the claims against them but had failed to do so. Mr. Gurvits declined to comment on the case, saying it had been assigned to another attorney, a Mr. Victor Worms. In response to questions, Mr. Worms asserted the New Jersey court transferred Radaris.com to Atlas as part of a default judgment against Radaris.com, which is not a legal entity.

“We have made a motion to vacate that default judgment on the grounds that it is void since a non-entity has no legal capacity to sue or be sued,” Worms replied. “We also intend to pursue all appropriate appeals because we believe the transfer of Radaris.com amounts to a forfeiture in violation of various constitutional principles.”

While radaris.com still comes up prominently in results when searching online for U.S. residents by name, the domain no longer sells detailed personal dossiers on millions of Americans. Its homepage now displays a notice from Atlas, as well as links to our previous reporting on Radaris.

Atlas told KrebsOnSecurity that it has obtained more than 10,000 emails and documents in the course of litigation, and that those messages confirm our previous reporting on the owners and operators of Radaris and its myriad companies.

Atlas said the emails clearly establish that the nominal legal vehicles — Radaris America, Inc.; Bitseller Expert Limited; Digital Orbit Corp; Core Solutions Group Inc; Lucky Solutions Inc; Virtura Corp; Veripages Inc.; Nuform Solutions Inc.; Growth Data Advisors Inc.; Property Experts, Inc — are all administered by the same three or four people from the same mailboxes, share one bank or payment card set, and are all managed from one virtual office address.

“The corpus establishes, with documentary evidence generated independently by banks, payment processors, hosting providers, registrars, software-as-a-service vendors and the operators’ own systems, that radaris.com and at least twenty-five other people-search websites are one operation run by a small Boston-area group whose administrative, financial and technical functions sit on the difive.com mail domain and its successors (centerex.com, scienteco.com, eprofit.com, realmo.com, pub360.com),” reads a summary shared by Atlas.

Atlas said the emails show Radaris.com earns approximately $42,000 a month, while Veripages.com earns around $45,000 monthly via its partnership with the Lifetime Value Company, a marketing and advertising firm whose brands include PeopleLooker, PeopleSmart, NumberGuru, and Bumper, a car history site.

According to Atlas, the emails also showed the Radaris family of websites earns as much as $25,000 each month from their partnership with Onerep, a company that claims to help people remove their information from people-search sites. In March 2024, KrebsOnSecurity revealed how the Belarusian founder of Onerep had launched and operated dozens of people-search sites over the years and was continuing to operate one of them (Nuwber), effectively spreading the disease and selling the cure.

The domain radaris.com now redirects to this notice from Atlas about the court-ordered domain transfer.

All told, the New Jersey court has so far transferred 14 domain names from the Radaris family of companies to Atlas. Radaris.com now redirects to a notice of the court-ordered domain transfer.

The Radaris family of companies is still potentially facing fines of $1,000 per alleged violation of Daniel’s Law. For the time being, however, Daniel’s Law is facing a constitutional challenge from virtually all of the 150 other consumer data broker firms being sued by Atlas.

The data broker industry responded by having at least 70 of the Atlas lawsuits moved to federal court, challenging the New Jersey statute as overly broad and a violation of the First Amendment. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit has not yet issued a decision on the constitutional challenge, but either way the case is widely expected to be appealed all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court.

Meanwhile, at least 14 other states have now passed laws modeled after the New Jersey statute, with more states considering similar measures. However, West Virginia’s Daniel’s Law was ruled facially unconstitutional under the First Amendment by a federal district court in August 2025.

Justin Sherman is a privacy expert and author of the forthcoming book “The Middlemen,” which examines how the data broker industry powers modern surveillance. Sherman said federal lawmakers have long faced intense lobbying by the technology industry against more restrictive U.S. data privacy laws, but that many powerful industries are now working against passing comprehensive data privacy legislation.

“These days at the federal level, add in the intense amount of lobbying against these laws from social media companies, big tech, cryptocurrency firms, and now AI proponents in the mix who claim that limiting their data scraping is somehow going to collapse the whole U.S. economy under Chinese rule,” he said.

Sherman said people-search companies will continue to thrive unless and until Congress enacts meaningful consumer privacy and data protection laws that are relevant to life in the 21st century. That’s because virtually all state privacy laws exempt records that might be considered “public” or “government” documents, including voting registries, property filings, marriage certificates, motor vehicle records, criminal records, court documents, death records, professional licenses, bankruptcy filings, and more.

At least 25 states have passed or implemented laws requiring age verification for residents seeking to access adult content online, but there is no federal law that limits how the companies that are scanning everyone’s drivers license can use, share or keep the data provided. Had such restrictions been enshrined in law, we may have avoided the recent breach at IDScan.net, which exposed the drivers license information on more than 153 million Americans when the records were briefly turned into a point-and-click identity theft service on the dark web.

“The average person can look at Daniel’s Law and have a perfectly normal reaction, which is that everyone should be covered, not just police and judges,” Sherman said. “But we don’t need more wake-up calls. We’ve had eight million wake-up calls already on the need for better privacy laws. The lack of comprehensive federal privacy law is not for a lack of knowledge, and anyone claiming otherwise is either not reading the news or kidding themselves.”
