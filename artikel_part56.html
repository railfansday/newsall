#2752. New water lily can be seen from space

Published Oct 18, 2026 11:12 AM EDT

One cold December day in 2021, Colorado carnivorous plant grower Jeremiah Harris shared some pictures of wild Amazonian giant water lilies (Victoria amazonica) plants in the Inírida River in Guainía, Colombia to Instagram. There’s just one problem. No known Victoria water lily species grow there.

When water lily expert Carlos Magdalena saw the post on Instagram, he suspected a new plant species could be involved. To investigate the matter, he used satellite imagery to remotely search the Inírida River and its surrounding lakes and confirmed the presence of Victorias.

And yes, you can see these enormous plants from space. Victorias grow the world’s second largest single plant leaf.

Magdalena is a botanical and scientific research horticulturist at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew in the U.K. Along with his colleagues, the team went on a five-day expedition to Inírida. The team included members of the Indigenous reserves del río Atabapo, one of whom had seen the lilies during his childhood.

However, the trip didn’t exactly go according to plan. By their last night, they had found only two water lily plants, neither of which had flowers. This was a problem, because botanists need a flower to differentiate species. As if that wasn’t bad enough, the water lilies they found seemed to be struggling with a late rainy season.

But when Magdalena described their situation to the expedition’s boat captain, Gustavo Melo, he said he knew where they could find more of this water lily. They didn’t have much time to do so, with the team’s flight back to Bogotá was at 9:00 a.m. the next morning. Thus at 1:00 a.m., Melo, a member of the Indigenous Tucano community, led them to a small lake. There, they found five more Victorias, one of which was blooming.

“This is one of those extraordinary stories where indigenous and horticultural knowledge with the help of social media and technology came together to uncover a truly extraordinary botanical achievement,” Magdalena said in a statement.

The  researchers were able to collect what they needed to start the official scientific description of the new species: Victoria colombiana. They even managed to make that early flight. In their recent study published in the journal Phytotaxa, he and his colleagues also announced the discovery of another new Victoria species, Victoria mattogrossensis.

The team estimates that there are only between 30 and 50 wild V. colombiana plants, and is extremely vulnerable to extinction. They are currently working to get the plant officially categorized as Critically Endangered by the IUCN Red List.

“Now that we know this water lily exists, our responsibility is to understand it better and protect it. With so many plants in the wild threatened with extinction, we simply cannot wait any longer to take action,” Magdalena added. Experts are considering starting a naturalized population in a more accessible lake in Indigenous territory and researchers also are hoping to cultivate the species in Bogotà.

Next, we need to make sure that this div is only shown when the card is facing the viewer. Happily, CSS has an elegant way to handle this scenario, built right into the language!

We need to learn about a couple more properties.

The backface-visibility property allows us to specify whether an item should be visible when it's rotated more than 90 degrees in either direction.

In this case, we also need to add transform-style: preserve-3d to the parent element (the one responsible for the animation). This property allows elements to be positioned in 3D space, and it allows backface-visibility to work correctly in this context.

Eagle-eyed readers—or, those who can read Chinese—might've noticed that this effect is backwards. Right now, we only see our white "back" when the card is facing forwards!

It makes sense, because both the card and the backside are facing the same way. We're only hiding the backside when the whole thing is rotated around.

We can fix this by being a little sneaky, and giving our backside element a 180-degree Y-axis rotation. When the card is facing forwards, our backface will be facing away, and backface-visibility: hidden will hide it.

We can also apply a very slight Z-translate, to push the element a bit further from the viewer than the card. This addresses an issue where the elements can appear to flicker, because both the card and the backdrop are occupying the same point in 3D space. We push it away from the user so that the backside is actually behind the card itself (which means it'll be in front of the card when it's rotated).

Spacial orientation is a hard thing to visualize (especially with nested rotations!), so don't be discouraged if it's not immediately obvious why this trick works. Playing with the live-editable code should help!

#2755. Nearly 3,000 exploding stars challenge our understanding of the Universe

The largest collection of exploding white dwarf stars ever compiled is providing fresh evidence that dark energy, the mysterious force responsible for the Universe’s accelerating expansion, may change over time. The findings challenge a central assumption of modern cosmology and could help scientists better understand how the Universe evolves.

An international research team led by scientists at the University of Queensland’s School of Mathematics and Physics brought together observations of 2,884 Type Ia supernovae. These powerful stellar explosions serve as important tools for measuring distances across the Universe, allowing astronomers to trace its expansion history.

PhD candidate Ryan Camilleri said the project establishes a new international standard for supernova cosmology. By bringing decades of measurements into a unified dataset, researchers have created their most detailed picture yet of how cosmic expansion has changed over time.

“We’ve rebuilt 3 decades of astronomical observations into a single, consistent framework,” Mr. Camilleri said.

“We combined our data with other cosmic measurements including relic light from the Big Bang and maps of how galaxies are distributed through space.

“Instead of confirming the standard model of cosmology which assumes dark energy is fixed and unchanging, we have more evidence that dark energy may change over time.”

The new collection brings together historical supernova measurements and findings from the Dark Energy Survey (DES) published in 2024.

To make observations collected over different periods directly comparable, the scientists revisited older data using updated analytical methods. Advances in understanding supernova behavior allowed the team to improve the consistency and accuracy of measurements gathered over decades.

“Over the years we’ve learned a lot more about how supernovae behave so we’ve been able to go back and apply that improved understanding to older data,” Mr. Camilleri said.

The researchers also had to account for differences among the telescopes used to collect the observations. Other factors, including cosmic dust and the masses of galaxies, can influence the light astronomers receive from distant supernovae and therefore affect distance measurements.

“Extensive work has been done to link observations from different telescopes with different capacities and take into account issues such as cosmic dust and galaxy mass which can affect the light coming from a supernova.

“We also incorporated more subtle effects such as gravitational lensing, which is the bending and magnification of light around large objects as it travels from a supernova to Earth.”

Astrophysicist Professor Tamara Davis said the expanded dataset represents an important advance in efforts to understand the nature of dark energy.

Under the standard model of cosmology, dark energy is treated as a constant property of the Universe. However, recent observations have raised questions about whether that assumption fully explains how cosmic expansion has evolved.

“Our supernova data from DES in 2024 first showed hints that dark energy may be time varying, and this new compilation also sees a deviation from the standard model although in a slightly different direction,” Professor Davis said.

The findings are especially interesting because a separate astronomical project has also produced evidence suggesting that dark energy might not remain constant.

“Similarly results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) found hints of variations in dark energy in its surveys of relic sound waves from the early Universe.

“So, 2 completely independent measurements have found hints of time variation in dark energy, challenging the standard model that dark energy doesn't change.

Although the different measurements do not point to exactly the same departure from the standard model, they provide additional reasons to investigate whether dark energy changes as the Universe ages.

Understanding dark energy could have implications extending far beyond the expansion of the Universe. Researchers believe that learning more about its behavior might also provide insights into one of the greatest unresolved problems in theoretical physics: reconciling gravity with quantum physics.

“All of this research may also hold the clue to explain how gravity and quantum physics fit together.

“We know these 2 theories are each immensely successful in their own realms, so if we can figure out how to put them together that would be a huge step in theoretical physics.”

The researchers plan to expand the dataset as additional observations become available. One important source will be the Dark Energy Bedrock All-Sky Supernova program (DEBASS), which is identifying hundreds of supernovae at distances closer to Earth than those surveyed by DES.

Incorporating these nearby explosions will provide further measurements that scientists can use to investigate the Universe’s expansion and test whether dark energy truly changes over time.

The cosmology findings are available on arXiv here, while the accompanying measurements of host galaxy masses can be found on arXiv here.

The new results build on decades of work by hundreds of astronomers and researchers worldwide. Contributing projects include the Dark Energy Survey, Foundation Supernova Survey, PanStarrs I, Sloan Digital Sky Survey, Supernova Legacy Survey, Carnegie Supernova Project, and the Center for Astrophysics supernova survey.

#2757. Your B.C. election guide to key climate and environment issues

Shannon Waters has been reporting on B.C. politics for nearly a decade. Since joining The Narwhal in 2024, her work has focused on energy, resource extraction and Indigenous Rights.

With the provincial election just days away, promises from B.C.’s main political parties are piling up. Voters have been offered tax breaks, tax increases, price caps and new health-care resources. But none of the main parties vying for votes has had much to say about the environment or climate change. It took more than two weeks for the BC NDP and BC Greens to release their climate platforms. The BC Conservative Party put out its full platform — with the party’s first explicit mentions of climate change — on Oct. 18, two days after advance voting began and just six days before the final vote.

After nearly a decade in government, the BC NDP isn’t offering much in the way of new climate-focused policies in this election. The party’s climate platform, released on Oct. 10, includes a pledge to cap the amount of power available for AI and data centres, a policy already enacted into law last year. If re-elected, Leader David Eby is promising to hold data centres in B.C. to high standards for water efficiency and require them to report their water use publicly.

The BC Conservative platform mentions climate just once, stating “climate decisions” should be based on three principles: protecting people from extreme weather, reducing emissions in B.C. and beyond and delivering measurable results. “The need to develop our natural resources must always be weighed against the need to conserve our bounty through responsible resource management,” the platform states.

Unsurprisingly, the BC Green campaign platform promises to put climate policy at the centre of the provincial agenda by making climate targets central to “major policies, infrastructure investments and industrial decisions.” In addition to a dedicated climate platform, many of the party’s campaign promises include climate-conscious components. The Greens’ economic platform focuses on ways to build “a prosperous, resilient, low-carbon economy” and the party’s housing platform includes a commitment to reduce emissions related to buildings by focusing on lower-carbon designs and materials, as well as conserving existing housing stock.

With all three party platforms published, The Narwhal dug in to see where the parties stand on key issues — and what they’re promising to get rid of.

Both the NDP and the Conservatives are committing to expanding the liquefied natural gas (LNG) industry in B.C. Producing natural gas usually involves hydraulic fracturing, or fracking, a process that shoots high-pressure water mixed with chemicals underground to fracture the rocks and release the gas. Making LNG requires running massive compressors 24-7 to cool gas into a liquid. It’s an energy-intensive process that — so far in B.C. — is largely powered by burning natural gas.

The Conservatives have promised to make B.C. an “energy superpower” by doubling LNG production by 2032 and tripling it by 2035. When that policy dropped, the NDP was quick to point out the five LNG projects in development are already on track to more than triple the province’s LNG production by 2035.

The Conservative energy platform also pledges to get rid of B.C.’s industrial carbon tax and replace it with new rules that it says would ensure B.C.’s gas exports are competitive with the United States and Qatar.

A BC Conservative government would take a new approach to tallying B.C.’s greenhouse gas emissions, one that runs counter to the 2016 Paris Agreement. The Conservative platform proposes taking credit for “emissions avoided through cleaner B.C. exports,” using the example of exported gas replacing coal.

If his party forms government, Leader Lorne Doerkson has also promised to use deregulation as a tool to spur economic development, partly by bringing back a ministry dedicated to getting rid of rules, processes and laws it deems burdensome or unnecessary. For every new regulation a Conservative government makes, it would axe two existing regulations, according to Doerkson.

The NDP is promising to use some oil and gas royalty revenue — $50 million to start — to promote climate action and create jobs. The “Clean Futures Fund,” as the NDP platform dubs this promise, might sound familiar, possibly because some of the revenue raised by B.C.’s consumer carbon tax was once dedicated to similar spending priorities.

Speaking of oil and gas revenues, Eby refused to confirm whether his government is still committed to its original plan to collect 50 per cent of profits reaped by gas companies in B.C. The new gas royalty system is scheduled to take effect in 2027.

“We’re going to hold on to high royalty rates,” Eby said in response to a question from The Narwhal about whether he remains committed to the 50 per cent target announced by the B.C. government in 2022.“We’re going to demand as much as possible and we’re also going to build the economy.”

The BC Greens’ platform promises a 50-50 profit split under the new gas royalty framework. The Green climate platform notes B.C. is expecting to collect about $1.1 billion in gas royalties in the 2027-28 fiscal year. Bumping the province’s share of that royalty revenue up to 50 per cent would yield over $4 billion, according to the Greens.

The Greens have also pledged to end further fossil fuel development in B.C., banning new fracking operations, LNG facilities or expansions and pipelines, and axing provincial subsidies, favourable tax policies and public financing for fossil fuel production.

Leader Emily Lowan was the only provincial party leader to criticize LNG Canada’s decision to proceed with Phase 2 of its Kitimat LNG facility. The final investment decision to proceed with the expansion — which has been fully permitted since 2018 — was announced on Sept. 28 and celebrated by both Eby and Doerkson.

Neither party leader mentioned the integrity issue with the existing LNG Canada facility’s flare stack which resulted in Kitimat community members being subjected to excessive noise, black smoke, a towering flame — at times reaching the height of New York’s Statue of Liberty or London’s Big Ben — for months.The consortium of companies behind LNG Canada wants the facility’s permit changed to accommodate higher emissions levels to account for its need to keep the flare burning 24-7 until the stack can be redesigned in 2028.

One of Doerkson’s first public statements after becoming interim leader of the BC Conservatives was to declare his desire to repeal B.C.’s Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act, or DRIPA.

The pledge, which is a top priority for the Conservatives, appears in the party’s platform under a section titled “protect private property rights” — despite the fact that DRIPA doesn’t deal with private property.

A Conservative government would pass a law requiring information about Aboriginal Title claims and ongoing negotiations to be made public. The proposed law would also publicize information about legal actions and agreements related to First Nations Rights and Title.

The party’s platform also promises to “restore equal access to Crown land and provincial parks,” a pledge that appears to target annual temporary closures of Pipi7íyekw/Joffre Lakes Park that restrict visits to members of Líl̓wat and N’Quatqua First Nations, whose unceded territories encompass the park. These reconnection periods have been taking place since 2023 and are intended to allow the Nations to conduct traditional harvesting, as well as ceremonial and cultural practices.

Under a BC Conservative government, reconciliation with First Nations would focus on “practical cooperation, clear and fair treaty agreements and economic opportunities,” per the platform. It emphasizes opportunities for First Nations to benefit from projects via revenue sharing and ownership opportunities.

The BC NDP platform doesn’t mention DRIPA but Eby confirmed near the beginning of the election campaign that he would stick to his plan to amend the law to block further court challenges.“We do have to respond to that court decision, it’s not up for debate,” he said on Sept. 25. “We’ve been working with First Nations over the summer to see if we can find a path forward that we all agree on.”

The Greens are promising to leave DRIPA as is and to work with First Nations to develop a process aimed at resolving disputes proactively as a way to avoid court cases — something some NDP MLAs have often stated the law was intended to do.The Greens are the only party with a dedicated Indigenous Rights section in their platform.

An NDP government would stick with its commitment to protect 30 per cent of B.C.’s lands by 2030, a promise the party has been working on since 2022.

In the party’s 2026 campaign platform, the 30-by-30 goal is linked to the Northwest Critical Conservation Corridor, a federal initiative announced by Prime Minister Mark Carney in November 2025 and referred to the Major Projects Office as a potential national interest project. According to the NDP, the corridor will “add protections to an area equivalent to 12,000 Stanley Parks, while enabling responsible natural resource development.”

The NDP is also promising to continue funding the Watershed Security Fund at $30 million per year. The fund was created in 2023 with a $100-million commitment from the province making this another stay-the-course pledge from the party.

The BC Conservative platform claims conservation is a conservative value but doesn’t offer many specific conservation-focused commitments. The party pledges to increase enforcement in parks and wilderness areas with more conservation officers in the field, and to establish sustainable fish and wildlife population objectives.The platform promises a science-based approach to wildlife management with input from hunters, anglers and First Nations. It also repeats a 2024 campaign commitment to develop a made-in-B.C. species-at-risk law. The Conservative platform doesn’t mention B.C.’s existing 30-by-30 commitment. In May 2024, former BC Conservative leader John Rustad told The Narwhal he would abandon the goal.

The Greens would make 30-by-30 a minimum goal. The party also promises to boost funding by BC Parks and recreation sites across B.C. to $114 million, a 50 per cent increase.

In its tourism strategy, the Green Party platform promises to put environmental impacts front and centre when considering development opportunities for new and existing tourism destinations.

The platform has a section dedicated to water management and protection, including a commitment to implement a proactive, long-term approach to protecting water security and ensuring industrial users pay a fair share for the water they use.

None of the party platforms mention the future of Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas (IPCAs), which have been a key tool for advancing conservation efforts in B.C. and Canada. In northern B.C., members of the Kaska Nation say the snap election has slowed efforts to finalize protection of Dene Kʼéh Kusān, a proposed Indigenous-protected area that would cover one of the province’s largest intact wilderness areas.

B.C.’s forestry industry has struggled for years with multiple mill closures and hundreds of jobs lost since the 2024 election. At the same time, the province’s forests have been impacted by wildfires and drought.

On the campaign trail, Eby pointed to the support his government secured from Ottawa to offset the impact of U.S. tariffs on softwood lumber and efforts to get more woodfibre to mills. The NDP platform promises to continue to support the forest industry by providing more cash for mills to upgrade their operations, spending $120 million to protect jobs at mills that are at risk of closing and focus on making sure mills get the fibre needed to keep operating.

The party’s promises to protect B.C.’s forests are less robust.

A re-elected NDP government would dedicate $50 million to permanently protect some of B.C.’s “most irreplaceable and rarest” old-growth forests. The party’s platform makes no mention of the 2020 old-growth strategic review, despite a 2024 campaign commitment to fulfill all of its recommendations.

The BC Conservatives are promising to do many of the same things the NDP has done but better, bigger and faster. The party’s forestry platform promises to make more trees available for harvest — but does not address how the party would ensure companies actually harvest the maximum amount allowed, something that has not happened in years due to largely to the dwindling availability of economically viable trees to log. The Conservatives are also promising to focus on streamlining permitting, reduce costs for forestry companies and support value-added manufacturing.

The platform projects Conservative forestry policies would double government revenue from stumpage fees by 2028-29. The 2026 provincial budget did not include a specific revenue estimate for stumpage but estimated total forestry-related revenues will increase 3.3 per cent per year through 2029.

A Conservative government would “encourage Indigenous participation in the forestry industry by investing in skills training” and dedicate a portion of federal funding for targeting the forest industry to First Nations.

The party is also promising to “resolve” the issue of old-growth deferrals by “protecting original old growth” and making the rest eligible for logging. The platform does not define original old growth but does say that B.C.’s “most endangered and rarest ecosystems” would also be protected.

The Green platform proposes shifting B.C. forestry from a fibre-supply model to one that prioritizes ecosystem health, wildfire resilience, cultural values and healthy communities. The Bundibugyo virus is one of six known strains of Ebola. It was discovered in 2007 when an outbreak of hemorrhagic fever occurred in the Bundibugyo area of Western Uganda.

When people think of Ebola, they usually think of the Zaire strain, which is the deadliest known version and has a mortality rate of up to 90%! The Bundibugyo strain is still a nasty virus with a mortality rate of 25-40%, but it is less lethal than Zaire.

Ebola viruses are filoviruses. They look like a stringy thread under a microscope.

Ebola viruses spread through close contact with the bodily fluids of an infected person or an animal.

The Bundibugyo strain is very rare, with only two known outbreaks before 2026.

2007 – Uganda – this was the original outbreak in the Bundibugyo district – 131 known infections.

2012 – Democratic Republic of Congo – 52 known infections.

2026 – Democratic Republic of Congo – ongoing outbreak at time of writing

The natural reservoir of the Bundibugyo strain is thought to be local bat populations.

There are vaccines and antibody treatments for Ebola, but these are specifically designed for the Zaire strain. Bundibugyo has a slightly different outer protein, so treatments targeted at the Zaire version don’t work as well on Bundibugyo.

#2765. 40 years later, Chernobyl's nuclear fallout is not what scientists expected

Four decades after the Chernobyl (known today as Chornobyl) nuclear disaster, microscopic radioactive particles remain scattered throughout the surrounding soil. The explosions that devastated the reactor released enormous quantities of debris, leaving behind contamination that continues to pose serious health risks. Even after 40 years, particles measuring just 8 to 50 micrometers remain highly radioactive, and entering affected areas still requires protective clothing.

Scientists have now taken a closer look at these tiny remnants and uncovered a surprising finding: some have preserved much of their original internal structure despite decades of exposure to the environment.

"There are three classes of these particles," explains Tobias Weissenborn, a physicist and doctoral candidate at Leibniz University Hannover. "First, there are particles that are chemically and physically still very similar to the nuclear fuel uranium dioxide. Then, there are particles that are partially or fully encased in, or completely fused with, their zirconium layer."

The second category originated during the extreme temperatures reached inside the reactor. As the nuclear fuel melted, it combined with zirconium, a highly resistant metal used in the fuel's protective outer layer.

"The third type of particle was created when the graphite-moderator caught fire," Weissenborn adds.

Under normal operating conditions, the graphite moderator slows fast neutrons, helping sustain the nuclear chain reaction. During the disaster, however, the graphite ignited and continued burning for ten days.

"In the process, the fuel oxidized into various uranium oxides" - such as U3O8.

Unlike more stable materials, this uranium oxide is mechanically fragile and can readily break apart into microscopic fragments. Wind can then carry these radioactive particles through the environment, where inhaling them presents serious health hazards.

Scientists Examine Chornobyl's Radioactive Dust in Unprecedented Detail

Despite decades of research, scientists still do not fully understand why radioactive particles from Chornobyl break down at different speeds. As these materials weather, they can produce several forms of uranium oxide, making their long term behavior difficult to predict.

Investigating these changes requires exceptionally precise equipment, including synchrotron X-ray diffraction, a technique that reveals the arrangement of atoms inside crystalline materials.

Dr. Christoph Hennig, a crystallographer at the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf (HZDR), has now completed the first successful structural analysis of radioactive fragments of this kind at the Rossendorf Beamline in Grenoble.

Working with Weissenborn, Hennig analyzed six particles recovered from the Chornobyl region. By identifying the different crystalline phases within them, the researchers gained information that could help determine how quickly radioactive substances escape into the surrounding environment.

"The colleagues from Hanover and I used various methods to isolate the particles from Ukrainian soil samples and attach them to tungsten electrodes," Weissenborn explains.

Because the samples were highly radioactive, they had to be enclosed within several layers of containment before being transported to Grenoble. There, Hennig's research team used X-ray diffraction to examine their internal structures by measuring how X-rays scattered as they passed through the material.

"First, we had to figure out how to examine the particle structure," Christoph Hennig explains. "Ultimately, we fully rotated the particles in an X-ray beam focused down to 100 micrometers - about the thickness of a human hair. We measured each particle from 2,000 different angles to seamlessly capture all the reflections."

The measurements allowed the scientists to distinguish different uranium oxide phases within the radioactive fragments, revealing details that had previously been inaccessible.

A Surprising Discovery About 40-Year-Old Nuclear Fallout

The results revealed something unexpected. Despite spending four decades in the environment, the nuclear fuel inside the six examined particles had retained much of its original crystal structure.

This suggests that at least some of the radioactive debris produced by the Chornobyl disaster is more chemically stable than researchers previously believed.

That stability could have important environmental implications. Rather than rapidly releasing radioactive substances, the particles appear capable of retaining fission products within their structures. If this behavior occurs more widely, it could help limit the movement of radioactive contaminants into nearby soil and water.

However, the researchers emphasize that their findings should not be interpreted as evidence that Chornobyl's radioactive contamination is becoming harmless.

"However, every single particle has a different structure," warns Weissenborn, "and our experiment only studied six such particles from two different locations."

The relatively small sample means the findings cannot yet establish how radioactive particles behave throughout the entire contaminated region. To understand their stability more comprehensively, scientists would need to investigate many additional particles collected from a much broader range of locations.

Why Chornobyl's Radioactive Legacy Remains Unpredictable

Even with substantially more data, predicting the long term dangers of Chornobyl's radioactive debris would remain challenging. Individual particles can differ in composition, structure, and resistance to environmental weathering, potentially affecting when they release radioactive materials.

"And even if we obtained some averages at some point, we still wouldn't be able to make universal statements about health risks in the region," Weissenborn cautions. "Because even if the particles decay in a largely uniform patten, there will always be outliers, more persistent particles, that will release radionuclides at a later point in time."

These persistent particles could continue releasing radioactive substances long after other remnants have broken down, complicating efforts to assess future contamination and health risks.

For that reason, the new findings do not suggest that restrictions on the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone can be removed anytime soon.

Research into the disaster's enduring consequences is continuing. Weissenborn and Hennig are already conducting additional experiments focused on highly radioactive transuranic phases found in the remaining debris. Their work could provide a clearer picture of how Chornobyl's nuclear materials change over time and how long their environmental effects may persist.

Kristin O’Connor, who coordinates efforts to remediate Hamilton Harbour at Conservation Halton, says the waterway’s transformation began as Hamilton expanded during the 1800s and 1900s.

“It was consciously decided that the Hamilton Harbour would be the dumping ground for industry and for sewage,” O’Connor said of her belief about how the contamination problem began across the harbour.

Over time, natural shorelines were filled in, wetlands disappeared and large sections of the waterfront were converted into industrial lands. The result was decades of deteriorating water quality, algae blooms, contaminated sediment and damage to fish and wildlife habitat. O’Connor points out a visiting urban planner described the harbour as the world’s largest and most beautiful septic tank in 1958.

In 1972, seeing a number of similarly degraded shorelines around the Great Lakes, Canada and the United States agreed to work together to clean up some of the most deeply polluted sites. The two countries signed the Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement in 1987, which listed 43 official areas of concern, where industrial pollution was the most deleterious. Hamilton Harbour was one of them.

The harbour ultimately had 11 of 14 possible categories of environmental impairment identified through the Great Lakes restoration framework. Among them were restrictions on eating fish and the degradation of benthos — small organisms that live in or on sediment at the bottom of waterways. Those two concerns are particularly relevant to the Kenilworth boat slip.

The contamination in the Kenilworth Avenue boat slip didn’t come from a single incident. For generations, Hamilton Harbour, extending off of Lake Ontario, was shaped by steel-making, shipping, urban development and the discharge of industrial waste and sewage.

The 1999 scientific report examined contaminant levels in 1996 and found run-off from Dofasco’s coal piles was heavily contaminated with metals and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, or PAHs. The group of chemicals is associated with sources including petroleum products, steel-making and coking operations — the process of heating coal at high temperatures to produce fuel for steel-making. Some are considered to be cancer-causing and toxic for aquatic life. After the study came out, Dofasco contracted work to capture and treat run-off from its coal piles.

Also found within the sediment, PCBs, once widely used in industrial equipment, can remain in the environment for long periods and accumulate in animal tissue. Environment and Climate Change Canada identifies the human-made chemicals as potential human carcinogens that are toxic to fish at low levels. The United Nations Environment Programme has reported they accumulate in the tissues of plants and animals, becoming more concentrated and harmful as they move up the food chain.

Dofasco acknowledged the sediment problem in its 2002 annual report, saying, “The company’s boat slip contains contaminated sediment from a time that pre-dates environmental awareness and control technologies.”

At the time, the company said it was working with the federal and provincial environment departments and other stakeholders to examine options for addressing the contamination. Neither Environment and Climate Change Canada nor Ontario’s Ministry of the Environment, Conservation and Parks responded to requests for comment by publication time.

ArcelorMittal Dofasco’s 2015 project description acknowledged the pollution could negatively affect local aquatic organisms and noted the cleanup was intended to prevent them and other wildlife from coming into contact with the most heavily contaminated material.

The Canada Water Agency, the federal agency responsible for freshwater governance, said in a statement to The Narwhal that cleaning up the Kenilworth boat slip, along with a nearby contaminated site known as the Strathearne boat slip, remains necessary to reduce contamination from PCBs, PAHs and metals.

After Hamilton Harbour was designated an area of concern, restoration efforts kicked off, starting with the development of a remedial action plan to address decades of environmental damage.

The Hamilton Harbour remedial action plan brings together governments, industry, conservation authorities, researchers and community organizations to restore the harbour. Dofasco and Stelco, Hamilton’s two major steel producers, have been involved in the process since its early years. O’Connor, the remedial action plan coordinator with Halton Conservation, says the Kenilworth boat slip was one of many environmental problems contributing to the harbour’s designation as an area of concern.

Over the decades, the partnership has worked on projects ranging from wastewater treatment improvements to shoreline restoration and fish habitat rehabilitation. One of its largest undertakings — and one that looks to be a model for success — is the cleanup of Randle Reef.

Located near the property of Stelco, Randle Reef was the largest contaminated sediment site on the Canadian side of the Great Lakes. A $138.9-million cleanup began in 2015, funded by the federal and provincial governments, municipalities, the port authority and industry. While Stelco was not deemed solely responsible for the pollution at Randle Reef, it supplied $14 million in steel to build a box to contain it. The sediment there was contaminated with the same toxins found at Kenilworth, and had to be dredged and moved into the steel container. The final phase, which involves installing a cap over the box, is scheduled for completion in 2027.

Kenilworth, however, has followed a different path.

ArcelorMittal Dofasco’s 2015 remediation plan outlines how it would contain the most contaminated sediment in the two proposed structures: one approximately 66 metres wide and 120 metres long and the other roughly 264 metres long and 44 metres wide. Less than 40,000 cubic metres of contaminated sediment was expected to be dredged from other areas of the boat slip and relocated into the containment structures.

Sediment containing naphthalene — the most volatile member of the PAH family — would be tested and, where required, transported to a licensed landfill for hazardous waste disposal. Other areas would be covered with sand to isolate contamination remaining after dredging. A temporary water treatment system would manage wastewater generated during the work, while long-term monitoring would assess the integrity of the containment structures. The approach was designed to minimize disruption to shipping operations while reducing exposure to contaminated sediment.

Unlike the multi-partner Randle Reef project, ArcelorMittal Dofasco was identified as the “sole proponent” for its boat slip remediation. O’Connor says the remedial action plan identifies the environmental work that needs to be completed, but individual partners are responsible for carrying out their own projects. “We don’t fund it. We don’t monitor it,” she said.

In its 2023 annual report, ArcelorMittal Dofasco listed a US$31-million environmental provision for the expected cost of remediating toxic sediment at the Kenilworth boat slip. The company’s 2025 annual report included a US$28-million provision for the project. These provisions are accounting estimates of expected environmental costs, not proof that money has been spent on remediation or that those amounts are held in a dedicated account.

In 2020, the International Joint Commission, a Canada-U.S. organization that investigates and mediates transboundary issues involving fresh water, reported that ArcelorMittal Dofasco was expected to clean up contaminated sediment near the Kenilworth boat slip over two years beginning in 2021.

A Hamilton Harbour remedial action plan fact sheet originally published in 2018 and updated in 2023 continued to describe ArcelorMittal Dofasco as obtaining provincial approval to manage PCB, PAH and metal-contaminated sediment in the boat slip. Meanwhile, meeting records from the Bay Area Implementation Team, the group of partners responsible for implementing the remedial action plan, show the project remained under development.

At a June 13, 2023, meeting, ArcelorMittal Dofasco reported conducting additional sediment sampling and working with an engineering consultant to review the results. The company was preparing an updated environmental summary report as part of the permitting process and reviewing the engineering design.

A year later, the project still had not moved into construction. Meeting notes from June 20, 2024, state a tender was being prepared for proponents to bid on the remediation work. The notes state that technical options and logistics were being reviewed as well as further consultations with the Indigenous community.

By November 2025, the project timeline was still being reviewed, according to notes, but ArcelorMittal Dofasco remained committed to the cleanup.

The most recent update provided to the Bay Area Implementation Team came from ArcelorMittal Dofasco on May 29, 2026: “The current market context and threat to the Canadian steel industry as a result of the U.S. tariffs creates a very difficult environment for the company and the industry. So much so that every investment must be carefully reviewed and managed in order to continue to be a sustainable business. Given this, there is no further update or progress since the last meeting.”

The statement comes amid substantial uncertainty facing Canadian steel producers.

U.S. President Donald Trump imposed 25 per cent tariffs on Canadian steel and aluminum in March 2025, before doubling them to 50 per cent that June and escalating further with additional trade pressures through 2026. In steel-making communities, including Hamilton, workers have faced job losses as a result. That includes at Stelco, which has laid off hundreds of workers in the past weeks.

But that doesn’t account for the past decade of delays on remediating the Kenilworth boat slip.

Chris McLaughlin, executive director of the Bay Area Restoration Council, said the precise circumstances behind the years of delays remain unclear. He called for greater leadership from the federal and provincial government in addressing the project’s technical and economic obstacles, and said the public deserves clearer commitments and timelines from the company for completing the work.

“There is a moral imperative to reconcile with our destructive industrial past,” he said. “Effective cleanups and restorative actions with public engagement and community connections create meaningful improvements and sustain hope.”

Across the Caribbean, people have installed roughly twice as many solar panels as official statistics would suggest. That is the finding of a new report, which calls on countries to make it easier for new solar installations to connect to the grid.

China, the biggest manufacturer of solar globally, has exported far more panels to the Caribbean than have officially been installed. An analysis of customs data by energy think tank Ember suggests the region had installed 2.3 gigawatts of solar between 2021 and 2024. And yet only about half of that was included in official tallies of power capacity.

In countries from Pakistan to Nigeria to Cuba, people hit by rising fuel prices are turning to solar. By and large, households and businesses install rooftop arrays that are not connected to the central power system. That means their surplus energy is going to waste instead of being sold back to the grid.

In the Caribbean, energy shocks are particularly pronounced as island nations with little in the way of fossil fuel reserves are heavily dependent on imports. Across the region more than 90 percent of electricity is generated using fossil fuels, mostly imported oil and gas. As a result, electricity in the region is among the most expensive in the world.

Renewable energy offers a cheaper alternative, and yet the nascent solar buildout has yet to yield real gains for Caribbean states. Analysts say reforms are needed to speed installations of solar and bring them onto the grid. For instance, in several countries utilities can effectively block competing power sources, including solar installations, from connecting to the power grid. Analysts say that regulators, not power utilities, should decide which power sources can connect.

“Secure, affordable energy is within reach for the Caribbean,” said Wilmar Suárez, senior energy analyst for Latin America at Ember. “Leaders who act decisively will position their countries at the forefront of this historic opportunity.”

#2771. Fahrenheit, Celsius and a STEM Challenge

When checking what to pack for a holiday, you need to know whether the temperature is in Celsius or Fahrenheit. 40°F is winter coat weather, but 40 °C is a very hot day!

Celsius and Fahrenheit are both temperature scales, it’s a bit like measuring the same thing with two rulers, but starting with one at 0 and one at 32. You’d get a completely different measurement.

The difference between the two is down to the people who invented the scales. Anders Celsius based his scale on pure water ( 0-100 ), and Daniel Fahrenheit based his on the coldest temperature he could create in his lab and human body temperature.

The same number in Celsius and Fahrenheit varies a lot, until you get to the magic -40°, which is the same in both!

The Celsius temperature scale was invented by Anders Celsius, a Swedish astronomer, in 1742. Although his version is actually the opposite of what we use today. He set 100° as the freezing point of water and 0° as the boiling point, which avoided the use of negative numbers for very cold temperatures. After Anders Celsius passed away, Carl Linnaeus switched it to make 0° the freezing point of water, which is the Celsius scale we use today!

The Fahrenheit scale was invented in 1724 by a German physicist called Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit.

Daniel Fahrenheit was the first person to use mercury in a thermometer. He realised that mercury was better than water or alcohol, which had previously been used, because it didn’t freeze as easily and expanded smoothly.

Fahrenheit wanted zero degrees to be the coldest temperature he could create in his lab. He mixed water, ice, and ammonium chloride (a type of salt) and set the point on his thermometer where the mercury dropped to zero. He decided the freezing point of water should be 32°F, then took the temperature of his wife’s armpit and rounded it to 96° (this was later changed to 98.6°F, which is human body temperature).

Daniel Fahrenheit used ammonium chloride, water and ice to create very cold slushy water in his lab. When ammonium chloride is dissolved in water, it absorbs heat from its surroundings, which makes the water colder than before it was added. This is an endothermic process.

The ammonium chloride particles also get in the way of water molecules, making it harder for them to bond and form ice. This further lowers the freezing point of water. Lowering the freezing point forces the ice to melt. To melt, it must draw heat from its surroundings, which further lowers the water temperature, pushing it well below its normal freezing point without turning into ice.

#2774. NASA’s Swift Mission Ends, Leaves Behind Scientific Bonanza

NASA’s Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has ended its science mission now that the spacecraft is orbiting less than 171 miles (275 kilometers) above Earth’s surface. The greater atmospheric drag at this height prevents the satellite’s telescopes from steadily gazing at their astronomical targets.

Swift conducted its final observation on Oct. 20. It is expected to re-enter the atmosphere in the next few weeks.

“Swift delivered an incredible, lasting return on investment for the nation, designed for a two-year primary mission but productive for more than 10 times as long,” said Shawn Domagal-Goldman, director, Astrophysics Division at NASA Headquarters in Washington. “In that time, Swift played a major role in advancing our understanding of the changing universe and was part of a revolution in astrophysics that showed how critical rapid-response observations are to the entire community. To support rapid response to cosmic events in a post-Swift world, we’re exploring our ability to use and augment NASA’s fleet of existing space telescopes, from fast-to-orbit commercial capabilities to efficient, innovative new missions. And Swift leaves us with an extraordinary archive of multiwavelength observations that will fuel new discoveries for decades more.”

In its storied career, Swift has observed more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts, the most powerful explosions in the cosmos. It has studied more than 1,400 supernova explosions, witnessed stars shredded by black holes, monitored active galaxies, conducted an all-sky X-ray survey, studied asteroids and comets, and more. Swift data has contributed to about 7,000 published scientific papers.

“Swift was designed as a satellite dedicated to studying gamma-ray bursts, outbursts that came and went so quickly that no one could target them with large telescopes to learn more,” said S. Bradley Cenko, the mission’s principal investigator at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, Maryland. “Swift needed to detect and locate gamma-ray bursts, communicate their position to other observatories, and then swing its telescopes around to image the burst locations before their afterglows faded out. This typically took Swift about a minute to do.”

Led by its first principal investigator, Neil Gehrels, Swift was born as a multiwavelength observatory, with co-aligned Optical/Ultraviolet and X-ray telescopes that could quickly image and refine a burst’s position. The mission launched into orbit on Nov. 20, 2004, and went to work the following month. In 2018, NASA renamed the mission to honor Gehrels following his death.

Speed was especially important for an elusive population called short gamma-ray bursts, which reach peak brightness in less than two seconds. Until Swift, it had been impossible to locate these bursts rapidly and precisely enough to observe their afterglows. In May 2005, Swift achieved this milestone with GRB 050509B, which lasted just 0.03 seconds. Swift turned to the burst fast enough to detect 11 X-ray photons, making this the first short burst with a detected afterglow. These observations validated long-standing theoretical models, showing that short gamma-ray bursts likely come from mergers of two neutron stars — objects with the mass of the Sun that have been crushed to the size of a city.

Another factor led to Swift’s success as a kind of astronomical multitool. Its flexible planning system enabled astronomers to request "target-of-opportunity" observations that could be commanded from the ground in as little as a few minutes, or to set up monitoring programs for observing specific sources lasting from as little as a few minutes to months. The system could schedule up to 75 independent targets a day.

"The unique speed of response of the Swift hardware, the extraordinary success of the rapid mission operations, and the closely knit teamwork of the Swift Flight and Science Operations teams placed Swift at the crossroads of burgeoning areas of research," said John Nousek, the mission’s operations director and professor of astronomy and astrophysics in the Eberly College of Science at Penn State in University Park, Pennsylvania.

The Swift archive contains nearly 22 years of brightness measurements of all kinds of cosmic outbursts, multiwavelength spectra, more than 1.5 million images from its Ultraviolet/Optical Telescope, and much more. The full dataset, publicly available as part of NASA’s commitment to open, gold standard science, spans approximately 12 terabytes, the storage equivalent of about 600 high-definition movies.

As part of the agency’s previous planning for Swift’s end of mission, NASA will continue to prioritize finding new options to react rapidly to cosmic events, using current missions to help fill the gap in the meantime.

#2776. NASA’s Curiosity rover reveals a spectacular new view of Mars with a mystery on the horizon

NASA's Curiosity rover has captured a spectacular new panorama of Mars, providing its clearest look yet at a series of mysterious cliffs shaped by wind. Scientists have been eager to examine these distant formations up close, and the latest images offer an exciting preview of what lies ahead. Taken at 8:30 a.m. local Mars time, the scene reveals striking blue tones across the foreground while morning sunlight illuminates rugged cliffs known as yardangs along the horizon.

Curiosity recorded the panorama using its Mastcam on August 11, during the mission's 4,982nd Martian day, or sol. Six separate photographs were transmitted to Earth and combined to create the sweeping view. Unlike most Mastcam photographs, the images were processed without white balancing, allowing the scene to retain the distinctive colors and lighting of the Martian morning.

Curiosity Approaches Mysterious Martian Cliffs

The yardangs stretch approximately 10 miles (16 kilometers) across the northwestern portion of Mount Sharp, a 3-mile-tall (5-kilometer-tall) mountain that Curiosity has been climbing since 2014. The rover recently achieved a major milestone, gaining 0.6 miles (1 kilometer) in elevation, a greater climb than any other rover has completed on Mars.

As Curiosity continues toward these unusual formations, researchers anticipate increasingly detailed views of the cliffs. More importantly, they hope that examining the rocks firsthand will help explain how the remarkable landscape developed.

What Mars' Ancient Rocks Reveal About Its Past

Mount Sharp contains numerous layers of sedimentary rock, each preserving evidence from a different chapter of Martian history. Investigating these layers has helped scientists reconstruct a time billions of years ago when lakes and streams existed in this region of Mars.

As the planet's environment changed, those bodies of water eventually disappeared, leaving deposits of salty minerals behind. Once additional material stopped accumulating on the mountain, powerful winds may have gradually eroded portions of the existing rock, carving out the distinctive yardangs visible today.

Yet the unusual appearance of this particular layer has raised questions about its origins. Its color differs from the surrounding terrain, and its rock layers are tilted in an unexpected direction. Scientists suspect that processes beyond ordinary wind erosion may have contributed to its formation.

"The yardang layer looks out of place. It's the wrong color, the layers tilt at an odd angle, and it almost appears plastered on," said Ashwin Vasavada, Curiosity project scientist of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California. "But that's what makes it exciting to reach. No one is sure exactly what created this layer, but one idea is it may be ash deposited by ancient volcanic eruptions."

NASA Plans to Investigate the Cliffs in 2027

Curiosity is now in the second year of its fifth extended mission, which began with an investigation of unusual spiderweb-like boxwork ridges. Over roughly the next year, the rover will continue traveling through rock layers containing abundant sulfates and carbonates, minerals that provide evidence of ancient drying conditions on Mars.

Mission scientists aim to reach the bottom of the yardang formations sometime in 2027. Once there, Curiosity should be able to examine the mysterious cliffs using its robotic arm, gathering unique measurements that could help determine how they formed and reveal more about the environmental changes that shaped Mars billions of years ago.

#2777. Fifty Billion Seedlings: Can Ethiopia Replant Its Ravaged Forests?

Women water seedlings at a nursery near Buee, Ethiopia. Michael Tewelde / AFP via Getty Images

A legacy of deforestation has left Ethiopia struggling with more intense flooding and drought. The country is trying to restore its native forests through one of the world’s most ambitious and well-funded tree-planting campaigns, but such projects in other nations have often fallen short.

By Samuel Abate and Freddie Clayton • October 1, 2026

Niku Argane’s farm sits on a steep hillside in central Ethiopia’s semi-mountainous Gurage Zone, where years of flooding have carved deep gullies through the land. Soil that once produced enough barley and potatoes to feed his wife and four children — and left a surplus to sell in the market — has eroded, while drought has made what land remains harder to cultivate.

“We have never stopped farming, but our production declined because of land degradation,” the 32-year-old said from his farm in Ambele Kebele.

Over the last century, Ethiopia nearly eradicated its forests as trees were cleared for farming, grazing, and fuel. The loss of tree cover and tree roots allowed wind and rain to wash away fertile topsoil and contributed to declining crop yields. That vulnerability is now being compounded by climate change. Ethiopia is among the countries most vulnerable to its effects: Heavier downpours are sluicing away depleted soils and triggering landslides, while more intense droughts, combined with the loss of shade that trees once provided, rapidly dry out the land.

Land degradation costs the country an estimated $4.3 billion a year through smaller harvests and lost income to farmers, which also means there is less food to eat. With more than 100 million Ethiopians dependent on agriculture for their livelihoods, the consequences reach far beyond the environment. Climate shocks and crop losses are among the biggest drivers of acute hunger; in 2024, 15.8 million Ethiopians were estimated to need food assistance, roughly 12 percent of the population.

Now the country is trying to reverse this trajectory. Argane planted 80 tree seedlings on his farmland and another 50 in a communal area — a small contribution to a vastly larger effort. In just the last seven years, tens of millions of Ethiopians have planted more than 47 billion seedlings across the country in one of the world’s largest environmental restoration campaigns. Volunteers for the program, which aims to plant 50 billion seedlings by 2030, are planting trees to rebuild native forests and integrating them into farmland. In the process, they are sequestering carbon to help protect future generations from ever more extreme weather.

Such programs often fall short, but Ethiopians are finding hope in the scale of their ambitions. “Reforestation is one of the most difficult things you can imagine,” said Aad Kessler, a researcher at the Netherlands’ Wageningen University. “It almost always fails, but this is so big that even if 30 percent of the trees survive, it’s still huge.”

In the early 20th century, forests covered an estimated 35 to 40 percent of Ethiopia. Its highlands were greener, with forests spanning much of the country’s cool, fertile uplands. But as populations grew and settlements spread, trees were cleared for agriculture, pasture, fuel, and construction. As soils lost their fertility, cultivation moved onto increasingly marginal land, including steep slopes, where tillage and the loss of protective vegetation led to the erosion of fertile topsoil, which washed into rivers and reservoirs. By 1989, forest cover was estimated at less than 3 percent, while Ethiopia’s highlands were losing around 1 billion metric tons of topsoil every year.

Farms in North Gondar in the Ethiopian highlands. The spread of agriculture has stripped the region of trees. Danita Delimont Creative

After the devastating droughts and famines of the 1970s and 1980s, Ethiopia’s government and international donor organizations began investing heavily in soil and water conservation, excluding livestock from worn-out land, planting trees across the country, and building terraces and check dams — small, low barriers of rocks and logs placed across gullies — to slow the flow of rainwater. By the 2000s, restoration programs were being implemented at increasing scale.

The government supercharged that effort in 2019 by turning tree-planting into a nationwide campaign with a scheme known as the Green Legacy Initiative, which mobilized millions of individuals and groups, including farmers, public servants, schools, and religious organizations, and distributed free seedlings across the country. In 2024, the government established the Green Legacy and Landscape Restoration Special Fund, committing 0.5 percent to 1 percent of the annual federal budget — roughly $73 million to $145 million — to restoring landscapes. It was an unusually large commitment for a low-income country, and the program’s success and ambition, according to development experts, contributed to the selection of Ethiopia as the host of next year’s U.N. climate conference — a prestigious honor that will help the nation showcase its greening efforts.

In August, 26.6 million Ethiopians planted 800 million tree seedlings in a single day, according to government figures, beating the previous year’s tally of 714.7 million. One of the volunteers, Yeshihareg Teferi, traveled at sunrise from her home in Addis Ababa to Tulu Dimtu so she could hunch in the rain to dig holes in the mud. It was Teferi’s fifth year participating in the annual event. Previously she’d planted indigenous species, including African wild olive and African fern pine. This time, she chose juniper. “We have a responsibility to ensure future generations inherit a green and fertile environment, rather than barren land,” she said.

There are already signs the work is having an impact.

The government reports that national forest cover rose from 17.2 percent in 2019 to 23.6 percent in 2023, while studies of restored areas have found substantial reductions in soil erosion. Between 2013 and 2023, the annual deforestation rate fell from more than 350 square miles to around 100 square miles.

Volunteers plant seedlings on the outskirts of Addis Ababa as part of a nationwide tree-planting drive in early August.

Such efforts can also benefit wildlife. In Abijata-Shalla National Park, in the Ethiopian highlands, workers replanted degraded areas and built terraces and contour bunds (small earthen ridges that run across hillsides to slow runoff and allow water to soak into the soil). As trees and native grasses returned, more water infiltrated into the ground, and water levels on lakes in the park began to rise, said Kumera Wakjira, director-general of the Ethiopian Wildlife Conservation Authority. More water spurred the return of fish and fish-eating birds like cormorants and African darters. Grant’s gazelles and wild dogs are also expanding into areas where they were previously scarce, Wakjira said.

But planted trees are not guaranteed to survive. A 2022 study of 176 reforestation projects in Asia found that around half of planted trees did not live five years. And trees alone do not make a forest, said Endalkachew Wolde-Meskel, a soil scientist and senior researcher at the Center for International Forestry Research: That process usually requires the regeneration of grasses and other vegetation, in addition to soil- and water-conservation measures and management by local communities. “The objective is not really ‘plant trees,’” he said. “It is to restore the functioning of the entire landscape.”

Emiru Birhane Hizikias, an Ethiopian restoration ecologist at Norway’s Nord University, said it was a “blessing, politically speaking,” to have the prime minister lead on tree planting but that the scale of the Green Legacy Initiative had outpaced the systems needed to make it work effectively on the ground. Too often, he said, the focus is on getting huge numbers of seedlings into the earth rather than ensuring their survival. You can’t expect a baby to grow if you don’t care for it, he said, but “that’s how plantation in Ethiopia is being done.”

Abreham Berta Aneseyee, an environmental scientist at Ethiopia’s Wolkite University and an adviser to the Ministry of Agriculture, disputes that characterization, saying the planting campaign is accompanied by practical advice from government experts in forestry and farming. The experts go from farm to farm, advising smallholders on how to plant and care for different types of seedlings, he said, including how deeply to place them in soil and how to conserve soil with structures like bunds. They also advise farmers on managing forests and protecting trees rather than cutting them down.

A 2026 study of more than 44,500 seedlings planted in southern Ethiopia under the Green Legacy Initiative in 2023 found that about 80 percent had survived, with rates significantly higher among older seedlings, wet-season plantings, and seedlings whose planters had received training in best “plantation practices.” In Humbo and Boloso Sore districts in Ethiopia’s southwestern Wolaita Zone, the Green Legacy Initiative has been layered onto longer-running efforts to sustainably manage land, with communities building check dams to stabilize gullies, digging half-moon ditches to trap water and let it soak into the soil, and planting trees alongside grasses and other vegetation.

Left: Half-moon ditches trap runoff, helping to nourish plants and combat erosion. Right: A check dam in a gully stems erosion by slowing the flow of water. Courtesy of the Alliance of Bioversity International and CIAT

Ethiopia’s approach has few direct parallels in Africa for its combination of political leadership, funding, public mobilization, and scale, said Kessler of Wageningen University. He compared the effort with China’s restoration of the Loess Plateau, one of the world’s most highly eroded regions. In the 1990s, China launched major restoration efforts on the plateau, including a World Bank-backed watershed rehabilitation project and, starting in 1999, the Grain for Green program, which paid farmers to restrict cultivation and plant trees, shrubs, and grasses. By 2016, more than 11,500 square miles of rain-fed cropland had been converted to more dense forest or grassland, contributing to a 25 percent increase in vegetative cover in a decade.

The comparison has limits, though. China paid farmers to take degraded slopes out of cultivation, while millions of Ethiopian smallholders still depend on the land for food and livelihoods. But Kessler said the two efforts shared a fundamental approach: “Do it massively, and then see where you end up.”

Kessler’s work, which includes a government-backed field project in Ethiopia, takes a more participatory approach, involving farmers and communities in decisions about what to restore and how. “Of course it is better [than top-down action],” he said, “but it takes more time.”

Even as Ethiopia invests in rebuilding forests and soils, climate change is altering the conditions landscapes must withstand. In the arid pastoral regions of Afar and Somali, the Green Legacy Initiative focuses on drought-tolerant species, but it is unclear whether the trees planted today will be able to withstand the conditions they face decades from now.

Volunteers search for bodies in the aftermath of a 2024 landslide in the Geze Gofa district in southern Ethiopia. Michele Spatari / AFP via Getty Images

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change projects more frequent and intense rainfall in East Africa as global temperatures rise. In July 2024, exceptionally heavy rainfall in Ethiopia’s Geze Gofa district, in the southern highlands, triggered two landslides that killed at least 257 people. Recent studies also suggest that climate change is making El Niños more frequent and severe, with consequences for East Africa. This summer, the Famine Early Warning Systems Network linked El Niño to “historically poor” summer rains in parts of Ethiopia, leaving some areas with their driest June and July on record.

For some farmers, the changing climate has already complicated the most basic requirement of restoration: getting seedlings to survive their first season. Belayneh Mamo, 65, a farmer from Ethiopia’s northern and drought-prone Amhara region, relies on July rainfall for his teff, sorghum, maize, and wheat for bulgur. He also receives government-provided coffee, neem, and gesho seeds, but was unable to plant any crop until August, when the season’s first rains arrived.

“In previous years, September was a period when crops were already developing and food such as bulgur and maize were becoming available for consumption,” he said. “The delays affected the seedlings we plant, our crops, and the availability of animal feed.”

On Argane’s hillside in the Gurage Zone, however, signs of restoration are already visible. Since 2019, he has planted grass, fodder, fenugreek, and fruit trees along the edges of his fields, helping to hold soil in place while providing feed for the four cows, six goats, and three sheep that he keeps for milk and meat. His barley harvest has risen from about 1,800 pounds per acre to roughly 3,200, while the trees and grasses have reduced erosion and slowed the flow of water across his land during heavy rains.

“Restoring degraded land isn’t just about protecting the environment,” Argane said. “It’s also about making life better for my family and community.”

#2779. Toronto’s mayoral race has more than two candidates — why haven’t we heard from them?

Denise Balkissoon joined The Narwhal in 2021 as a founding member of its Ontario bureau. Her first (unpaid) job, as child to a municipal politician, saw her deliver many flyers and knock on many doors in north Scarborough.

This article was produced in collaboration with the Toronto Star.

Every election, I consider whether I’m voting for a candidate — or against one. My dream is to hopefully fill in the bubble beside the name of someone exciting. Instead, I often find myself drearily choosing someone okay (I guess) to block someone I can’t stand. The upcoming Toronto mayoral election is a case in point.

The two front-runners each have a caricaturish portrait of the city. The incumbent, Olivia Chow, lives in a shimmery mirage where Care Bear stares transform traffic jams into affordable homes. Her challenger, Brad Bradford, lives in an apocalyptic hellhole where rats keep trying to gnaw his leg off. The rest of us watch as this once-shiny city dulls, made sticky by a spiderweb of problems. We need sophisticated, strategic ideas, and have been given a choice between cops and school lunches.

No, actually. There will be 53 mayoral candidates’ names on the ballot on Oct. 26. But Torontonians tend to ignore the so-called fringe, perhaps burned by eras when kooky or frightening candidates got too much airtime. We rarely meet, never mind evaluate, let alone choose, a promising unknown.

Hoping to find a bubble worth filling, I went in search of options — and actually found a little hope. Before I name names, though, here’s how I did it.

First, I used the Toronto Star’s Vote Compass tool, which showed how my takes on the city’s challenges, costs and priorities compared to candidates’ positions. I used that to narrow down which platforms to read in The Local’s candidate tracker, clicking through to websites of those who seemed serious and promising.

It wasn’t quick, but given the threats to democracy from misinformation, shrinking attention spans, dark money, competing crises and plain old apathy, the effort seemed worth it. Then again, I get to count this as work. Not everyone has the time or ability to do it. And Toronto has a type of electoral system that is “really challenging for voters,” according to Jack Lucas, a political science professor at the University of Calgary.

Lucas is co-director of the Canadian Municipal Barometer, an ongoing study of local government. One of his interests is the role and value of political parties at the municipal level. In some provinces, municipal candidates can run with party affiliation, as they do federally or provincially. In Ontario, including Toronto, each candidate — and winner — is a lone wolf.

Theoretically, this discourages hard divides between “conservatives” and “progressives,” since councillors vote independently on every issue, allowing them to disagree or join forces from one cause to the next. Lucas says polarization is happening anyway, though, which the cops vs. school lunches showdown suggests is true.

Meanwhile, voters are left adrift in a sea of unknown candidates. All alone, they need to seek out and analyze information on not just policy, but integrity, experience and the ability to actually get the job done. Lucas believes a party system helps voters discern who’s worth their time. Without them, he says, it’s not surprising people gravitate towards known quantities.

I’m not quite sold — to me, independence and coalition-building are unique, important aspects of municipal governance we should try to strengthen, not abandon. Also, parties can be risk-averse and boring, stoking stereotypes and prejudice. But Lucas did sway me when he said the muscle of party machinery can help new candidates find their feet. “It’s a lonely enterprise,” he said, “and if you don’t already have a network of support behind you, it can be really challenging to break through.” That, plus parties have money.

Which brings me to mayoral candidate Laura Dean, who has raised $25,000 for her campaign. It’s more than she expected, she told me. It’s also oodles less than the nearly $1.6 million Chow spent in 2023. It’s not enough to buy even one ad between Blue Jays’ innings, Dean said, let alone the back-to-back spots Bradford is running.

Dean has spent more than a decade advising Ontario municipalities on land use (including housing starts!) as a partner at a Bay Street law firm. It’s a career she chose after time spent at the Ministry of Municipal Affairs and Housing and the now-defunct provincial climate secretariat. That last bit is what made this environmental journalist give her a call.

“When this election cycle came up, I was really shocked by the tone and just the quality of the discourse — it’s so poor. And, more, extremely polarizing,” Dean said about deciding to run. “The Canadian public so desperately needs more options and good quality conversation.”

Dean isn’t starry-eyed about her prospects and that’s fine. Plenty of worthwhile candidates need to try and try again. The point is, not every unknown is kooky or scary. Many are worthwhile, and with dozens of options, it’s bad practice to let polls decide front-runners (and debate invitees) before the race even starts. If a wider choice sounds appealing, Dean and other non-celebrity candidates will headline an “alternative” mayoral debate at City Hall on Oct. 20.

Dean said the enthusiasm she’s received has her already considering her options for 2030. While many people say they’re glad she’s running, she said, they aren’t ready to fill her bubble just yet. Torontonians are practiced in voting for the person they consider okay (I guess) as they try to block the candidate they can’t stand. Maybe next time, we’ll believe we deserve better.

#2785. Scientists discover a surprising secret behind the human brain’s wrinkles

Most primates have relatively large brains covered in distinctive folds and grooves. However, common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) are an unusual exception. These small monkeys have brains that are almost entirely smooth, with very little folding. Scientists at the German Primate Center (DPZ), the Leibniz Institute for Primate Research in Göttingen, have now identified several cellular processes that help explain this difference.

Using miniature brain structures known as organoids, the researchers discovered that certain neural progenitor cells, which produce neurons, develop differently in marmosets than in humans. These differences involve the cells' structure, the speed at which they divide, and the timing of their development. Together, these changes reduce the number of neurons produced, contributing to the smaller size and smoother surface of the marmoset brain. Published in Science Advances, the findings provide new insights into primate brain evolution and could eventually improve our understanding of human brain development disorders.

Why Some Primate Brains Are Smooth Instead of Folded

Brain size and the extent of surface folding are important features that distinguish different primate species. The human brain, for example, has a highly folded surface resembling the ridges and grooves of a walnut. This arrangement greatly expands the brain's surface area, creating room for billions of neurons that support complex thinking and other advanced mental abilities.

Scientists believe that the common ancestor of modern primates already possessed a moderately sized brain with at least some folding. Yet not all primates living today retain these characteristics.

Common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus), small monkeys native to South America that are becoming increasingly important in biomedical research, have brains with almost no visible folds. Researchers at the German Primate Center set out to determine why their brains develop so differently from those of humans and other primates.

"We wanted to understand which changes at the cellular level cause the marmoset brain to grow less and form fewer folds," explains Lidiia Tynianskaia, one of the study's two first authors and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution at the DPZ. "At the beginning of development, the common marmoset brain exhibits the typical structure and composition of a large, folded primate brain. As development progresses, processes must therefore occur that effectively slow down the production of nerve cells."

Scientists Discover What Slows Brain Growth

To investigate these developmental differences, the team compared brain organoids grown from human and common marmoset cells. Their attention centered on neural progenitor cells, the cells responsible for generating neurons during brain development.

These progenitor cells play a major role in determining the eventual size and shape of the brain. The more neurons they produce, the greater the potential for the cerebral cortex to expand and develop folds.

The researchers found that the marmoset's smaller, smoother cerebral cortex is not the result of a single developmental change. Instead, several differences involving distinct types of progenitor cells work together to limit neuron production and brain folding.

"Our investigations have shown that certain progenitor cells in the common marmoset divide significantly more slowly than in humans," explains César Mateo Bastidas Betancourt, also a first author of the study and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group. "Other progenitor cells have a simpler structure than their human counterparts, with fewer processes, and are therefore less proliferative. Both of these factors ultimately result in fewer nerve cells, which contributes to a smaller size and less folding of the cerebral cortex in marmosets."

The scientists also identified important differences in when certain progenitor cell characteristics and behaviors emerge during development. Compared with humans, marmosets have a shorter overall period during which these cells can multiply rapidly.

Together, the slower rate of cell division, simpler cell structures, and shorter window for rapid growth help explain why the marmoset brain produces fewer neurons and develops far fewer folds.

Miniature Brains Reveal Important Developmental Clues

Much of the research relied on brain organoids, small, three-dimensional structures grown from cells that reproduce important features of early brain development. These miniature models allowed scientists to investigate developmental changes across multiple samples and compare the timing of key processes.

"The study combines the advantages of in vivo and in vitro methods," says Michael Heide, head of the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution. "Organoids are well-suited for obtaining statistically robust results because such sample sizes are not feasible in primates. We subsequently repeated some key experiments in fetal brain tissue to confirm the results from the organoids."

One particularly important finding involved the relationship between development in organoids and development in actual marmoset brains. The researchers determined that a marmoset brain organoid grown for 50 days closely matches the developmental stage of a natural marmoset brain at day 90.

Establishing this relationship was essential because brain organoids and actual brains do not necessarily develop at the same rate. By identifying corresponding developmental stages, the scientists could make meaningful comparisons and interpret their experimental findings more reliably.

What Smooth Monkey Brains Reveal About Human Brain Evolution

The findings help explain how relatively small differences in the behavior and developmental timing of brain-building cells can contribute to major differences in brain size and folding among primates.

They also offer a potential explanation for how the wide variety of primate brain shapes and sizes emerged over evolutionary time, including why some species developed relatively smooth brains while others possess much larger, heavily folded ones.

Beyond their evolutionary significance, these discoveries provide a foundation for investigating how the human brain develops and what happens when those processes are disrupted. A better understanding of the cellular mechanisms that control neuron production and brain growth could ultimately help researchers study developmental disorders affecting the human brain.

#2787. For the First Time in a Century, the Atlantic May See No Hurricanes

August and September passed without a single Atlantic hurricane, thanks in part to a powerful El Niño. Forecasters are now raising the prospect that the season could conclude with zero Atlantic hurricanes.

“We are witnessing one of the most extraordinary Atlantic hurricane seasons in modern history,” said Alex DaSilva, lead hurricane expert at AccuWeather. “There is an increasingly likely scenario in which the Atlantic season ends without a single hurricane for the first time in more than a century.” The last time was in 1914, and before that 1907.

The possibility of a hurricane-free season is made all the more remarkable by the fact that early weather records are highly suspect, as weather watchers would have failed to count hurricanes that formed over remote parts of the Atlantic. It is very possible that 1914 and 1907 produced uncounted hurricanes. Reliable data dates back only to 1966, the year the U.S. began routine satellite weather monitoring.

Why has this year been so unusually quiet? It is not for a lack of the ocean heat that fuels the formation of hurricanes. Sea surface temperatures in the Atlantic are the second warmest on record. But a super El Niño, which scientists say has been intensified by warming, is driving powerful westerly winds. These winds are thwarting the formation of hurricanes in the Atlantic, according to John Morales, a meteorologist at the NBC affiliate in Miami.

“Plus, the air over the Atlantic basin has been mostly sinking,” he said. “Clouds don’t form in sinking air,” much less the clusters of thunderstorms that eventually give rise to cyclones. At the same time, experts say, dry air has also stymied the formation of hurricanes.

The Atlantic hurricane season typically peaks in late August and early September, with diminished odds of a hurricane thereafter. Of the three previous super El Niños, only one produced an Atlantic hurricane after October 1.

“Don’t tune out. Stay informed,” said Morales. “But fingers crossed that the rest of the 2026 Atlantic hurricane season stays as dead as a doornail.”

In Kaska territory, summer turns toward fall earlier than in much of British Columbia. Pressed up against the Yukon border, where the Alaska Highway cuts across the landscape, it’s a place where leaves start changing colour in August, and by September, snow already dusts the mountaintops. Seasonal change has always held meaning for Kaska, and this year autumn was supposed to mark something new: the final few steps in their effort to protect a massive swath of their territory, called Dene Kʼéh Kusān.

But when BC NDP Leader David Eby called a snap election in late September, Kaska Nation leaders say it stalled that process. Now, they’re worried the election could derail, or at least slow, the entire multi-year planning process for the roughly three-million-hectare protected area.

“We’re disappointed an election call has once again interrupted our discussions with the government as we had hoped to achieve a protection designation within the last political mandate,” Liard First Nation Chief Harlan Schilling said in an emailed statement.

Liard First Nation is one of four nations that make up the broader Kaska Nation, whose traditional territory stretches across northern British Columbia and southern Yukon. Two decades ago, the nation created the non-profit Dena Kayeh Institute to protect its language, culture and lands. In June 2019, the institute announced plans to create a massive Indigenous Protected and Conserved Area to protect one of the province’s largest intact wilderness areas. It is called Dene Kʼéh Kusān, which, translated from the Kaska language, means “always be there.”

“Dene K’éh Kusān is a vision that includes all British Columbians. It will protect some of Canada’s most important wildlife habitat and create world-class opportunities for hunters, anglers, naturalists and backcountry recreationalists,” Schilling said.

In June 2024, the British Columbia government signed an agreement with the Kaska nations to undertake land-use planning in its territories. In the coming years, the ministry explained, the government, the nation and other stakeholders would sit down for a comprehensive land-use planning process that would formalize provincial protections for Dene Kʼéh Kusān. That process stalled a few months later when the province went to the polls in October 2024, but picked up again after the election. On July 2, 2026, the province issued a press release announcing public consultations on the new protected area.

“This work shows what’s possible when we all come together to protect the places that matter most,” Tamara Davidson, then-minister of environment and parks, said at the time.

That process was supposed to conclude this fall, with Schilling expecting to finalize protections for the area before the end of the year. Now, the election has stalled the process. He’s worried about the lasting impact the delay could have, especially since a temporary ban on mineral staking inside the area is set to expire in January.“The current pause on mineral staking in Dene K’éh Kusān is set to expire just three months after the election,” he said.

Since the election was called, polling shows the BC Conservatives with a narrow but consistent lead over the BC NDP. Projections from 338Canada point to a likely change in government, with the Conservatives currently expected to win the most seats.

Tim Burkhart, director of landscape protection at the conservation group Yellowstone to Yukon, said in an emailed statement the next government will have “a generational opportunity” to protect the unique wilderness area, but added time is running out, given the scheduled expiry of the mineral tenure pause. “The window to act is closing,” he said.

“The years of planning, broad stakeholder endorsement and public engagement are done, and there is consensus to secure this wild legacy forever,” Burkhart, whose group supports the Kaska project, said.

“We want to ensure all parties know protecting this unique wilderness is important for British Columbians and Canadians,” Schilling said.

No major party directly addresses Dene Kʼéh Kusān in its platform, but the BC NDP has reaffirmed a commitment to meet the pledge to protect 30 per cent of B.C. lands and waters by 2030, known as 30-by-30, and “work with First Nations on this goal.” As of December 2025, 15.9 per cent of the province was protected areas, according to the federal government. Since Dene Kʼéh Kusān covers around four per cent of the province, it would go a long way toward closing the gap.

The BC Conservatives have made no specific promises or platform announcements on conservation in this election. And in May 2024, former BC Conservative leader John Rustad described the 30-by-30 conservation goal as “nonsense” and pledged to scrap it if he formed government.

The Narwhal reached out to the BC Conservative and BC NDP candidates running in the riding of Bulkley-Stikine, where most of Dene Kʼéh Kusān would sit, but neither responded. The BC Green Party is not running a candidate in the riding.

For now, Schilling and others working on protecting the region will have to wait until the votes are counted on Oct. 24 to figure out the path forward.“We’re hopeful the next government will see the importance of this initiative and bring it to completion,” Schilling said.

#2796. Scientists discover a hidden immune weapon against deadly lung cancer

Scientists have identified a previously overlooked type of immune cell that can detect and destroy small cell lung cancer, the most aggressive form of lung cancer worldwide.

The discovery, reported in the journal Cancer Cell by researchers at WEHI, revealed that patients with greater numbers of these specialized immune cells tended to survive longer. The findings point to a previously unrecognized immune defense that could provide a new way to attack tumors that are notoriously difficult to treat.

The research team also demonstrated that medications already in use could enhance the ability of these immune cells to kill cancer. This approach could eventually improve existing treatments and increase the number of patients who benefit from immunotherapy.

Why Small Cell Lung Cancer Is So Difficult to Treat

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is among the deadliest cancers, with fewer than 7% of patients surviving for five years after diagnosis.

Immunotherapy has dramatically improved treatment for several types of cancer, but its success against SCLC has been much more limited. Only a small proportion of patients experience lasting benefits.

A major challenge is the cancer's ability to escape immune surveillance. SCLC tumors frequently avoid detection by the conventional immune cells that many immunotherapy treatments depend on, allowing the disease to persist despite the body's natural defenses.

The findings published in Cancer Cell suggest there may be another way for the immune system to recognize and attack these tumors. By identifying a previously overlooked immune pathway, researchers have uncovered a potential strategy for overcoming the cancer's ability to remain hidden.

Professor Kate Sutherland, the study's senior author and a laboratory head at WEHI, said the discovery could help address a longstanding challenge in lung cancer research: finding ways to make immunotherapy effective for more patients.

"For decades, we've known that small cell lung cancer is exceptionally good at hiding from the immune system," Prof Sutherland said.

"We've found an immune cell that appears to be able to see through that disguise.

"Strikingly, patients whose tumors contained more of these specialized immune cells often had better treatment outcomes.

"This is the strongest evidence yet that gamma delta T cells are present in small cell lung cancers and appear capable of fighting the disease.

"It suggests these cells may offer an alternative immune attack route for tumors that are able to evade conventional cancer-fighting immune cells, opening up new possibilities for treating a cancer that has seen few advances over the past decades."

Miniature Tumors Reveal a Powerful Immune Response

To investigate whether gamma delta T cells could directly attack lung cancer, the scientists developed miniature tumors in the laboratory using tissue samples donated by patients. The samples were obtained through the Victorian Cancer Biobank in partnership with the Royal Melbourne Hospital.

These miniature tumors, often called "mini tumors," provide one of the most realistic laboratory models of SCLC created so far, allowing researchers to study how cancer behaves and responds to potential treatments.

The team then introduced gamma delta T cells collected from the same patients' blood into the tumor models. The results showed that these immune cells were highly effective at identifying and destroying SCLC cells.

The scientists went a step further by testing whether two medications already approved for other uses could make this immune response even stronger. The drugs were tarlatamab (a cancer treatment approved for SCLC) and zoledronate (approved for osteoporosis and cancer-related bone disease).

Each medication contributed in a different way. Zoledronate increased the number of available gamma delta T cells and improved their ability to identify the lung cancer tumors. Tarlatamab helped bring the immune cells and cancer cells together, effectively directing the immune attack toward the tumors.

When used together, the drugs substantially increased the ability of gamma delta T cells to kill cancer cells in laboratory experiments. The findings suggest that combining existing treatments could provide a new strategy for strengthening the immune system's response to SCLC.

Could Existing Drugs Lead to New Lung Cancer Therapies?

Dr. Jin Ng, the study's first author, said the discovery could also support a treatment approach known as adoptive cell transfer. This involves collecting immune cells, multiplying them outside the body, and then delivering large numbers of those cells back into a patient to help fight cancer.

Because methods for expanding and reinfusing gamma delta T cells have already been developed, the researchers believe their findings could help accelerate the development of new treatments.

"For patients with lower gamma delta T cell levels, therapies such as zoledronate may offer a way to boost their cancer-fighting immune cells and responsiveness to treatments," Dr. Ng said.

"As therapies that expand and reinfuse gamma delta T cells already exist, our findings raise the exciting possibility of rapidly translating this discovery into new treatment strategies.

"This could open an entirely new avenue for personalized treatment approaches in small cell lung cancer and other solid cancers that are often missed by conventional immune cells."

Clues From Major Lung Cancer Immunotherapy Trials

The investigation began with an examination of data from two influential clinical trials, IMpower133 and CASPIAN. Both studies helped establish immunotherapy as a standard treatment option for patients with small cell lung cancer.

Researchers noticed evidence pointing toward an important role for gamma delta T cells and used sophisticated genomic and computational techniques to investigate further.

Analyses conducted by WEHI's genomics and bioinformatics teams revealed that patients with stronger gamma delta T cell signatures were among those who benefited most from immunotherapy.

Together with the laboratory findings, these results strengthen the evidence that gamma delta T cells play an important role in the immune response to SCLC and could become useful targets for future therapies.

The WEHI team is now working with scientists at the Doherty Institute to build on the discovery, with the goal of translating the findings into a new class of immunotherapy drugs for small cell lung cancer.

The research received support from Cancer Council Victoria, the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), The CASS Foundation, and The International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer.

#2797. When Bolsonaro Tweeted, Amazon Deforestation Surged

Jair Bolsonaro in 2020. Marcos Corrêa via Flickr

As president of Brazil, Jair Bolsonaro not only weakened environmental regulations and gutted agencies charged with protecting the Amazon, he also railed against conservation. An analysis of his tweets and those of his ministers finds a link between their rhetoric and the acres of forest lost.

The study looked at thousands of tweets from Bolsonaro and his ministers of agriculture, environment, and foreign affairs from 2019, their first year in office. It identified 38 posts which signaled that illicit deforesters faced little risk of reprisal from the government.

Comparing these posts against a database of Amazon deforestation, the study found that forest clearing surged in the months when Bolsonaro and his ministers inveighed against conservation on Twitter. In 2019 alone, it estimates, such tweets were linked with the destruction of as many as 420,000 acres of rainforest. The findings were published in The Journal of Politics.

In 2022, Bolsonaro was defeated in his bid for reelection by Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, who has since ramped up enforcement in the Amazon, leading to a sharp drop in acres cleared. This year, deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon is on track to hit its lowest level on record.

A new analysis from Carbon Brief finds that, compared to a scenario in which Bolsonaro won a second term and deforestation remained high, Lula has managed to save 5 million acres of rainforest, an area roughly the size of New Jersey.

This week, Brazilians will head to the polls to decide their next president in a contest that will pit Lula against Flávio Bolsonaro, son of the former president. Flávio has taken a similar stance on environmental protection as his father. Analysts say the election, in which the candidates are tied in the polls, could be hugely consequential for the future of the Amazon rainforest.

#2798. What does Bill C-39 mean for First Nations? Senators question Carney government

Carl Meyer has been reporting on environmental and climate policy for a decade and is a member of the Parliamentary press gallery in Ottawa.

When Senator Michèle Audette questioned the Canadian government over its latest plan to fast-track industrial projects, she started by speaking in Innu-Aimun.

For the senator, an Indigenous women’s rights advocate who was a commissioner in the National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls, speaking the language was a way to centre her Innu community’s past, as Canada tries to shape its future.

“It was to remind, in that important space, that there was a moment when the government had tried to erase us through legislation and policies,” Sen. Audette, who comes from the First Nation of Innu Takuaikan Uashat Mak Mani-Utenam in eastern Quebec, told The Narwhal in an interview.

The people she was reminding were a trio of federal ministers who had appeared at a special Senate hearing on Oct. 6 to answer questions about Prime Minister Mark Carney’s Bill C-39, the Building Canada Strong Act.

The 243-page omnibus bill is the latest step in the government’s controversial reshaping of how major projects are scrutinized and approved in Canada. Major projects are things like mines, ports or pipelines, and can affect everything from watersheds to wildlife and can lead to changes in greenhouse gas emissions and public health.

Among other changes, the bill would centralize many Indigenous consultations under a new hub within the Impact Assessment Agency of Canada, limit environmental assessments of all projects subject to federal reviews to a maximum of one year, and consolidate all required permits into a single decision issued by a single minister after consulting with colleagues.

It would also let projects inside designated zones, deemed “regions of national interest,” bypass normal approval processes, allowing some to start before the assessment is complete and empowering the government to change or remove conditions that have already been imposed on a project.

Carney’s government has said the bill is a response to complaints from workers, investors, communities and Indigenous groups that approval processes are too slow. It said new legislation is needed to get federal authorities to work together more effectively, so big projects are built faster.

“This in no way reduces our responsibility to assess projects against environmental standards or to fulfill our obligations to Indigenous Peoples,” Privy Council president Dominic LeBlanc, who is responsible for the government’s major projects and free trade push, told the senators.

But a wide swath of Indigenous leadership across the country has united in opposition to what they perceive as the bill’s infringement on the constitutional rights that guarantee an ability to influence decisions that affect their territories.

Many have also recoiled at the majority government’s hurry to transform its bill into law before the end of the year. Special committees in the House of Commons and Senate have been set up to examine the bill simultaneously, instead of the standard procedure where committees at each level take up a bill in succession.

“Prime Minister Carney is weaponizing the parliamentary process and his majority to force the bill through Parliament [and] override the inherent, Treaty and constitutionally protected rights of First Nations,” a joint statement from the Chiefs of Ontario, the British Columbia Assembly of First Nations, the Assembly of First Nations Quebec-Labrador and the Nishnawbe Aski Nation of northern Ontario said on Oct. 8.

“This extraordinary maneuver has one purpose: to push the most consequential economic legislation in a generation through Parliament before First Nations, or anyone else, can meaningfully scrutinize it. It is fundamentally antidemocratic.”

The Carney government has given itself broad discretion to determine what exactly is in the “national interest.”

Last year’s Building Canada Act, which first established this special designation for projects, laid out different elements the government “may consider” when determining “national interest” status.

These are: Canada’s autonomy, resilience and security; the economic or other benefits to the country, the likelihood of the project’s success, how it advances Indigenous interests and how it contributes to clean growth or tackling climate change. But the government also allowed itself to consider “any factor” it deemed relevant, and these elements are not required to all be met as a prerequisite to projects being given the “national interest” designation.

In fact, the first project the Carney government listed was the West Coast Oil pipeline, now named Pacific Link, which the Canadian Climate Institute expects will usher in 20 million tonnes per year of new greenhouse gas emissions — not exactly the type of project that tackles climate change. Meanwhile, the Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis has said the economics of the pipeline are also debatable.

The new bill extends the same set of broad parameters and discretion to determining “regions of national interest.”

As she questioned the federal ministers who had come to answer questions on Bill C-39, Sen. Audette asked if their examination of these regions would include “not only the Indigenous Peoples who are directly affected by the projects but also those upstream and downstream who will feel the environmental, social and economic impacts.”

She told The Narwhal she is concerned the bill may frame Indigenous consultations over these zones as primarily being related to development. Some First Nations’ traditional territories cover vast areas and may overlap with the zones, for instance, even if their actual reserves may not.

“They’ll say, ‘Oh, there’s no First Nation, there’s no reserve on that road,’ but go back before it became Canada, we were there,” she said. “We are still there. We never surrendered.”

The government has said “national interest” regions could be anything from an industrial area to one earmarked for “energy production” or a telecommunications network. The label could even be applied without a specific project in mind, but for “future development needs.”

It said it would first carry out a regional assessment to consider cumulative impacts of development in the area, which would include Indigenous consultations, before designating a zone.

“We don’t want to be a third party or a stakeholder,” Sen. Audette said. “Even if you don’t see a reserve, we are involved, and we want to make sure that what you do, we are at the same level, on the same page.”

In his response to the senator on Oct. 6, Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon, who is also the bill’s sponsor, said First Nations would be involved “at the very beginning of the process, from the conception stage onwards.” He also said he and LeBlanc have already “experienced a lot of very positive feedback from rights holders” about the government’s new approval process for projects.

Senator Suze Youance, a civil engineer, told the federal ministers she felt Bill C-39’s shortening of approval timelines was “causing a lot of concern among environmental groups and for certain communities.”

She asked whether the government had considered changing its proposed process “in order to find an acceptable middle ground between economic haste and environmental prudence.”

LeBlanc responded, “The way we reconcile those priorities is reflected in our commitment to act rigorously and in the fact that environmental standards have not changed, nor have our responsibilities toward Indigenous Rights holders.”

Senator Rosa Galvez, an expert on pollution and health who introduced legislation to align the financial sector with climate objectives, said that while Canada has been slow at building some projects, the reason “was not always because of environmental concerns or Indigenous Peoples’ rights. It was also due to a lack of investment.”

She said, during some previous nation-building moments in Canada’s history, reforms that were meant to simplify the regulatory process actually “brought new forms of fragmentation.”

“How will scientific independence be maintained when a specialized regulator assumes greater responsibility for environmental reviews?” she asked.

LeBlanc said the responsibility around oversight evaluation was not being diminished and that the regulators that are responsible will still work with other federal, provincial and independent agencies, and include public consultations.

“The public participation process under any of these agencies doing these reviews has not been changed,” LeBlanc said. “Public participation remains.”
