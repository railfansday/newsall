#451. Your guide to Proposition 39: Stricter voter ID requirements

See more from the L.A. Times in Google Search. Set us as preferred

Proposition 39 brings the national debate around U.S. election integrity home to California by proposing stricter new voter ID requirements for state elections — mandating that voters present government identification every time they vote in person, or cite a specific government ID number when voting by mail.

The measure is backed by prominent California Republicans and ultrawealthy supporters of President Trump, who is pushing even more onerous voter ID measures at the national level. The measure’s backers say it is necessary to prevent voter fraud and block noncitizens from casting ballots. They contend, without evidence, that such problems are widespread in California.

The measure is opposed by the state’s Democratic establishment and prominent civil rights groups, who say it will disenfranchise many eligible voters, particularly those of color, who are poor or live in rural communities, or who move often or have changed their names. They say the latter group would include many married women.

The opponents also contend — as do many independent election experts — that California already has robust measures for verifying the eligibility and identity of voters, and that voter fraud, including by noncitizens, is exceedingly rare.

A poll by the UC Berkeley Institute of Governmental Studies and co-sponsored by The Times last year found that most Californians support the idea of requiring a government ID every time a voter casts a ballot. A more recent Berkeley IGS poll found that when voters were given the added context that supporters say the measure is necessary to prevent fraud and opponents say it could disenfranchise eligible voters, they were much more split.

Proposition 39 would require anyone voting in person in California to present a government-issued ID, and those voting by mail ballot to include the last four digits of the government-issued ID number they provided when they registered to vote.

In-person voters without ID would be turned away, while mail ballots without an attached ID number that matches their registration would be rejected. Critics note the measure does not explicitly require election officials to reach out to voters to correct or “cure” a ballot that is missing an ID number before rejecting it, as they are required to do when a voter’s ballot signature does not match what’s on file.

The measure also would require the state to provide free voter ID cards to eligible voters upon request, and state and county election officials to verify the U.S. citizenship of registered voters using government data and publicly report the findings of that work.

Under current law, Californians are required to provide identification when registering to vote and must swear under penalty of perjury — a felony — that they are eligible to vote and a U.S. citizen. They must sign their mail ballots, and their signature is compared to the one the state has on file to verify their identity. It is already against the law for noncitizens to vote.

Proposition 39’s provisions echo but are less onerous than those proposed in the SAVE America Act, the strict voter ID and proof of citizenship measure Trump is pushing in Congress.

The Legislative Analyst’s Office estimated Proposition 39 would “increase state and local government costs each year by tens of millions of dollars to low hundreds of millions of dollars.”

Proposition 39 is supported by leading state Republicans and backed by major Republican and Trump donors.

The campaign to collect signatures and get the measure onto the ballot was led by Julie Luckey, director of the group Californians for Voter ID, who has insisted that it is “not a Republican or Democratic issue,” but “common-sense policy.”

Luckey is the mother of billionaire tech entrepreneur Palmer Luckey, a major Trump fundraiser and Republican donor and the co-founder of Anduril, which manufactures autonomous weapons systems and recently won a $363-million contract with U.S. Customs and Border Protection for hundreds of autonomous surveillance towers along the southern border.

According to campaign finance reports, Californians for Voter ID has been bankrolled by other Trump megadonors as well, including Richard Uihlein, chief executive of the shipping and packing supplies company Uline, who has given the campaign $17 million.

Another major backer is Assemblymember Carl DeMaio (R-San Diego) and his Reform California group, which has given the campaign more than $3 million and unsuccessfully challenged the state’s official summary of the measure, which describes it as one that “prohibits citizens from voting” unless they provide a government-issued ID.

DeMaio has said the measure will “restore integrity” in California elections, including by ensuring only citizens vote, and would be viewed as a “watershed event” where Republicans “turned the tide in California against far-left insanity.”

Proposition 39 is opposed by leading state Democrats, major civil rights organizations including the American Civil Liberties Union and major labor unions in the state, who say the measure would disenfranchise eligible voters and expose voters to identity theft by forcing them to write personal ID numbers on mail ballot envelopes.

According to campaign finance reports, the ACLU of Northern California has contributed hundreds of thousands of dollars to defeating the measure. Quinn Delaney, a major Democratic donor and fundraiser for former President Obama, and Patty Quillin, a major philanthropist and wife of Netflix co-founder Reed Hastings, both have given $1.5 million.

The Service Employees International Union Local 2015, which represents long-term caregivers, provided $1 million, the SEIU California State Council for Working People gave $500,000, and the California Teachers Assn., through its independent expenditure committee, gave another $500,000.

Arnulfo De La Cruz, president of SEIU California and SEIU Local 2015, said in a statement that the group’s members “know our nation is stronger when working people make their voices heard and have their ballots counted,” and that strict voter ID laws “are part of Trump and MAGA extremists’ agenda to rig the election system so they can cling onto their own power and get richer from corporate corruption.”

Sen. Alex Padilla (D-Calif.) recently convened a news conference where a slate of Democratic leaders — including state Senate President Pro Tempore Monique Limón (D-Goleta) and Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas (D-Hollister) — called on Californians to vote no on Proposition 39.

The Democrats called the measure a “dirty trick” from “Trump acolytes” akin to historic poll taxes designed to disenfranchise Black voters, and a power grab that would seriously harm millions of eligible voters, especially in Black and Latino communities.

“Proposition 39 is a direct assault on everything California stands for. Our people, our values and our fundamental belief that every eligible voter here in California deserves to have their voice heard and deserves to have their vote counted,” Rivas said.

Trump and his allies have for years claimed widespread fraud in U.S. elections, particularly in big blue states such as California. After his return to the White House last year, Trump began a fresh crusade to rewrite election laws across the country.

Courts have rejected those efforts repeatedly, including on the grounds that the president does not have authority over elections. Backers of Proposition 39, including DeMaio, have been pushing for voter reform for years, but the effort gained steam this year as frustration with the limits on Trump’s executive power over elections mounted among his supporters.

DeMaio has said that the measure is badly needed to disrupt fraud and address plummeting public trust in elections. Padilla has said it is being pushed solely because “Donald Trump is scared” of his party losing power.

#452. Inside Meta’s Efforts to Prevent School Shootings

Larry Baimbridge spent thirty-one years with the Houston Police Department, including stints as a homicide detective and as commander of the SWAT team. In 2023, he took a job as chief of police for the Spring Branch Independent School District, most of which is within the city. The job was more low-key—no more shootouts with snipers—but it came with its own challenges. After the 2022 shooting in Uvalde, Texas, which left nineteen elementary-school students and two teachers dead, the state legislature had neglected to pass any meaningful restrictions on guns; instead, the state required that every school campus have an armed security officer. “Of course, they didn’t provide enough funding for it, but that was the mandate,” Baimbridge said.

The Spring Branch district served thirty-one thousand students across forty-odd campuses—“a very wealthy area on one side of the freeway and a very poor area on the other side,” Baimbridge said. Late in the school year, on February 18, 2025, he got a phone call from Brian Ennesser, a supervisory special agent at the F.B.I.’s Houston field office. The two men had met the year before, when Ennesser formed a threat-mitigation task force, a group that specializes in disrupting targeted mass violence, including at schools. Baimbridge knew right away that something was up; Ennesser usually texted or e-mailed. “Hey, man, we’ve got a problem,” Ennesser said.

The F.B.I. had received a tip about a potential threat to a local school. It involved two teen-agers, one a student at Spring Branch, the other at a school district north of Houston. Both were girls. “Once we identified it being two females, we were double-checking our work,” Ennesser later told me.

Baimbridge got to the Spring Branch school quickly and briefed its administrators on the threat. As they walked down a hallway, a young woman came out of the bathroom. The assistant principal’s eyes widened—it was the girl they were looking for. Baimbridge stopped her and asked her to come with them. “And she just kind of put her head down, which, from my years in homicide, I knew if I got a head slump I’ve got them,” he told me. “She didn’t ask why.”

Baimbridge later learned that the two teen-agers had met in a therapy group; when their parents didn’t want them to meet up outside of treatment, they kept in touch online. At some point, they allegedly discussed plans for a violent attack. That day, as Ennesser and a handful of other F.B.I. agents spoke with one of the girls in a conference room, Baimbridge sat outside with her mother. With the woman’s permission, he pulled a binder from the girl’s bag and began to flip through it. He was struck by the skill of her drawings. Then he stopped on a page that appeared to be a jumble of names, places, and dates connected by lines, like some kind of confounding word map. “You know that movie ‘A Beautiful Mind’?” he asked me. In the middle of the page were two names: Eric Harris and Dylan Klebold, the perpetrators of the Columbine High School murders. (Both girls pleaded guilty, one to conspiracy to commit murder and the other to making terrorist threats; they will remain on probation until age eighteen, and one will remain under continuous monitoring and receive psychiatric care, according to the F.B.I.)

Mass murderers have long attracted macabre groupies, but, like many ugly human tendencies, the phenomenon has been accelerated and amplified by the internet. According to law enforcement, the Texas girls were participants in the T.C.C., short for True Crime Community, an online fandom that celebrates perpetrators of mass violence. In the past two years, at least nine attacks and sixteen disrupted plots have been linked to the T.C.C., according to the Institute for Strategic Dialogue, a nonprofit think tank with a focus on hate and extremism.

In the aftermath of the arrests, law enforcement announced that the girls’ plot had been discovered through their online activity. “Everyone was, like, ‘Oh, so the F.B.I. really is spying on us?’ And, no, it’s not the F.B.I.,” Baimbridge said. The initial tip had come from a little-known team inside Meta that has spent the past decade honing a method to identify potential school shooters. Algorithmic social media, which has helped create the conditions for the spread of online content that might inspire such shootings, is reckoning with how it can become part of the solution.

Before Emily Vacher was recruited by Meta, in 2011, she was an F.B.I. agent specializing in crimes against children. At Meta, Vacher began working with the law-enforcement-outreach team, which she now helps lead. In the immediate aftermath of high-profile incidents, such as the Sandy Hook school shooting, in Newtown, Connecticut, the team would make contact with law enforcement to offer assistance. “You know, are there any co-conspirators? Is there any other impending danger they need to know about?” Vacher said.

As an agent, Vacher had spent time working with the behavioral-analysis unit in Quantico, the heavily mythologized home of the Bureau’s criminal profilers. There, she studied the work of Mary Ellen O’Toole, a longtime profiler with a specialty in school shooters. O’Toole had come to realize that many people who commit mass violence telegraph their intentions in advance, a phenomenon she referred to as “leakage.” School shooters, it turned out, were particularly prone to it: an F.B.I. study of active-shooter attacks between 2000 and 2013 found that nearly nine out of ten shooters under the age of eighteen had leaked their intent.

If would-be perpetrators were dropping hints about imminent violence online, Vacher thought, it should be possible to detect and stop them—“to think about disrupting attacks, not just investigating them,” she said. In 2018, she helped assemble a group of people with expertise in school shootings, including former journalists and police investigators, to study the online footprints of past attackers and to identify behavioral patterns. They worked with a team of engineers to build systems that use A.I. tools in order to scan for signals. Content flagged by the automated systems is passed along to a human for further review of the user’s online behavior: the posts they make and share, their interactions with others, the prompts they feed into A.I. chatbots. When there is evidence of “substantial steps, overt acts,” as Vacher put it—a list of targets or a manifesto, say—the investigations team provides relevant information to law enforcement. Last year, Meta did so around eight hundred times worldwide.

The process can unfold rapidly. According to prosecutors, last fall, a student at Benjamin N. Cardozo High School, in Queens, posted a photograph on Instagram of schoolwork with a text overlay that read “boutta shoot the school up.” The post was flagged by Meta, which passed the user’s information along to the F.B.I., which in turn notified the N.Y.P.D. After law enforcement matched the I.P. address associated with the post to a phone number and a name, they searched the student’s backpack and found a loaded pistol. Just over two hours after posting the picture, the student was taken into custody. (The student was charged with gun possession and making a terroristic threat.)

But few cases are as straightforward as that one. Publicly announcing an attack in advance is “part of the whole lore in this community,” Vacher said, “but they can’t be so overt that they just immediately get caught.” (Meta’s community standards ban threats of violence, in addition to content that “glorifies, supports or represents . . . multiple-victim violence.”) Young people, adept at evading content bans, know how to speak in a densely coded language that often outpaces moderation tools built to target more explicit forms of radicalization. “The swastika is easy to understand,” Katherine Keneally, the senior director of threat analysis and prevention at the Institute for Strategic Dialogue U.S., said. “The Atomwaffen Division logo, that’s obvious. But if there’s, like, an A.I.-generated version of a person who committed a shooting in the Philippines doing a TikTok dance with a number behind them that signifies the number of people killed in the attack—that’s a lot more difficult to understand.” Keneally said that many social-media companies have downsized their trust-and-safety teams in recent years, allowing more T.C.C. posts to slip through the cracks.

Instead of using perpetrators’ names, T.C.C. posters may invoke Arlene (Eric Harris’s nickname for the shotgun he used in the Columbine attack) or Smiggles (a screen name used by Adam Lanza, the Sandy Hook shooter). When people began to catch on that Meta’s moderation tools were flagging profiles that used widely known images of shooters, they began using childhood photos instead. “You have to have someone who can recognize that, O.K., this is not the normal Adam Lanza picture we’re used to seeing—this is Adam Lanza much younger,” a member of Meta’s investigations team told me. As with any fan culture, the lore and language are self-referential and constantly evolving. “Any time there is an actualized attack, the imagery, the iconography, anything associated with the attacker—the music they listened to, the clothes they wore, the specific weapons they used, the things they wrote—tends to get recycled back into the canon,” the team member said.

The details of past attacks become a template for future violence: in the moments before a fifteen-year-old girl in Wisconsin killed a classmate, a teacher, and herself, in December, 2024, she posted a photo of someone in a bathroom stall, flashing a hand signal associated with white supremacy. A month later, a seventeen-year-old boy in Tennessee posted a similar image before killing a classmate and himself. An act of violence doesn’t need to be high-profile to make an impact. An incel-motivated stabbing at a school in Mexico City last year barely made the news in the U.S., yet it quickly became absorbed into the T.C.C. symbology.

The vast majority of young people who post in T.C.C. communities won’t commit violence, of course. In 2012, I wrote about the Columbiners, a kind of precursor to the T.C.C. The Columbiners congregated on Tumblr, where they posted swoony, heart-covered memes about Harris and Klebold. When I spoke with Columbiners, I was more worried about them than about anything they might do; their memes struck me as a clumsy and self-consciously edgy way of expressing their rage and pain and feelings of alienation. “The experience of engaging with TCC narratives is often deeply emotional for online users,” a report from the I.S.D. notes. One worrying sign is when someone moves from romanticizing or idolizing school shooters to identifying with them, according to Vacher: “They’re not just saying, ‘Oh, this guy’s amazing.’ They’re buying the clothes that that shooter wore, or they’re finding the gun that the shooter used, or they’re getting floor plans for the school, or buying tactical gear.”

Social-media companies must weigh competing claims of privacy, liability, and caution when deciding what to report to law enforcement. Overreporting runs the risk of shunting troubled kids into the criminal-justice system merely for expressing morbid thoughts. But the consequences of underreporting can be dire. Last June, the automated-review system at OpenAI flagged an account used by Jesse Van Rootselaar, a teen-ager whose conversations with ChatGPT featured repeated discussions of gun violence, including mass-shooting scenarios. A team reviewed Van Rootselaar’s activity but decided against reporting it to law enforcement, evidently concluding that it did not constitute an immediate or credible threat. OpenAI shut down the account, but Van Rootselaar soon created another one, on which the discussions continued, albeit in a slightly more coded way: the planned shooting was hypothetical, Van Rootselaar now said. The chatbot gave advice about how not to get flagged again. “You can still be twisted. Just be clever about it,” it advised. During the following eight months, Van Rootselaar “discussed building and using firearms and homemade explosives, fantasized about becoming a notorious mass killer, and asked about the timing of previous real-world school shootings,” according to reporting by Mother Jones. In February, after killing her mother and brother, Van Rootselaar opened fire at Tumbler Ridge Secondary School, in British Columbia, killing six people, and then herself. The province and multiple victims’ families have filed lawsuits against OpenAI. “I am deeply sorry that we did not alert law enforcement,” Sam Altman, the C.E.O., wrote in an open letter.

Meta’s investigations team is just “one small piece” of the work of preventing school shootings, Vacher told me. “This is very much a societal problem. We need schools and parents and mental-health professionals and teachers and law enforcement.” But, after speaking with members of the company’s team—sharp, sincere people doing potentially lifesaving work—I couldn’t help feeling that a kind of cognitive dissonance underlay the whole project. “It’s all well and good to report specific cases to law enforcement, but at the end of the day the platforms are also playing a significant role in the spread of this content that is radicalizing people to engage in violence,” Keneally, of the I.S.D., said—namely, by creating algorithmic feeds that amplify extreme content while also decreasing funding for trust-and-safety programs. (TikTok, she noted, is the mainstream platform on which T.C.C. content seems to spread the fastest and farthest. When reached for comment, a TikTok U.S. spokesperson said that the platform removes content that violates its community guidelines, permanently bans accounts dedicated to glorifying attackers, and takes action against additional accounts that may be operated by the banned user.)

The more time I spent in this world, with its strange mixture of coy menace and clout-driven nihilism, the more I thought about Columbine. Twenty-seven years later, the attack on a school in the Denver suburbs retains an archetypal mystique for generations of troubled young people. Harris and Klebold, early to catch on to the self-mythologizing possibilities of new technology, obsessively documented themselves and their fascination with violence, both online and off. It’s as if they were native speakers of a language that hadn’t quite been invented yet. ♦

#454. Brazil election 2026: Full second round results as Lula wins comeback against Flávio Bolsonaro

After a shock defeat in the first round, the incumbent Brazilian president, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, has turned the election around to win the runoff.

Brazil’s president is elected purely by the popular vote. A winning candidate needs one more vote than 50% of votes cast; there is no role for the parliament and no electoral college.

Because no candidate achieved 50% in the first round on 4 October, the two leading candidates go into a runoff. One of them is Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, the incumbent leftwing president, commonly known as Lula. The other, and the leading candidate in the first round, is Flávio Bolsonaro, the son of the far-right former president Jair Bolsonaro.

Bolsonaro senior is serving a 27-year prison sentence for plotting a failed coup attempt after he lost to Lula in the 2022 election, but has endorsed his son and hopes to be released if he wins. Bolsonaro junior is a lawyer and former senator from Rio.

In 2022, Lula beat Jair Bolsonaro in the second round, with an electoral base concentrated in the north-east of the country, while Bolsonaro led in the south. But on that occasion Lula entered the second round as the leading candidate from the first round, and won the bellwether state of Minas Gerais, north of Rio de Janeiro. In the first round of the 2026 election, Bolsonaro beat Lula in Minas Gerais by 48.43% to 43.33%.

Never, since the return of democracy to Brazil in 1985, has the first round runner-up managed to turn the game around and win the second vote. But Lula’s campaign has been buoyed by massive, student-led anti-Bolsonaro protests after the first round and hopes to stage a historic fightback on Sunday.

Lula is already the longest-serving president in Brazil’s modern history, serving two terms from 2003 to 2010, before returning to office in 2023. He will turn 81 on Tuesday. Bolsonaro is 45.

#456. Gadi Eisenkot campaigns on Moroccan roots in challenge to Netanyahu

As Israel prepares to vote, Gadi Eisenkot is leveraging his Moroccan heritage in Likud strongholds such as Sderot. From historical divides to October 7, identity and trauma are redefining the city's political loyalties.

Ahead of Tuesday's elections, party advertisements greet people arriving by train to the outskirts of the western Israeli city of Sderot.

Campaign posters with various slogans promote the right-wing Likud and the far-right Otzma Yehudit parties. However, one poster is seen more than any others: that of the liberal Yashar party.

Its slogan, "Rosh Memshala Dialna," combines the terms "prime minister" in Hebrew and the words "of our own" in Moroccan Arabic.

And there is a good reason why Eisenkot's campaign in Sderot and other cities on Israel's sociopolitical periphery focuses on his Moroccan roots.

Sderot — located in the Negev desert, about 75 kilometers (45 miles) to Tel Aviv's south and just a kilometer away from the Gaza border — is a city with a large Moroccan Jewish community. In recent decades, the city has become symbolic of Moroccan-Jewish culture in Israel, finding its expression in music, cinema and state politics.

Historically, Sderot is considered a bastion of the Israeli right, and especially a stronghold of Prime MinisterBenjamin Netanyahu's Likud. In 2022, Likud received more than 40% of the votes in Sderot, compared with 23% nationwide. Other members of Israel's far-right coalition received a lot of support there in the last election.

This pattern reflects deeper historical divisions in Israel between Ashkenazim, or Jews of European origin, and Mizrahim, or Jews mostly from Middle Eastern and North African backgrounds. According to estimates, half of Israel's Jewish population is of Mizrahi origin, amounting to about 4 million people.

Mizrahi Jews have faced discrimination and neglect since the early days of the Israeli state. This inequality still shapes the economic and social reality in Israel ― and its political discourse.

The issue has received special attention in the 2026 campaign. Eisenkot, Netanyahu's major challenger, is of Moroccan origin and grew up in Eilat, a city with a large North African Jewish population. Eisenkot's Yashar party accused Likud of sidelining its Mizrahi politicians. The campaign slogan, "Netanyahu: Let the Mizrahim give interviews,” mocked that omission.

On live television, the right-wing journalist Yinon Magal — one of Netanyahu's most vocal supporters — claimed that Eisenkot was being controlled by elitist Ashkenazim and called him a "monkey."

Netanyahu, who is of European origin, condemned the remark.

Morocco-born Daniel Ben-Simon, an award-winning Israeli journalist and a former member of parliament, told DW that Eisenkot's embrace of his roots might have been forced upon him by Magal's comments — but it has historical value nevertheless.

"People don't use terms like 'Dialna' with pride," Ben-Simon said, referring to Eisenkot's embrace of the Arabic term "of our own" in campaign posters. He said Israeli society had long tried to erase the Arab identities of North African Jews.

Eisenkot's embrace of his identity serves as a way of "leaving the frame" set by society. "By using such a term, you're creating a new identity: an Israeli-Moroccan identity," Ben-Simon said. "Eisenkot is the first to allow this double identity to be expressed and still feel at home."

After Israel's founding, the ruling socialist party, Mapai, whose leadership was largely Ashkenazi, pursued discriminatory policies against Mizrahim. Mizrahim were often expected to "Europeanize” rather than preserve their own culture, heritage, or Arabic language.

#463. Alaska’s Senate Race Could Be Decided by Salmon

Perhaps the most important thing to know about Alaska’s Senate race, a close contest between the Republican incumbent, Dan Sullivan, and the former congresswoman Mary Peltola, is that salmon are pretty amazing fish. There’s the swimming upstream, the spawning, and the dying. Bears love salmon. Orcas love salmon. People love salmon. Without salmon, the first Alaskans, crossing the Bering land bridge some fifteen thousand years ago, could not have survived. On a recent trip to Alaska, I was, at various points, fed salmon for dinner, offered salmon as a gift, and given salmon for barter. Directly or indirectly, salmon feeds almost everything in the state, including its politics. Alaska’s bid for statehood, in the late nineteen-fifties, was in part a matter of keeping the hands of Seattle businessmen off local salmon stocks. Business deals are hammered out at fishing retreats. In 2010, a seaplane carrying Ted Stevens, likely the most powerful politician ever to come out of Alaska, crashed en route to a salmon lodge near Bristol Bay. He died wearing his waders.

People in the Lower Forty-eight care a lot about Alaska’s Senate race. This cycle, it has been twice as expensive, per capita, as Michigan’s, and eight times Texas’s. The attention is unusual. In federal elections, Alaska reliably votes Republican; every time Donald Trump has been on the ballot, Alaska has voted for him by ten points or more. But, amid historically low approval ratings for the President, the Democrats are hoping to flip the seat, an essential part of any attempt to wrest back control of both houses of Congress. Trump, meanwhile, is clearly nervous about holding the seat. He recently headlined a rally for Sullivan in Wasilla, a small city north of Anchorage. “This is worth the eight hours on a plane,” Trump said. “And don’t forget, I got eight hours going back, too.”

In January, Chuck Schumer recruited Peltola to run, calling her the “last piece to the puzzle” of his midterms plan. Peltola, the state’s former at-large House member, launched her campaign with the slogan “Fish, Family, Freedom.” To most outsiders, this read as folksy branding for a race that would inevitably be dominated by Donald Trump. But Peltola really wants to talk about fish. Recently, I saw her speak at the elementary school in Utqiagvik, an Arctic town at the state’s northern tip. It was a very unusual stump speech. She apologized for the “super negative, toxic ads” flooding the state, including her own. She mentioned her opponent just once: a flattering anecdote about his advocacy for an oil project she supports. She didn’t mention Trump at all. And even though, compared with the rest of the state, salmon is not so big in Utqiagvik, Peltola still mostly talked about fish.

This piscine primacy is relatively new in Alaska’s politics. In past election cycles, fish have always been one issue among many. But Alaska’s fish are in crisis. In recent years, runs of Chinook, or king salmon, the biggest and most revered species on the Yukon River, have been down as much as eighty per cent from average levels, according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Chum salmon, a more ordinary variety, have had an O.K. year, but they’ve been down as much as ninety per cent. Some species and some areas are worse off than others, but the general trend is bad, including for the state’s other two iconic seafood catches, halibut and crab. As I travelled around the state, I was surprised to find that virtually everyone I talked to was upset about the fish situation. One political consultant explained, “A lot of people come into the state, and they’re, like, ‘Why the hell is Mary Peltola “fish, family, freedom”?’ I’m a white guy who lives in Anchorage, and my freezer is currently full of rockfish, halibut, and salmon that I’ve caught because that’s just what we all do.”

The crisis is worst up rivers such as the Yukon and the Kuskokwim. For many thousands of years, entire Athabascan and Yup’ik villages have subsisted on salmon. Indigenous residents have been delivering emotional testimony at meetings of the North Pacific Fishery Management Council (N.P.F.M.C.), which was created by Congress to oversee the region’s federal fisheries. “I got nine grandkids. My two youngest ones haven’t even tasted fish,” Robert Albert, a local from Tanana, said at a meeting in February. Life and social structures in remote river villages are rooted in summer fish camps, where the community catches, processes, and preserves the salmon that feeds them through the winter. But stocks have got so low that, in 2024, the Alaska government, in conjunction with Canada, shut down fishing for king salmon on the Yukon River and its Canadian tributaries for seven years—one complete king-salmon life cycle. The fish camps stand empty. “I wish I could express to you what it feels like for a man who provided for multiple families to become a beggar,” Carrie Stevens, who testified to the N.P.F.M.C. as a representative of the Council of Athabascan Tribal Governments, told me. Janessa Newman, from Rampart, told the council, “My immediate concern is whether I can morally bring my own children into this world without fishing on the Yukon River.”

Research scientists with the N.P.F.M.C. and N.O.A.A. attribute the die-off, primarily, to climate change. But activists in Alaska have mostly focussed their ire on a type of fishing known as trawling. In the Bering Sea and the Gulf of Alaska, the main target of commercial fishing boats is pollock, a medium-sized, mild fish that swims in fantastically vast schools. Trawlers catch pollock by dragging enormous nets; some open wide enough to swallow a thousand M.T.A. buses. Any net you put in the water will incidentally catch other, non-targeted fish. This is known as bycatch. Every year, the Alaskan pollock industry brings up around twenty thousand king salmon and around a quarter million pounds of halibut as bycatch. Recently, anger at this collateral damage, heightened by the fish shortage, has evolved into a political movement.

On Facebook, the group Stop Alaskan Trawler Bycatch, launched in early 2020, has close to sixty thousand members, almost a tenth the population of Alaska itself. It calls for a ban on trawling. “You know how ‘Tiger King’ came out during lockdown and got big? The Facebook group was the same thing,” David Bayes, who moderates it, told me. Peltola, who is Yup’ik and grew up along the Kuskokwim, joined the group in its early days. At the time, she was the head of the Kuskokwim River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission, a nonprofit that co-manages fishing resources with the government. Two years later, in a special election in 2022, she emerged out of a crowded top-four primary to defeat Sarah Palin, becoming the first Democrat since 1970 to win Alaska’s sole House seat. That campaign’s defining issue was trawl bycatch. This year, she is campaigning on a call to ban out-of-state factory trawlers. This anti-trawl sentiment has caught on, politically, around the state. It has become a marquee issue in races for governor, the state legislature, and even in municipal elections. “There was one guy running for school board talking about trawl,” Bayes told me.

I’d assumed that pollock people—businesspeople—would lean Republican, and the salmon people—the conservation-oriented—would lean Democratic, but that’s not really the case. “There’s nothing partisan about it,” Peltola told me recently. “When you ask folks who are MAGA why they’re splitting the ticket and voting for me, it’s largely because of trawling.” (The campaign later pointed out that a lot of those voters are also animated by high fuel prices.) Politics in Alaska are idiosyncratic. They rarely map onto a national partisan model. Republicans outnumber Democrats two to one, but independent voters outnumber both. “Partisan politics doesn’t work that well for a candidate,” Tony Knowles, the last Democrat to serve as governor, told me. A government staffer in a remote region put it to me, “Locally, no one gives a shit about the parties.”

One oddity of Alaska politics is that, though only twelve per cent of voters identify as Democrats, both the State Senate and the State House of Representatives are currently controlled by coalitions made up mostly of Democrats and independents, with some Republicans. Coalition governments are not uncommon in the state, where politicians are fond of saying that pragmatism trumps partisanship. Sometimes it’s more about vibes. “Somebody is a magnificent pain in the ass, and we don’t want them in our caucus so we build a caucus to exclude them,” Joelle Hall, a longtime union leader, told me. Imagine a congressional coalition formed by kicking out the lawmakers, on either side, who are annoying—the Ted Cruzes and the John Fettermans. Peltola tried to explain this unique approach by telling me that the current coalition “took the hardest people to work with and just put them to the side, and then everybody else joined as a majority to move forward and solve problems. And that is a model I am hoping we can export to every state in the nation.”

At one debate during that race, they wore matching earrings; Peltola had given them to Palin as a gift. (“It’s an Alaska-chick thing,” Palin said.) Peltola is even closer with Lisa Murkowski, Alaska’s other incumbent senator, a Republican who, despite supporting Sullivan in this race, had endorsed Peltola during her previous campaigns. After Peltola won her first election, Murkowski offered to let her stay at her place in Washington. “That’s the thing about Alaska: there’s only seven hundred thirty-five thousand of us,” Peltola told me. “We’re all stuck with each other until we die.”

“Alaskans consider themselves more Alaskan than Republican, and more Alaskan, quite honestly, than they are American,” Scott Kendall, an attorney and longtime G.O.P. political consultant in the state, told me. “We like each other. In June, back in D.C., he introduced a trawling bill that offered wonkish, incremental changes to fishing practices in Alaska. The bill never made it off the Senate floor, but it did give him something to run on. In Anchorage, Arthur Hackney, who runs a Sullivan-aligned PAC called We Stand with Dan, showed me an ad he’d recently cut. On the screen, the text read “This Election is About Fish, Family, Freedom,” Peltola’s exact campaign motto. Then it said, “On all of these, Senator Dan Sullivan actually delivers results.” Hackney explained, “People don’t understand how easy it is to just take the other person’s thing and you claim it for your own.” A strategist working with the Democrats told me it was surreal to see Sullivan trying to co-opt fishing. “He’s a marine, good-looking guy. Alaska’s core constituency is composed of Natives, fishermen, rural legislators, and unions, with some chamber-of-commerce types mixed in. Peltola is a gun-owning, fish-catching Alaska-bush native, who has worked for mines and supports oil drilling, and is allied with the state’s largest labor unions. “You’ve got a once-in-a-generation candidate who is all the pieces of that coalition as one person,” the strategist told me. “You worry that the federal PACs will come in and fuck all that up. Trawling is a wedge issue in Kodiak. Many people rely on trawling, directly or indirectly, for work, but small seiners, long-liners, and crab fishermen have major issues with it. #466. Prague honors Roma resistance fighters against Hitler

More than 1,400 Roma fought against the German Nazi occupation in Czechoslovakia. A new exhibition in Prague showcases the stories of partisans and soldiers whose fates barely received any attention for decades.

For more than half a million Roma in Czechia and Slovakia, October 15, 2026, marks an important milestone.

That day, an exhibition on a largely forgotten chapter of Czech history opened at the Museum of the Romani National Revival in Prague. The exhibit explores the role of Czech and Slovak Romani resistance fighters during the Nazi German occupation and the dismantling of Czechoslovakia from 1938 to 1945.

Until now, Czech and Slovak Roma appeared in the history of World War II almost exclusively as victims of the Holocaust against Roma and Sinti. In the so-called Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia, Nazi Germany murdered approximately 90% of the Czech Roma during World War II.

However, what is less well known is that a large number of Czech and Slovak Roma fought against the German occupiers as partisans in resistance groups and as soldiers in the Czechoslovak Underground Army, or Defense of the Nation, ON.

The exhibition at the Museum of the Roma National Movement therefore marks a turning point in how the Roma are viewed during World War II. At least that is the hope of museum director Emil Scuka, a lawyer, former prosecutor and one of the best-known Czech Roma activists.

"We were not only victims; we were also heroes," Scuka said in his speech to several hundred attendees at the exhibition opening, most of whom were Roma from Czechia and Slovakia. "Many people, not even among the Roma themselves, are aware that we Roma fought against Nazism," he noted. In his view, "organizing and opening the exhibition was something my generation owed to these Roma heroes and today we are repaying that debt."

Scuka called on those present: "Bring your children and grandchildren here so they can see for themselves and realize that the Roma have heroes too!"

He also pointed out that this topic had been completely marginalized in Czech and Slovak historical writing for decades and that the Roma had therefore decided on their own to document the fates of their war heroes.

The exhibition presents the detailed life stories of 100 of the most significant Roma partisans and Roma soldiers from the Czech underground army and resistance groups who fought against the Nazis.

"We found over 1,400 Romani fighters in the archives. From among them, we selected the 100 most interesting stories for this exhibition," said exhibition curator Radoslav Scuka at the opening.

Among these figures is Anton Facuna, a sergeant in the American intelligence agency Office of Strategic Services (OSS), who was flown to Slovakia in October 1944 on a secret mission. Slovakia had been a puppet state of Hitler from 1939 to 1945.

Another is Josef Serinek, known by the nickname "Black Partisan from Vysocina," who managed to escape from the Nazi concentration camp for Roma in Lety and subsequently founded and commanded the large Capajev-Cerny partisan unit in the Bohemian-Moravian Highlands.

In 2022, Czech President Milos Zeman posthumously awarded him the Medal for Heroism. Toward the end of his life, Serinek shared his memories with historian Jan Tesar, who later wrote the three-volume book "Ceska cikanska rapsodie", which translates to Czech Gypsy Rhapsody.

Except for Tesar's book, the history of Roma resistance against Nazism has not been researched by Czech and Slovak historians to this day. "I am not aware of any scholarly historical work that deals with Roma resistance against Nazism," Prokop Tomek, a historian at the Military History Institute in Prague, told DW.

"The number of 1,400 Roma who took part in the fight against Nazism as members of the resistance or in the foreign army does not strike me as an exaggeration and corresponds to the proportion of the Roma population in Czechoslovakia," Tomek said, adding that the "exhibition on the Roma resistance against Nazism during the war is undoubtedly groundbreaking."

To this day, many Roma in Czechia and Slovakia do not publicly acknowledge their ethnic identity. This is partly due to experiences with the Holocaust during the Nazi era that have been passed down through generations. Czech historians and politicians continue to debate the extent to which the Czech authorities of the Protectorate were complicit in the extermination of the Czech Roma.

However, one thing is certain: the police registration of all Roma, which had been enshrined in law in Czechoslovakia since 1927, facilitated the extermination of the Roma by the German Nazis.

According to estimates by the Czech government, approximately 250,000 Roma live in the country today. In the 2021 census, however, only 21,000 people identified as Roma.

Of all ethnic groups in Czechia, which has a population of 10 million, the Roma are among the three largest ethnic minorities in terms of numbers. However, they are the most socially disadvantaged, have very little political representation, and are therefore scarcely present in Czech politics.

Agencies would have to spell out application requirements up-front and could not make repeated requests for more information or project changes. Environmental impacts would be judged under the laws and standards in place when an application is submitted. Applicants could propose a single project alternative for review, rather than requiring agencies to develop and evaluate several.

The measure caps public comment periods, while current law only sets minimum lengths. It also limits what courts may consider in CEQA lawsuits and, if a court finds that an agency violated the law, it could halt only the affected portion of a project while the issue is fixed.

The state Legislature can amend the measure only with a two-thirds supermajority vote, and any changes would need to further the initiative.

#500. Kim Jong-un crosses newly opened bridge into China

North Korean leader travels over New Yalu River Bridge to meet senior Communist party member

Kim Jong-un has visited China, inaugurating a bridge that connects the two countries and saying he hopes to expand cooperation with his neighbour.

The North Korean leader arrived in the Chinese border city of Dandong on Saturday after crossing the New Yalu River Bridge, according to South Korea’s unification ministry, which handles affairs related to North Korea, and China’s official Xinhua news agency.

He met Wang Huning, a member of the standing committee of China’s Communist party. The pair toured a car factory, and promised to strengthen the bilateral relationship.

Kim said his country “stands ready to work with China to carry forward their historical friendship and expand practical cooperation, thereby delivering tangible benefits to the peoples of both countries”, according to Xinhua.

Wang then accompanied Kim to the bridge to send him off, Xinhua said.

The visit appears to be timed to underscore the strength of Kim’s ties with Beijing ahead of the 25 October anniversary of China’s first major victory in the Korean war, which came days after Chinese troops crossed the Yalu River into North Korea in October 1950.

Japan’s Kyodo news agency said on Saturday that traffic controls had been imposed around the bridge and near a museum about China’s role in the war, and that hotel bookings nearby had been restricted.

Diplomatic sources told the agency the measures were linked to a visit by a senior North Korean official.

The 3km bridge opened in mid-October, 12 years after China finished building it, after being held up by delays on the North Korean side. In early September, North Korea and Russia opened the first road bridge across their own short border, leaving Pyongyang newly linked by road to its main backers.

The trip is Kim’s sixth visit to China and his first since September 2025, when he attended a military parade in Beijing alongside the Chinese president, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin. Xi visited Pyongyang in June, his first trip there in seven years.

Relations with South Korea, meanwhile, have deteriorated. Kim’s sister, Kim Yo Jong, has issued about 10 statements criticising Seoul since late September, a frequency South Korea’s unification ministry has called unusual, and has also announced plans to resume loudspeaker broadcasts at the border within days.

Xi will host the Asia Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen on 18-19 November, which has fuelled speculation about a meeting between Kim and Donald Trump.

Reuters reported on Friday, however, that US and South Korean officials see little chance of Kim meeting the US president this year.
