#551. Demand for UK government action 10 months after British man’s death in Grenada

Police closed case of Andrew Frederick despite autopsy report ruling his death a homicide

A time-critical investigation into the death of a British father whose body was found in a ransacked property on the Caribbean island of Grenada has stalled because of diplomatic delays between the two countries.

Charity workers supporting the family of Andrew Frederick are demanding immediate government intervention to clear the path for a full investigation to begin almost 10 months after his death, which has been ruled a homicide by a pathologist.

The Murdered Abroad charity has called on the foreign affairs select committee to step in after Grenadian police found there was “no basis … that any homicide has occurred” and closed the case.

This was despite a report from an autopsy, done in the week he died, saying Frederick, 47, had suffered “blunt force trauma”, was tortured, including by removal of his toenail, and ruling the death a homicide.

Frederick’s body was found on 4 January. Since then, the family say they have been forced to focus on their fight for justice instead of coming to terms with their loss.

Murdered Abroad’s founder, Eve Henderson, said: “We understand there are challenges but [almost 10 months] on, this family is still being met with obfuscation. If a family this determined cannot get the FCDO [Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office] to put their diplomatic powers to good use, what hope is there for the 60 to 80 families bereaved by murder abroad each year?

“A minister must look urgently at how consular services treat these traumatised families or the foreign affairs select committee must step in,” she added.

A former Scotland Yard homicide commander who is supporting the family described Grenada’s actions as “incredible”.

“In the autopsy report … the pathologist, who is a qualified medical examiner, categorically states that he believes this is a case of homicide and that Andrew was tortured before he was killed,” David Johnston said. “And yet the police just choose to ignore that because it didn’t fit with their model. I mean, it’s incredible.”

After a sustained campaign by the family, British authorities became involved and Frederick’s body was repatriated to the UK in August, where a second autopsy was carried out, during which it was discovered his organs were missing.

The family described this discovery as “shocking and devastating”.

In a statement, they said: “We were already heartbroken that Andrew’s life was cruelly taken, but nothing could have prepared us for what we would learn afterwards. For six weeks after his death, Andrew’s body was held under the control of the Grenadian authorities. When he was later examined in the UK, parts of his neck and all of his organs, including his brain, heart, and lungs, were missing.

“That discovery has been shocking and absolutely devastating for our family; we are still struggling to process it. It has added pain upon pain and left us with deep hurt with so many unanswered questions. Despite our multiple inquiries, no one has told us where they are, what happened to them, or why we were never told. No family should ever have to ask these questions.”

Last month a UK coroner asked the Metropolitan police to conduct an investigation. Weeks later a communication from UK authorities, seen by the Guardian, said a British high commissioner had raised Frederick’s case with officials at the highest levels of government in Grenada, and indicated that authorities there had agreed to cooperate with any forthcoming formal request for the UK to assist with the investigation.

That formal request was sent weeks ago but the family say they are still waiting for Grenada “to honour their verbal commitment to the British high commissioner and approve the request to cooperate”.

“Every day for almost 10 months we have had to live without Andrew and without answers. A formal request from the UK, asking Grenada to cooperate with its investigation into Andrew’s death, still sits unsigned. It is the best chance we have of finding the truth,” the family statement said.

Johnston said the initial handling of Frederick’s case and the further delays could have a detrimental effect on the investigation.

“In a homicide investigation we have a phrase that we refer to as the golden hours, which are the first real 24 hours from the notification, or the discovery, of a homicide [which] are the most important and crucial … because evidence diminishes and can be lost if a crime scene is not properly noted and properly managed.

“In the case of the death of [Frederick] that’s exactly what happened … the police, in my view, failed to properly identify and preserve the crime scene and to treat the crime scene in the manner it should have been treated,” he said.

Johnston added that cooperation between British and Grenadian police could help to “bring some stability to that investigation”.

An FCDO spokesperson said: “We are supporting the family of a British man who died in Grenada, and are in contact with the local authorities.”

The Guardian has approached the Royal Grenada Police Force for comment but has not had a reply.

The family said: “With each passing day, our family’s heartbreak deepens. We need closure, and Grenada’s prime minister, who is also the minister of national security, can help make this possible. We appeal to him, with humanity and compassion, to sign the request without further delay.”

A covert recording captured Beau Lamarre-Condon telling a friend during a meeting in prison in December last year, “I fully take accountability that you’re involved because of me now,” a Sydney court has heard.

Lamarre-Condon, 31, is accused of fatally shooting couple Jesse Baird and Luke Davies with his police service weapon in Baird’s Paddington home on 19 February 2024.

He has pleaded not guilty to two counts of murder and one charge of aggravated break and enter.

Lamarre-Condon’s friend, whose identity is suppressed, told the New South Wales supreme court on Friday that Lamarre-Condon allegedly confessed to killing the two men, and told his friend it was an “out-of-body experience”, before then attempting to play it off as a joke.

On Monday, the friend was back in the witness stand, while a recording was played to the court of a conversation the friend recorded on behalf of police when he visited Lamarre-Condon in prison. At the beginning of the conversation, Lamarre-Condon asked the friend to turn around and show he was not wearing a wire.

“You’re so paranoid,” the friend said to Lamarre-Condon in the recording played to the jury. “Calm down.”

The friend said to Lamarre-Condon during the prison visit: “You put me in a scenario that’s very difficult for me.”

Lamarre-Condon apologised, stating, “I fully take accountability that you’re involved because of me now,” the court heard on Monday.

The friend, in the recording, said he was upset because Lamarre-Condon had said he was dumb. In response, Lamarre-Condon said “You’re not dumb OK,” before then telling his friend: “You’re not self aware in the way that I am.”

Lamarre-Condon accused his friend during the conversation of lying “about so much shit” in his statement to police. He said his friend was wrong to recollect that Lamarre-Condon had said Davies was killed to eliminate a witness and in fact he’d acted in self-defence.

“Don’t you dare switch up,” the friend responded during the prison visit.

Lamarre-Condon in the recording told his friend his entry into Baird’s home was not break and enter because Baird had got a key cut for him. He admitted that entering the Paddington terrace was trespass but claimed it was normal to return to a former partner’s home to retrieve belongings. He said he did not knock on the door to get the belongings back because he didn’t want to be slapped with an apprehended violence order, but he did call out hello when he walked inside.

Lamarre-Condon, in the recording played to the jury, told his friend: “If I had planned to do this mate, I’d be cool, calm, collected.”

He said he would have had “cleaning ready to go” and he would not have used a police firearm.

“I can get [another gun] for $10,000, I could have done all these things,” he said in the recording played to court.

Lamarre-Condon told the friend he took him to the Royal national park before the murders in early 2024 and shot his gun to test how loud the gunshots were because he was planning to return there to take his own life.

He told the friend during the recording that he had been suffering from suicidality from trauma at work.

During the phone call, the friend can be heard threatening to leave multiple times.

“What you’re telling me mightn’t be the truth,” the friend said.

During cross-examination, Lamarre-Condon’s lawyer, John Stratton SC, suggested it was the friend’s idea to sink Bair and Davies’ bodies in a dam.

“I’m 100% sure it wasn’t me,” the friend told the court on Monday.

He said he warned Lamarre-Condon the bodies would float. “I gave him a scientific fact that he [could] search up on Google in his own time,” the witness said.

The friend admitted he told Lamarre-Condon – after the pair had discussed if the bodies should be taken to a national park – that bushland was nearby in the Royal national park.

The friend denied Stratton’s suggestion that he had given false evidence and was influenced by media reporting.

#553. Turkey's new deal on Mediterranean maritime boundaries with Libya is exacerbating issues with Greece and the EU

Turkey and Libya plan to redraw maritime boundaries in the Mediterranean as part of a new economic corridor. This has sparked strong protests from Greece, and has also drawn the European Union into the conflict.

On Tuesday, the parliament of Libya unanimously approved a maritime agreement with Turkey to establish a direct maritime economic corridor between the countries.

Turkey had agreed on the corridor with the UN-recognized Government of National Accord in western Libya in 2019. However, the agreement faced opposition from the rival Libyan government in the country's east. Now, though, the eastern parliament has reversed its previous opposition.

The decision, which was welcomed by Turkey's Foreign Ministry, is likely to contribute to tensions with other countries in the eastern Mediterranean — especially Greece .

The agreement establishes a direct maritime corridor between the southern coast of Turkey and the northern coast of Libya. Large oil and gas deposits have recently been discovered in the region in question — estimates suggest that natural gas reserves worth several hundred billion US dollars may lie beneath the seabed. Turkey and Libya aim to secure their economic rights to these raw materials and are seeking to jointly exploit the resources.

The planned corridor disregards Greek islands such as Crete, Karpathos and Rhodes. Greece's government insists that it possesses a comprehensive exclusive economic zone, or EEZ, around these islands and has already announced its opposition to the accord.

In a post on X, the Foreign Ministry announced that Greece firmly rejects the agreement and has the support of the European Union in doing so. "The European Union has described the Turkish-Libyan memorandum as null and void, stressing that it violates the sovereign rights of third states and is not consistent with the Law of the Sea," the ministry posted.

Similar wording came straight from Brussels. "I raised yesterday's Libyan vote on a maritime MoU with Türkiye and stressed that it infringes upon the sovereign rights of third states," Kaja Kallas, the European Union high representative for foreign affairs and security policy, posted on X.

Whose claims are legitimate in the eastern Mediterranean is the central point of contention. Greece primarily cites the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, or UNCLOS. According to Article 121 of the convention, inhabited islands that "support an economic life of their own” have the same rights to an EEZ as the mainland. This applies to Crete, Rhodes and Lesbos.

Athens cites precedents such as a 2022 ruling by the International Court of Justicethat found that Colombia had violated the sovereign rights of Nicaragua by authorizing fishing activities in the Central American country's EEZ. The ICJ found that when interests overlap a fair demarcation must be reached that takes coastlines, geographical conditions and the implications of a potential boundary into account.

Turkey's government argues that the geography of the Mediterranean plays a special role: If every small Greek island off the coast of Turkey and Cyprus  were granted a full EEZ, Turkey would be largely cut off from access to the open sea. This, the government argues, must be given greater consideration when demarcating the boundary.

Unlike Greece, Turkey has never signed UNCLOS. Therefore, it does not agree to have the dispute heard by a UN arbitration tribunal. Moreover, even international experts on the law of the sea disagree on what a "fair division” might look like in this specific case.

When Turkey pushed for the agreement in 2019, a brutal civil war was still raging in Libya. At that time, Turkey intervened militarily in the west to support the government in Tripoli, thereby repelling the offensive by eastern Libyan General Khalifa Hifter.

Since then, the relationship has changed fundamentally: A successful charm offensive by Turkey's government appears to have led to a political rapprochement with Hifter and his camp. Turkey's government now maintains mutually advantageous relations with both parts of Libya. In addition to the countries' plan to develop energy resources in the Mediterranean together, Turkish construction companies have secured contracts worth billions in recent years for Libya's reconstruction. Furthermore, the Turkish state-owned oil company TPAO and the Libyan National Oil Corporation are already working on joint plans for seismic surveys off of Libya's coast.

Turkey's government has established itself as a new military partner for Hifter's forces, supplying weapons and offering training programs. In doing so, Turkey is pushing back against the influence of Egypt and Russia in eastern Libya and strengthening its role as a geopolitical player in the region.

With the agreement now approved in the regional parliament, eastern Libya also aims to secure its share of the lucrative natural resources. This could give Hifter greater international influence in the future. Disputes in the eastern Mediterranean are likely to escalate further as a result of the agreement — particularly once Turkey and Libya begin extracting oil and gas from the disputed maritime area.

The agreement also jeopardizes a major project that is still in the planning stages: the EastMed pipeline, which is intended to transport natural gas from Israel and Cyprus via Greece, thereby making an important contribution to energy security in the European Union. However, the corridor established by Turkey and Libya cuts across the planned route of the pipeline. Turkey has also argued that projects involving cables or pipelines on its claimed continental shelf require its consent.

In the long term, this could lead to a division of the eastern Mediterranean region into two competing energy and defense blocs: The Turkey-Libya axis might be countered by an EU-supported alliance of Greece, Cyprus, Egypt and Israel.

#554. Republicans see shrinking boost from redistricting

House Republicans are quietly scaling back hopes for the 10-seat boost they sought from redistricting nationwide, amid a souring political climate and polls showing a collapse in GOP support among Latino voters in Texas and Florida.

Why it matters: On their down days, Republicans privately worry that the great redistricting war of 2026 will net them only one seat nationwide, according to top GOP strategists.

Zoom out: At the beginning of the cycle, Republicans viewed redistricting like an umbrella in a storm: It could keep their House majority dry in a light drizzle — but in a political hurricane, it wouldn't be enough.

  • They're not second-guessing the redistricting strategy, even as they wince at the price tag. According to AdImpact, Republicans have spent $133 million on TV ads trying to win in 15 redrawn districts that were supposed to be easy pickings.
  • Still, they no longer talk seriously about redistricting saving their House majority, as they did a year ago — before the Iran war helped boost inflation and sink President Trump's approval ratings.

By the numbers: Early this summer, Republicans believed they had created 16 more GOP-friendly seats: five in Texas, four in Florida, two in Ohio and one each in Alabama, Louisiana, Missouri, North Carolina and Tennessee.

  • But since then, courts tossed a Missouri map and the political environment has darkened, with Democrats holding an 8-percentage-point lead in new Washington Post-ABC polling.

GOP support has eroded particularly among Latino voters, raising Republican fears of losing seats their mapmakers counted on after Trump nearly won the Hispanic vote outright in 2024.

  • "Texas, I'd be surprised if we got all five," Rep. Tom Emmer (R-Minn.) told Axios this month. "Florida, I'd be shocked if we got all four."

The other side: Then factor in California, where Gov. Gavin Newsom, eager to show his party he was willing to fight back, engineered a new map with four more solidly Democratic seats — and one slightly more competitive seat.

  • Democrats are supremely confident they can win all five — and their main super PAC has already stopped super PAC spending in one.

Zoom in: So, two weeks before the Nov. 3 election, Republicans are squinting at the map and expecting to win 7 to 12 of the 15 remaining seats they redistricted.

The breakdown: GOP strategists privately think Republicans can still win 2 to 4 of the 5 seats that were redrawn in Texas, and 2 (maybe 3) of the 4 new seats in Florida.

  • They could go 0 for 2 in the redrawn seats in Ohio. The revamped seat in North Carolina is a jump ball.
  • They're banking on winning all three new seats in Tennessee, Alabama and Louisiana.
  • Under their most pessimistic calculations, Republicans worry their mid-cycle redistricting wave could yield just 7 new seats in red states.

But then factor in Newsom's countermove in California, which put Democrats in a strong position to flip 5 seats.

  • And in Utah, a judge forced the state to redraw its districts, obliterating a GOP-friendly seat — and creating a Democratic-leaning one in the Salt Lake City area.
  • So as it stands now, a worst-case scenario for the GOP's final score after all that redistricting: Republicans gain 7 seats and Democrats gain 6.
  • A year of nationwide redistricting to net one Republican seat.

The details: Republicans are confident about three pickups in Southern states, where the Supreme Court's Voting Rights Act decision allowed GOP legislatures to redraw maps.

  • They have already conceded one redrawn seat in Ohio as part of their midterm triage.

Then there's Texas. Republicans are truly confident about just two of the five seats they redrew: The 9th District in the Houston area and the 32nd District in the Dallas area.

  • The main Republican House super PAC has pulled out of Texas' 28th District in South Texas, essentially conceding the seat held by Rep. Henry Cuellar (D). It was rejiggered from a district that Trump won by 7.3 points in 2024 to one he would have won by 10.4 points.
  • In Texas' 34th District, which touches the Rio Grande Valley and was similarly redrawn to include more Republicans, parties are spending roughly $18 million on TV ads. Democratic Rep. Vicente Gonzalez had a small lead over Republican Eric Flores in the latest NYT/Sienna poll.
  • And in Texas' 35th District in Central Texas, which went from one that Kamala Harris won by 33.7 points to one that Trump would have won by 10.4 points, both parties have spent close to $6 million, according to AdImpact. It's too close to call.

The bottom line: In a true blue wave, Republicans could lose seats Trump won by double digits. But in that scenario, Republicans would be unlikely to blame their losses on redistricting.

  • They'd point the finger at Trump.

#555. Hemingway’s Vendetta

On June 4, 1952, Ernest Hemingway stood at his typewriter at Finca Vigía, his home outside Havana, and wrote to one of his favorite correspondents, Harvey Breit, of the Times Book Review. The letter wanders from topic to topic: his favorite saint (St. Jude), some has-beens of American literature (John Dos Passos and John Steinbeck), and why he stands while typing. “I write standing up because sitting down broadens the ass,” he says. “I have had a narrow, hard ass all of my life and I keep it and a good left hook as other writers preserve their clippings and I hope to be buried with both of them.”

Hemingway also brings up a thirty-year-old writer and former bullfighter named Barnaby Conrad. Conrad fought bulls in Spain in the mid-nineteen-forties, and published his first novel, “The Innocent Villa,” in 1948. He had sent it to Hemingway, along with a fan letter. “He sounded like a good, straight kid,” Hemingway tells Breit, “and I am pretty sure he did whatever he said he did with bulls.” He says he meant to reply, but wasn’t sure what to say about “not a very good novel.” He clipped Conrad’s letter inside the book and forgot about it.

Now Hemingway has received Conrad’s newly published second novel, “Matador.” It’s bound to be derivative of his own meditation on bullfighting, “Death in the Afternoon,” he tells Breit: “Death” is the ur-text, the book that derives from the art itself, whereas all subsequent books derive from “Death”; and, on the whole, “I am not proud of what it has begotten.” As for “Matador,” “I can’t tell about this book until I read it and I hope the hell it is good.”

Seven days later, on June 11th, Hemingway is back at the typewriter, addressing the same correspondent about the same young novelist. Now that he has read “Matador,” the good, straight kid has become a fraud, a defamer, and, worst of all, a gentleman. The letter is so unhinged that he tells Breit to return it without showing anyone else. He has already written one draft too incautious to send at all.

If Hemingway let off steam in his June 11th letter, it soon built up again. Over the next five years, he bad-mouthed Conrad to his Scribner’s editor Wallace Meyer, his fellow bullfighting aficionado Rupert Bellville, the Chicago Tribune literary editor Fanny Butcher, the New York Post columnist Leonard Lyons, and C. W. Wilcox of Scribner’s Bookstore, in addition to Breit. Conrad, oblivious, continued sending him gushing letters.

As a boy in Hillsborough, California, near San Francisco, Conrad was drawn to what he called the Hemingway model of the intellectual man’s man. While studying art in Mexico City one summer during college, he began attending bullfights. At his third fight, he jumped into the ring, which was illegal but not uncommon, and made rudimentary passes using his Brooks Brothers raincoat as a cape. He got out intact; the raincoat didn’t. A Mexican matador offered to teach him the fundamentals of bullfighting, but during one lesson a bull struck him from behind, smashed his leg—Conrad later said his knee made a sound “like a popping log in a fireplace”—and broke several fingers. Back in the United States, he underwent surgery, and had to wear a brace and walk with a cane. His applications to the V-12 Navy College Training Program and a volunteer ambulance service were both rejected.

After graduating from Yale, Conrad joined the State Department as a code clerk and then got posted to Spain as a vice-consul, in late 1943. There, he befriended two of the towering matadors of the century, Juan Belmonte and Manolete, and began training with them. When Conrad was knocked down at a practice session, Belmonte ran to help him while Manolete lured the animal away. In 1945, billed as Niño de California, Conrad appeared on a program with Belmonte. He ultimately fought about thirty times, ten on programs with Belmonte, and considered resigning from the State Department to become a full-time matador, but another knee injury foreclosed that possibility. When he left Spain, at the end of 1945, Belmonte and Manolete both came to Seville to see him off.

Back in the United States, Conrad turned his adventures in Spain into “The Innocent Villa,” published in 1948 to mixed reviews. He then wrote a novel about a legendary bullfighter on the day of his last fight. Although the book includes a disclaimer that all its characters, apart from a brief appearance by Belmonte, are fictional, its protagonist is transparently based on Manolete, who had been fatally gored in 1947. Published in 1952, “Matador” became a best-seller.

Part of “Matador” ’s appeal grew out of Conrad’s experiences in Spain. Reviewers and feature writers stressed that he had studied under renowned bullfighters, entered the ring, and—the intellectual man’s man—emerged to tell what it was really like.

Not so, according to Hemingway. Conrad is “very skillful,” he tells Breit in the June 11th letter, but “he would never convince the boys.” The “Matador” author photo shows Conrad by a poster for one of his bullfights, and the last line is “Entrada por invitación.” If it was an invitation-only fight, Hemingway says, then “nobody was fighting nothing for money and the bulls were calves.” A calf presented nothing like the mortal danger of a full-grown bull, and killing one in the ring “is the worst form of chickenshit I know.” Hemingway further suggests that Conrad is not just a fraud but a fop, the “Gentleman Calf-Sticker” and the “Dean Acheson of bull fighting,” a reference to the patrician Secretary of State. “Gentleman” was an insult; Hemingway once told the editor Maxwell Perkins that F. Scott Fitzgerald was always a gentleman but “never a man.”

In “Death in the Afternoon,” Hemingway had called bullfighting “the only art in which the artist is in danger of death.” He tells Breit in the June 11th letter that he had fought “the hard way” during Pamplona’s famous bullfighting festival, the Fiesta de San Fermín, entering the ring “six mornings straight” for seven years (a number he would inflate to eleven years in a subsequent letter), facing “every sort of bitch and bastard animal” that had fought “as many as 20 times” in amateur events. (Animals that have fought previously are more dangerous, because they may have figured out that the cape is just a distraction.) This wasn’t calf-sticking, he says: “People got killed for nothing every morning.” Even so, he knew his limits. The matador Maera tried to persuade him to enter the ring with “one famous educated cow” by saying, “Ernesto you son of a bitch you could not be killed by a more distinguished animal.” But, he recalls, “I did not want any part of her.” Some people begged him to stay out of the ring altogether, before it was too late, he says, while others remarked that “if we get killed in July we do not have to be rained on and broke in October.”

As Hemingway acknowledged elsewhere, though, the Pamplona festival was hardly a showplace for grace under pressure. It featured an early-morning melee in which several hundred men would enter the ring. A heifer, a steer, or occasionally a young bull would be released, usually with its horns covered. The men would taunt and tease the animal until it charged, and then either cape it or run. Some men would get knocked down, butted, or trampled. Hemingway’s friend Donald Ogden Stewart broke several ribs in 1924, but “he was in no real danger,” Hemingway would write a few years later, adding, “It was a rough Spanish game but no rougher than boxing or professional football.”

In several descriptions of these amateur events, including some of his own, Hemingway sounds less like a matador than a rodeo clown. When he jumped on the back of one animal, according to his friend Gerald Murphy, it collapsed beneath his weight. In “Death in the Afternoon,” Hemingway claims that, to avoid getting injured, he once grabbed a bull’s horns and rode around the ring on its muzzle while spectators laughed uproariously. In the nineteen-fifties, the Spanish writer José Luis Castillo-Puche asked about his experience in the bullring. Hemingway replied that “even my friends thought it was a laugh riot.”

Hemingway’s prominence in the history of Spanish bullfighting, of course, stems from the page, not the ring. The first year he attended the Pamplona festival, in 1923, he claimed that he and his wife, Hadley, were the only English speakers there. When he wrote about bullfighting that year in the Toronto Star Weekly, he figured that readers needed a pronunciation guide: “ ‘Ole!’—pronounced Oh-Lay!—roared the crowd.” Through his articles, novels, short stories, and “Death in the Afternoon,” he introduced much of the English-speaking world to bullfighting. Everyone who wrote about the topic thereafter toiled in his shadow, and knew it.

Conrad certainly knew it: “Death in the Afternoon” introduced him to bullfighting, as he noted in his letters to Hemingway. But he proceeded to learn a great deal on his own by living in Spain, befriending matadors, and entering the ring. Hemingway followed Spanish bullfighting during visits to Spain between 1923 and 1931. Conrad knew it more intensely for a shorter time, while living there between 1943 and 1945. Though a decade separated their sojourns in Spain, the two men shared friends and acquaintances there. Conrad, accordingly, wasn’t just one more writer working in Hemingway’s shadow. He knew people Hemingway had written about—and Hemingway was aware of it. In “Death in the Afternoon,” Belmonte is the object of sweeping generalizations and grand judgments. Conrad got the impression that Hemingway and Belmonte must have spent extended time together and known each other well; Belmonte, however, said they had met only a few times. Conrad could compare the real Belmonte with the “Death in the Afternoon” “Belmonte.” He might try to do the same with Hemingway and “Hemingway.”

Not that one needed to be a potential fact checker to provoke an unfatherly response from Papa. Throughout his career, Hemingway sniped at other writers—his juniors (Conrad, James Jones), his mentors (Sherwood Anderson, Gertrude Stein), and his contemporaries (Fitzgerald, William Faulkner). He punched up, down, and sidewise. His manly posturing fit a pattern, too. Reviewing “Death in the Afternoon,” in 1933, Max Eastman likened his style to “wearing false hair on the chest.” When they encountered each other a few years later, Hemingway struck Eastman across the face with a book.

Along with being a calf-sticking impostor, Conrad, according to Hemingway, was a malicious defamer. Pacote, the Manolete-like protagonist of “Matador,” is Spain’s greatest bullfighter and plans to retire after one last fight. After his girlfriend calls him a coward, he gets drunk but sobers up before entering the ring and gives the greatest performance of his life. As Pacote delivers the fatal thrust with his sword, the bull raises its head and gores him. He dies in the infirmary.

Hemingway can’t see past the drunkenness. If Manolete was sober as he entered the ring for his final fight, the book is a malicious libel, he tells Breit in the June 11th letter. He tells Butcher, Lyons, and Wilcox the same thing. In a July 15th letter, he urges Breit to write a magazine article about “Matador” ’s slanders and their reception in Spain, given that “the Spanish regard Manolete with practically a religious veneration,” and offers to provide “detailed suggestions how you might build it.” Breit apparently didn’t take him up on it. If “Matador” did provoke any controversy in Spain, Conrad doesn’t mention it in his two memoirs, “Fun While It Lasted” and “Name Dropping,” nor in his autobiographical articles.

Eight years after “Matador,” Hemingway himself issued a harsher indictment against Manolete. In 1960, Life featured his three-part account of the 1959 Spanish bullfighting season, later gathered into a book called “The Dangerous Summer,” which proved to be his last major work published during his lifetime. In it, he blames Manolete, dead since 1947, for debasing bullfighting through vulgar showmanship and outright cheating. Hemingway may have seen his accusation as broadly comparable to the one he saw in “Matador”: dishonor. But there’s a difference between drinking before entering the ring once and corrupting bullfighting for years. There are differences, too, between “Matador,” a work of fiction, and “The Dangerous Summer,” a work of nonfiction; and between Conrad, a novice novelist when he wrote “Matador,” and Hemingway, a Nobel laureate in literature when he wrote “The Dangerous Summer.”

Hemingway’s accusations caused an uproar in Spain and attracted coverage in the United States. Manolete’s friends and admirers defended him from the charge that he had sullied bullfighting—including, in a letter to Life, Barnaby Conrad. After faulting Hemingway for misconstruing Manolete’s style in the ring, he closed on a gracious note: “Mr. Hemingway still writes more interestingly on the bulls than anyone going, whether I agree with him or not.”

Did any of Hemingway’s left hooks even strike their target? Three weeks after Hemingway’s June 11th letter, Breit described Conrad as “young and handsome” in the Times Book Review. The European Union wants to relax rules for pesticides, saying it will be easier for farmers to adopt more environmentally friendly options. But health and environmental experts say the risks are far too great.

As part of its drive to "streamline and simplify" EU laws, the European Commission has proposed sweeping changes for agriculture and food safety legislation. These include a move to ease rules controlling the use of pesticides and herbicides, which help fight pests and weeds.

Under current EU rules, which date back to 2011, primary pesticide components, or active substances, are only granted approval for limited periods of no more than 15 years.

The European Commission's proposed revamp of the system would make indefinite greenlighting — including of the controversial glyphosate, which has been linked to cancer and kills almost all plant life — the default. Safety reviews would only be done in exceptional cases, and regulatory authorities, not the industry, would bear responsibility.

The commission has said its goal is to cut costs, simplify work for authorities and help farmers stay competitive. Scientists and doctors, however, warn that concerns over human health and environmental impact from these toxic substances are being brushed aside.

The European Commission has said it's responding to complaints from farmers and the agriculture industry to clarify EU rules that govern pesticide use.

A joint statement released in May by 19 food associations, including CropLife Europe, which promotes pesticides, said complicated administrative procedures and a lengthy approvals process meant farmers were "left without any effective solutions to protect their crops against insects, weeds and diseases," putting food security and livelihoods at risk.

"The existing system of periodic renewal assessment of every active substance has become unsustainable," the commission's press service told DW, citing "chronic delays" and "bottlenecks" in the approval of new, more sustainable products.

In recent negotiations, EU member states said open-ended authorizations should be restricted to "low-risk active substances."

But their stance is still wanting to extend initial approval periods for other substances to up to 15 years, and a maximum of 25 years for renewals.

Herbert Dorfmann, a center-right Italian member of the European Parliament, said there is currently a yearslong delay in approving new pesticides, including more eco-friendly options, meaning farmers are forced to rely on older substances. Or, if these are banned, go without altogether.

The commission wants to "make the system more efficient and responsive," while guaranteeing that "the protection of human and animal health and the environment remain the highest priorities."

Dorfmann, who as co-rapporteur is responsible for interinstitutional negotiations on the issue, backs the commission's stance, saying the EU needs "to open up the system for innovation."

He aims to bring the parliament's proposal on the new pesticide rules to a vote among EU lawmakers before the end of the year.

Ecologists and medical experts, however, have spoken out forcefully against the proposed changes, saying the potential implications for human health and the environment are being downplayed.

In September, more than 500 experts from across the EU signed an open letter calling on the European Parliament to suspend its consideration of the new regulations and conduct an independent investigation into health and environmental impacts.

On October 7, UN rights experts also backed the call for a full review into whether the proposed changes violated the EU's obligations under international human rights law, especially with regard to agricultural workers and children.

Pesticides can persist in the environment, contaminating water and degrading soils. They can also drive biodiversity loss and poison other animals higher up the food chain.

When it comes to humans, the European Environment Agency has said it was "not yet possible to estimate the burden of disease linked to current levels of exposure to all pesticides."

However, exposure to chemical pesticides, including banned substances that still persist in the environment, has been linked to various cancers, chronic respiratory disease, neurological disorders and developmental delays, as well as problems with fertility and behavior.

And exposure is widespread: A survey conducted between 2014 and 2021 found that 84% of urine samples from both children and adults in five European countries contained residue from two or more pesticides.

Martina Vijver, scientific director and ecotoxicology expert at Leiden University in the Netherlands, said experts were "deeply concerned" that the EU's current plan "will weaken rather than strengthen the level of protection for pesticide regulation."

Speaking at the European Parliament in September, she said regular reassessments were key to evaluating the safety of pesticides with new findings, pointing out that the perception of risk changes over time.

"The regulatory system must remain capable of correcting yesterday's decisions with tomorrow's knowledge," she said.

Salome Roynel, a policy officer at the Pesticide Action Network Europe, an NGO based in Brussels, said the harmful effects of pesticides aren't often fully understood until years later, when the product is up for reconsideration.

"New science emerges all the time," she told DW, highlighting how 56 active substances have been banned in the EU since 2011 as a result of periodic reviews.

That includes the insecticide chlorpyrifos, previously used in orchards and on vegetable and cereal crops, which has been tied to harmful effects on children's brain development.

“People are feeling all these cultural divides that are played out through the proxy of housing and everyday issues around parking,” said Alan Good, a Lib Dem councillor for Ancoats and Beswick. “Residents tell me they look at the shiny new towers and there’s a resentment – a feeling that they’re not benefiting from what Manchester is benefiting from.”

Good said he felt Manchester was becoming a “northern version of London” as an “insane” property crisis compounded the misery for the city’s 20,000 people on the social housing register.

Dr Matthew Stallard, a University College London researcher and chair of trustees at Manchester Central Foodbank, said there was an uncomfortable disconnect between the city’s reputation nationally – with “Manchesterism” becoming a byword for a thriving metropolis with an eye on broader social benefits – and the reality for many in the city.

“If you look at central Manchester and all the skyscrapers and you go out in every direction by a mile and a half or two miles, you’re then in some of the most deprived neighbourhoods in the country. In Ancoats, it’s a very clear line where the money is and where the money’s not.”

In summer 2016, Burnham was keenly aware of Greater Manchester’s housing crisis. He told the Salford Star that if elected, he would stop large property firms benefiting from multimillion-pound taxpayer-funded loans from the mayoral authority.

There were concerns, he said, that the Greater Manchester combined authority (GMCA) fund had “been used to provide loans to large companies, which should have used their own finance instead, and that is too focused on city centre and luxury schemes”.

Barely a year later, Burnham signed off a £55m loan to Jersey-registered property firms owned by Sheikh Mansour, scion of one of the world’s wealthiest families with a reported fortune of about £300bn.

GMCA said its loans fund was available to all developers who met the lending criteria and that the loans had been repaid in full. But these decisions are now being investigated by councillors on GMCA’s scrutiny committee.

There are awkward questions for Burnham, too. Weeks before the loans were approved, he enjoyed hospitality from City’s owners in a meeting that came to light only last week after disappearing from public records. The prime minister initially said he had had only one “significant” meeting with the club’s bosses – in 2021 – but a further five have since emerged.

Burnham said last week he had asked GMCA to conduct a “manual trawl of everything, as I want everything possible to be out there”. He added: “There’s no question about that – full transparency.”

There appeared to be little transparency, however, when it came to the council’s long-term relationship with Manchester City’s owners.

In December 2015, it signed a 10-year “collaboration agreement” with Abu Dhabi giving the emirates first refusal on land near the city centre. A specific clause in effect gagged the council’s democratically elected politicians from announcing the deal without written approval from Sheikh Mansour’s firm.

Other Manchester developers only discovered this years later. “It’s blatant partiality on a massive scale. It stinks,” said one property consultant. A council spokesperson defended the clause as “a standard agreement between two parties that ensures announcements or information releases are agreed by all parties”.

All of the property, now worth an estimated £350m, is ultimately owned by Sheikh Mansour via Jersey-domiciled subsidiaries, with the super-rich Emirati receiving the rent paid by the hundreds of mostly young professionals who live there.

The council said it had received nearly £3m as part of an undisclosed profit-sharing agreement and a further £17.6m in council tax. However, the precise return to the taxpayer – and the terms under which the deals were struck – remain unknown.

Leese, 75, stepped down as council leader after 25 years in December 2021, becoming honorary president at Manchester City two years later. When approached by the Guardian, he denied the Abu Dhabi deal was shielded from scrutiny and defended his appointment at Manchester City.

“This was something that happened after I retired from the council. Whether it would have been appropriate when I was on the council, I would have questioned that,” Leese said.

One senior former council officer, who worked in the property and development team, described the culture in this period as “Kremlin-like” or akin to “a medieval court” where officials could show “no dissent” towards the decisions of the Labour leadership.

“When you’ve got a council that’s 99% one party, there wasn’t really room for discussion,” they said, adding that they felt some senior leaders got “blinded by schmoozing around” with Abu Dhabi executives and others.

Leese denied he got too close to Manchester City, the team he supports, or gave them favourable treatment as council leader. “There are a lot of players in Manchester that the city council is close to,” he said.

“That’s the way the city works – in partnership.”

Manchester city council has strongly denied claims of a “sweetheart deal” with Abu Dhabi after researchers at the University of Sheffield concluded it had “sold the family silver too cheaply” by giving the land to Abu Dhabi at a cheaper rate than comparable developments.

In 2017, three years after the first Abu Dhabi deal and the year Burnham became Greater Manchester mayor, council leaders made another huge bet. They paid £42m for a hollowed out retail park next to the £1bn Manchester Life development, described at the time as the biggest ever land purchase in Manchester city centre.

Its price was at least six times higher per acre than the adjacent land sold for £5m to Sheikh Mansour – and it would later be identified as the permanent future home of Burnham’s No 10 North.

This former retail park was also a partnership with Abu Dhabi. Again, the terms were shielded from scrutiny as members of the public were excluded from council hearings – but inside the town hall, the fallout was significant.

A senior council property officer, Dominic Hayes, resigned abruptly in March 2018 over concerns about the deals the council was doing with Mansour’s group. He listed a series of complaints in an email to the council chief executive, Joanne Roney, who the previous year had succeeded the all-powerful Howard Bernstein when he retired after 29 years in charge.

In a tense meeting days after the resignation, a senior council figure, Eddie Smith, is said to have told colleagues that Hayes had thrown them “under a bus” and that the leadership was furious. According to contemporaneous notes by a colleague, seen by the Guardian, Smith said Hayes had made “not acceptable [and] possibly dangerous” criticisms of a Manchester City board member who was a key figure in the Manchester Life deal.

Smith is then said to have commented: “You don’t criticise the council’s relationship with the football club in this organisation and hope to survive.” Smith, who left the council in 2020, denies making the comment.

Yet Hayes’ concerns were serious enough to warrant an internal investigation into governance at the council. It found that anxieties about the council’s deal-making were not limited to just one officer.

A second senior employee told auditors they were concerned that the council had “overpaid” for the rundown retail park, according to an official record of the meeting. That officer raised concerns that the Abu Dhabi-led project was “driving the master planning for a site they didn’t own”.

When Burnham became prime minister in July, the site was tipped as the permanent home for No 10 North. As scrutiny of the Abu Dhabi link grew in October, these plans were quietly shelved.

Council sources noticed a change of approach under Leese’s successor, Bev Craig, when she became council leader in December 2021.

Craig ordered a review of the Manchester Life deal to make sure it was “all legal, all above board”, she told BBC Radio Manchester this month, adding: “The answer was yes.”

One of the reviews commissioned by Craig, who succeeded Burnham this year as Greater Manchester mayor, was published in July. It found that while the deals with developers such as Abu Dhabi had delivered “significant benefits”, there were “weaknesses in documentation, monitoring and assurance”, particularly around the profit-sharing nature of these deals.

Prof Jonathan Silver, a University of Sheffield researcher who has analysed the Manchester Life deal, said the growing buy-up of land and property by Emiratis raised “major concerns about the fragility of the ‘Manchester model’ and its reliance on attracting flows of international finance, whatever the cost”.

Silver said there were “urgent questions” about what would happen to the hundreds of homes in the Manchester Life developments if Sheikh Mansour was forced or decided to sell Manchester City in light of the cheating scandal.

Leese remains confident the Abu Dhabi deals were a huge success for the city. Asked whether he would have handled the partnership any differently given the concerns raised by academics and others, he said: “No. Nothing that I can think of. It is a phenomenally good deal for Manchester.”

When you think about it intellectually or from a distance, what is Abbott’s persistent appeal to Texans at this late date, even if some of the trends that you are talking about, whether it’s school closures or rising prices, are, in fact, true?

With Greg Abbott, no, I never have. He doesn’t really have relationships with legislators. He’s a pretty isolated governor.

What is really at the center of the political conversation in Texas?

They’re donating plasma, yes, to be able to make ends meet.

What kind of business did your granddad build?

And, yeah, so, people soured on that. I think that is what is missing in this political debate, that capitalism in and of itself is not a bad thing. It can be a good thing. It used to be a good thing in this country. It’s just that it has been so distorted.

Would you agree that the most outstanding or glaring example of crony capitalism is the President of the United States himself, especially in the second term?

Yes, it’s bad. It’s bad. I will tell you also, Greg Abbott has been engaging in crony capitalism for a long time.

It sounds to me that your eagerness to discuss Greg Abbott is understandable. You’re running against Greg Abbott. But you don’t seem to want to dwell too much on Donald Trump. Am I wrong?

When I started this campaign, I made sure my whole staff understood we have one enemy: it is Greg Abbott. And so I’ve spent a year focussed on Greg Abbott. And I will say, listen, I have to win Trump voters to win this race. Latinos went for Trump in Texas last year.

And I think at a time when most Texans, probably most Americans, are recognizing that it’s really, really bad, we need to bring those people along and say there’s a better way forward. It’s a way forward that is maybe even almost post-partisan, where—because of the disappointments in both parties, because, let’s be honest, while it’s nowhere near as blatant as Donald Trump’s corruption, corporate influence has corrupted both political parties.

I can give you examples of elected Democratic leaders who will grandstand on culture-war issues only to vote with corporate interests again and again. I won’t name names because—

Because, again, David, I have one enemy in this race.

Now Charlie Brown is finally going to kick the football right through the goalposts; the Democratic Party is going to make a big comeback in the state of Texas. I am Catholic, Mexican, and superstitious, so I don’t want to jinx myself, but I will tell you that I’d rather be sitting where we are than where Greg Abbott is right now. The difference between where Beto was in 2018—when we came so close—is that even Beto will tell you he thinks he would’ve won [the Senate] had he had a strong candidate running for governor. We have a ticket this year. We’ve had, in the past, Beto or Colin Allred, right? We’ve had one star, but not a ticket. We have, for the first time, not just me and James Talarico but Democrats running up and down the ballot in every state, House seat, State Senate seat, congressional seat. We haven’t had that in years in Texas.

So what I’m hearing is a prediction for a blue wave in the state of Texas.

I think there’s no doubt a blue wave is coming, and our job is to maximize it, because it’s an opportunity of a generation, really. This is not going to be a normal election cycle. People are angry at [the] status quo for good reason, right? And so, yes, we have an opportunity to win that—if we don’t, they’re never going to let us get this close again, I will tell you. And we know here in Texas that Republicans are already planning to redraw congressional maps next year now that the U.S. Supreme Court has gutted the Voting Rights Act.

When they redraw the maps, when it’s all said and done, we’ll lose ten Democrats from Congress. We’ll lose five seats next year, [and then] the next U.S. census will show Texas gains another four to five seats. That’s about ten seats off the table for Democrats in Congress. There’s no Democratic control of Congress when that happens. Texas becomes too big, the math no longer works, and those are the stakes. We need a governor in Texas who will veto rigged maps. Voters in the southwest San Fernando Valley will elect a new representative to the Los Angeles City Council on Nov. 3, and it will be either businessman Tim Gaspar or local government veteran Barri Worth Girvan — the top vote-getters in the June primary for the 3rd District council seat held since 2013 by Bob Blumenfield, who is termed out.

The candidates are campaigning on issues including improving public safety, reducing homelessness, providing more affordable housing and spurring economic development.

Gaspar founded Gaspar Insurance in 2008 and sold it in 2021 after he built it into one of the largest independent insurance companies in the Valley, and worked with several nonprofit and community groups: Discovery Cube Los Angeles, YMCA of Metropolitan Los Angeles, Viewpoint School‘s board of trustees and the West Valley/Warner Center Chamber of Commerce.

He would bring a pro-business perspective to City Council and help it make decisions that support business owners and residents alike.

Gaspar came in first during the primary election with 46.1% of the vote. He is running with support from groups including the Valley Industry & Commerce Assn. and the Los Angeles Police Protective League, a union representing LAPD officers. He’s also been endorsed by Blumenfield, L.A. County Supervisor Kathryn Barger, real estate developer Rick Caruso and former Democratic state Assemblymember Bob Hertzberg, Worth Girvan’s former boss.

Worth Girvan is a longtime city and county employee, with nearly 20 years working in public service. Prior to running, she was L.A. County Supervisor Lindsey Horvath’s district director for three years, handling constituent services on the Westside and in the Valley.

Before that, she spent eight years as an aide to Hertzberg, who failed to take Horvath’s seat in 2022.

Worth Girvan secured 42.5% of the vote in the primary, only 4% shy of her opponent.

Worth Girvan said her two decades of experience at the city and county levels have helped her build relationships and gain deep knowledge of the communities she works with.

“It allows me to be able to make a phone call and to be able to know how to bring about the change that we need,” she said.

She’s been endorsed by the L.A. County Democratic Party and labor unions including the local chapters of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers and the Service Employees International Union. She’s also been endorsed by Horvath and former L.A. Mayor Antonio Villaraigosa.

Fundraising data will be updated regularly until the election.

Council District 3 includes the southwestern San Fernando Valley communities of Woodland Hills, Reseda, Tarzana and Canoga Park. It also includes the Warner Center, a commercial hub in Woodland Hills with a massive development plan a decade in the making.

They disagree on the mayor’s Inside Safe program, a housing-first project that offers motel or hotel rooms to people living on the street. While Worth Girvan supports Inside Safe as a good starting point, Gaspar doesn’t; the housing-first approach doesn’t work, he said.

Gaspar said he has no tolerance for human suffering or for encampments. It’s critical that people are offered “wraparound services” including mental health treatment, rather than just putting a roof over their heads at high cost to the city.

“As long as someone is on the street, sleeping in a tent or whatever the case is, they’re not getting help,” he said.

Worth Girvan said the housing-first mentality works, but there should be more permanent supportive housing built instead of relying on interim hotel housing.

“We can’t afford to stop there,” she said. Follow-up with new tenants should come from partnerships with the county’s Departments of Public Health and Mental Health, she said, and her experience and relationships with those departments will be key.

Both candidates raise concerns with State Bill 79, a state law that allows high-rises near transit centers, but for different reasons.

Gaspar said removing the community from shaping decisions about its future is detrimental and the state shouldn’t determine where or when such projects happen.

“When it comes to something getting built or any significant change within a community, the community deserves a right to be at the table,” he said.

Worth Girvan supports SB 79 but would advocate for local carve-outs for hillsides and high fire-severity zones to better balance the need for housing and community safety.

The southwest Valley needs more affordable housing near transit, Worth Girvan said, not luxury apartments or market-rate living. Any project in the district should have a clear plan for fire mitigation in the wake of the nearby Palisades fire and the Woolsey fire, she said.

Both candidates support the Warner Center’s 2035 development plan, which Gaspar called a “mini-SB 79.” The area plans to add thousands of housing units, all with the community’s support.

Worth Girvan supports Mayor Karen Bass for reelection over her challenger, Councilmember Nithya Raman, saying she agrees with Bass on key issues.

“I believe that, you know, there’s alignment there,” Worth Girvan said.

Gaspar declined to say who he supports, saying he wants to have a good relationship with whoever wins and to be able to work with the next mayor to serve his constituents. He’s “rooting for the city of L.A. at the end of the day,” he said.

Andy Burnham says he wants an “honest discussion” with the British public about the possibility of a return to free movement between the UK and the EU as part of his plans for a new relationship with the bloc.

The prime minister has been open about his desire to revisit the relationship between Britain and Brussels. He has said a new deal could mean the UK goes “all the way” and rejoins the EU.

Other options discussed by Burnham include joining the single market, which would require signing up to free movement, or being part of the customs union. Both would require an element of agreeing to rules set by the EU.

In an interview with the Sunday Times, he said: “I think you have to have a very factual, quite evidence-based debate about this.”

Burnham has previously committed to the red lines in Labour’s 2024 election manifesto, which ruled out the return of free movement, single market membership or a customs union.

It means any fundamental change in the relationship is likely to have to wait until after a general election, expected by 2029.

Burnham acknowledged freedom of movement had “very significant challenges associated with it”, but he suggested that European migration had changed in the past decade.

“It was a type of immigration that perhaps wasn’t as difficult [as now],” he told the Sunday Times. “Revolving-door migration where people would come and then go back is different. The balance of migration has changed, hasn’t it, since then? I think we’ve just got to have an honest discussion about those things.”

Burnham will meet EU leaders at a summit in Brussels on 20 November.

He argued there had been reciprocal benefits of free movement for Britons, particularly for young members of the population wanting to experience living in Europe.

Migration was one of the dominant topics of debate during the 2016 Brexit referendum campaign. In the 12 months to June 2016 – the month of the Brexit leave vote – net migration was at a near-record high of 335,000, according to the Office for National Statistics.

Decisions made after the Brexit result would lead to higher levels of migration, however. Net migration peaked at a record 944,000 in the year to March 2023 due to a surge of foreign workers encouraged to come to the UK by Boris Johnson’s Conservative government as the UK emerged from the Covid pandemic.

It has fallen sharply since then, with net migration dropping to well below pre-Brexit levels. It stood at 171,000 last year, almost half of what it was in 2016.

Burnham has said he will take the opportunity around the summit with EU leaders in November to lay out to the British public different options for the future relationship with the bloc.

He said he could not imagine recommending that the UK join the Schengen area, in which border controls have been abolished, or ditching the pound to adopt the euro.

Nigel Farage said the prime minister’s comments were a “total betrayal” of voters who backed Brexit, including in Burnham’s Makerfield constituency.

The Reform UK leader added: “He is also deluded to say immigration was different when we were members of the EU. We will fight this dishonesty.”

Chris Philp, the shadow home secretary, said: “Andy Burnham knows full well that a return to EU free movement would mean handing back control of our borders and allowing uncontrolled migration from across Europe. The British people voted to end free movement, and there can be no return to it.”

Another creative test: I asked both agents to create a 1980s-style WNBA Finals souvenir program, then later to turn my photos from Game 1 into a keepsake.

  • Dots' 38-page vintage program was more visually engaging, with player photos, graphics and extensive statistics. Muse's 35-page version was less attractive but had some fun touches, including a fictional computer ad and one for my imaginary brunch.

#583. Ukraine tries to shut Moscow's airports with drone strikes on Russia

Repeated Ukrainian drone attacks are increasingly disrupting operations at Moscow's airports. The pounding is part of Ukraine's plan to shut down Russia's airpace.

For months, drone strikes by Ukraine have forced the suspension of flights to and from airports in Russia. Moscow's Domodedovo, Sheremetyevo, Zhukovsky and Vnukovo airports have had to shut down operations almost daily in October, according to reports from the independent investigative media outlet Agentstvo. On some days, the airports were reportedly closed for up to nine hours.

In early September, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy announced that Ukraine would seek to cut off the airspace around Moscow and other destinations in Russia. "Today, we would like to warn every airline that uses Russian airspace, every insurer, and everyone who continues to use major Russian airports: Russian airspace will become completely unsafe," Zelenskyy said.

The following day, Ukraine sent a corresponding warning to the International Civil Aviation Organization.

Ukraine's airspace has been closed since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022.

Russia's aviation authority, Rosaviatsiya, has issued an emergency safety "carpet" protocol that is designed to immediately shut down the airspace over a specific region or around affected airports if an aircraft violates Russian airspace, weapons are used, or unidentified objects appear in the skies. Once the protocol is announced, drastic restrictions apply to all air traffic.

"If there is a military threat, a specific section of airspace is closed off, and this carpet is declared," Oleksandr Laneckij, the chief executive officer of the Friendly Avia Support consultancy, told DW. "Essentially, every drone poses a military threat."

Laneckij noted that Yemen's Houthis have managed to significantly disrupt Saudi airspace. "I think Ukraine's military capabilities far exceed those of the Houthis," Laneckij said.

The military analyst Jan Matvejev also said Ukraine would have little trouble severely disrupting Moscow-area air traffic. Though Ukraine has not yet managed to completely shut down Russian airspace as Zelenskyy plans, Matvejev said, its armed forces "remain committed to this idea."

"To close off the airspace and trigger the 'carpet' plan, all you need are simple drones that don't have to be equipped with a warhead or even have a long range," Matvejev said. Ukraine's arms industry can produce hundreds of combat-ready long-range drones in a day, he added. That is why he believes Ukraine could have the necessary resources to shut down Moscow's airports.

Indeed, Ukraine's efforts to shut down Russian airspace are "just getting started," Simon Shuster wrote in October in the US magazine The Atlantic, citing well-informed sources.

Matvejev said Russia's air-defense systems were not entirely effective in efforts to prevent Ukrainian drones from penetrating Moscow's airspace. "If it were possible to shoot them down, they would have done so by now," he said. He added that Russia would have deployed additional air-defense troops if they were available.

Laneckij said Russia might lower the threshold for declaring the carpet protocol, which could increase the risk of deadly incidents such as the 2024 Christmas day crash of an Azerbaijan Airlines flight. Investigators say the flight was struck by a Russian air-defense missile that was being used to defend against drones, killing 38 people.

Matvejev seconded the fear that Russia's defenses could again mistakenly target civilian aircraft instead of military objects. He said little had changed about Russia's air-defense protocol since the Azeri flight was shot in 2024.

In an operation against a low-flying drone, the target is visually identified and fired upon with a machine gun. In such a scenario, the danger to aircraft is extremely low, unless they happen to be flying in the immediate vicinity.

The risk to civil aviation increases significantly when anti-aircraft missiles are deployed, Matvejev said. A missile could have defects that are practically unavoidable in mass production and could veer toward an aircraft flying nearby. Matvejev said such defense measures could not guarantee the safety of civilian aircraft during operations.

He said drones did not have to carry explosives to be disruptive. A collision between an airplane and a basic drone can have serious consequences — and, he said, a drone sent from Ukraine all the way to Moscow must be relatively large, "weighting dozens of kilograms."

In the summer of 2023, Ukrainian armed forces attacked the airport in the Russian city of Pskov, where both military and civilian aircraft were parked. Earlier this month, drone debris fell on the Sochi airport, on Russia's Black Sea coast.

Russia also attacks Ukraine's airports. In September, Zelenskyy said Kyiv's airport had been struck, as well as the Boryspil airport near the capital.

Laneckij said three factors would determine whether international airlines suspend their flights to Russia: the military threat, the degree of demand and the insurance cost.

He said issues could emerge around insurance. When insurers rate the security risk of flying to Russia as very high, they could significantly raise their rates for aircraft headed there. This would cause ticket prices to skyrocket.

Competition within Russia's aviation market has already declined because many airlines are no longer in business, Laneckij said. This has increased demand for tickets from the remaining foreign airlines.

"Whether they stay or not depends on the price they have to pay for the danger faced," he said.
