#2501. Spooky Lava Lamps – Halloween Science

Are you ready to brew up some bubbly science this Halloween? Our spooky lava lamps are a fun alternative to a traditional pumpkin and a fantastic way for kids to learn about density and chemical reactions! Using just water, oil, food colouring, and an Alka-Seltzer (or effervescent vitamin tablet), you can create a science activity kids will remember for a long time!

I also have a pumpkin lava lamp that’s perfect for a Halloween evening if you place a torch behind it!

Fill the bottle or jar a quarter full with water.

Top up to the (near) top with the vegetable oil.

The oil and water should separate into two layers, water at the bottom and oil sitting on top. Add about 6-8 drops of food colouring once they have separated.

Pop in half an Alka-Seltzer tablet and watch the bubbles form.

Add more Alka-Seltzer bit by bit to keep the bubbles rising and falling.

Another idea is to use small plastic cups made to look like pumpkins or ghosts for a mini version.

Water and oil don’t mix because water is made of polar molecules. A water molecule has a positive electrical charge on one end and a negative charge on the other. Opposite charges attract, so water molecules stick together because the positive end of one water molecule is attracted to the negative end of another.

Oil molecules are different; they are non-polar, meaning their charge is more evenly spaced out. This means oil is not attracted to water. Oil is hydrophobic (water-fearing) and tries to stay as far away from water as it can.

Oil sits on top of the water because it is less dense than water.

When you add Alka-Seltzer (made of citric acid and sodium bicarbonate), it reacts with water to form carbon dioxide gas and sodium citrate. As the carbon dioxide bubbles rise, they carry bits of coloured water with them. When the gas escapes at the surface, the coloured water drops sink back down, creating a lava lamp effect.

#2502. Scientists Built a 3D Cloak That Makes Objects Almost Invisible to Thermal Cameras

An intricate metal-and-rubber lattice steers heat around objects, concealing their presence from thermal detection.

An object doesn’t have to be visible to give away its location. Sometimes, the way it disrupts the flow of heat is enough to reveal that something is hiding there, much to the despair of camouflaged soldiers who have to endure thermal vision drones on the battlefield. Now, engineers have developed a device that can make that thermal signature almost disappear (although sadly it won’t work for infantry).

Researchers at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and the Technical University of Denmark have built a three-dimensional thermal cloak that redirects heat around hidden objects, leaving the surrounding temperature pattern nearly undisturbed. Unlike earlier designs restricted to simpler shapes or only blocking infrared in a single plane, their cloak can accommodate complex geometries and work regardless of the direction of heat flow.

The breakthrough could eventually help protect sensitive electronics, improve heat management in computer chips, and potentially conceal equipment from certain forms of thermal detection.

There’s a catch, however. This isn’t the kind of invisibility cloak that could make a person disappear from a thermal camera. The device redirects heat flowing through solid materials, whereas the human body continuously generates its own heat and emits infrared radiation into the surrounding environment

Heat naturally flows from warmer regions to cooler ones. But if you place an obstacle in the gradient’s path, then the heat flow changes. A poorly conducting object, for instance, forces heat to spread around it, creating distortions that betray its presence.

A thermal cloak must do two things simultaneously: steer heat around the hidden object and restore the original temperature pattern on the other side.

Researchers have pursued this idea for years. In 2014, scientists in Singapore demonstrated a three-dimensional thermal cloak that concealed an air bubble inside metal. More recently, a 2024 study by some of the same researchers produced intricate thermal cloaks, heat rotators and concentrators, primarily using two-dimensional designs.

The challenge was making the effect work around genuinely complex three-dimensional objects, regardless of the direction of heat flow.

“A real thermal cloak should work no matter where the heat comes from,” said Shelly Zhang, an engineering professor at Illinois.

“Our device can hide a complex 3D object in an infinite number of directions while keeping the temperature inside stable and protected.”

To accomplish this, Zhang and her colleagues developed a lattice made from tiny intersecting aluminum bars, surrounded by a rubber-like material called polydimethylsiloxane.

Aluminum conducts heat readily; the rubber conducts it poorly. So by changing the thickness and orientation of the metal bars in three dimensions, the researchers could control precisely where heat traveled.

They used mathematical models to calculate the required properties throughout the cloak, then translated those figures into a continuous structure that could actually be manufactured.

The researchers used metal 3D printing to manufacture the aluminum framework, then cast the rubber-like material into the remaining spaces.

To test their design, they created an apple-shaped core concealed inside a pear-shaped shell. They placed the completed specimen between two aluminum plates. One plate was heated to 40 degrees Celsius (104 degrees Fahrenheit), while the other was cooled with iced water.

After an hour, an infrared camera recorded the temperature distributions across the specimen.

Outside the cloak, the temperature bands remained nearly straight and evenly spaced, as though the hidden core were absent. Inside, temperatures varied much less, indicating that relatively little heat crossed the protected region.

The team also fabricated and successfully tested a different, heart-shaped cloak.

But perhaps the most striking demonstration involved human faces. The researchers designed a lattice that could conceal a detailed face-shaped object, complete with glasses, inside another face-shaped structure.

The researchers even 3D-printed intricate, face-shaped cloaks, but only tested their ability to hide heat using computer simulations, not physical experiments. The cloak works by redirecting heat around an object, not by hiding heat produced by the object itself. That means it can’t yet conceal a person or other heat-generating object from a thermal camera.

Still, the work suggests that engineers could eventually route heat around delicate electronic components or maintain more stable temperatures in demanding environments.

“Any field that needs precise control of heat or needs to protect something from being detected thermally could benefit from this work,” Zhang said.

The 3D thermal cloak was described in the journal Nature Communications.

#2504. Earth’s Ice Ages May Have Started With a Planet-Wide Ocean Domino Effect

New research suggests that cooling in the Northern Hemisphere may have triggered far-reaching ocean changes that helped drive Earth’s past ice ages.

Growing ice sheets in the Northern Hemisphere may have helped push Earth into past ice ages by triggering changes in ocean circulation thousands of miles away, near Antarctica. New research from the University of St Andrews identifies a possible mechanism connecting these distant regions, helping explain how the planet naturally reduced atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) as ice ages developed.

Scientists have long known that the Southern Ocean plays a central role in regulating atmospheric CO2. Changes in its circulation and sea ice can increase the amount of carbon stored in the deep ocean, contributing to the cooling associated with ice ages. But researchers have struggled to explain how expanding northern ice sheets could influence waters so far south.

The growth of massive Northern Hemisphere ice sheets begins with changes in Earth’s orbit that alter how solar energy is distributed across the planet. According to the new study, published in the Journal of Climate, these expanding ice sheets may set off a much larger chain of events by cooling nearby North Atlantic waters.

As those colder waters move through the global ocean circulation system, they can eventually reach the Southern Ocean. If the northern cooling is strong enough, it could alter circulation around Antarctica and promote conditions that allow more CO2 to remain stored in the ocean rather than the atmosphere.

This proposed connection provides a physical explanation for how ice sheet growth in one hemisphere could help amplify cooling across the planet.

To test the hypothesis, researchers used computer simulations that cooled only the ocean’s northernmost latitudes (mimicking the effect of a large ice sheet). They then examined whether the temperature changes could travel south and alter Southern Ocean conditions in ways that reduce atmospheric CO2.

The results showed that cooling northern waters alone produced a substantial response. The simulated reduction in atmospheric CO2 reached 74% of the reduction produced when the researchers cooled the entire planet.

The experiments support the proposed connection between Northern Hemisphere ice sheets and Southern Ocean carbon storage. However, they demonstrate that the mechanism is physically plausible in a model, rather than establishing exactly how much it contributed to individual ice ages in Earth’s past.

Lead author Dr. Madison Shankle, from the University of St Andrews’ School of Earth and Environmental Science, explained:

“Essentially, we’ve produced evidence of a plausible mechanism connecting these two far-flung puzzles pieces – Northern Hemisphere ice sheets and Southern Hemisphere ocean circulation changes leading to CO2 drawdown – which has been a tricky thing to explain in our theory of Earth’s past ice ages. Our study takes the step of actually demonstrating them working in model simulations, which is a big step forward for this field.”

Ice age research has traditionally emphasized processes occurring around Antarctica, where ocean circulation strongly influences the exchange of carbon between the atmosphere and deep ocean. The new findings suggest that temperature changes in distant northern waters may also be an important part of that system.

Past ice ages provide natural examples of how carbon moves between Earth’s atmosphere and oceans over long periods. Understanding these processes can help scientists improve climate models and investigate how the global carbon cycle responds to changes in atmospheric CO2, including the additional carbon dioxide released by human activities.

I used to think that the z-index property had a hard dependency on the position property, that you had to set position to something like relative or absolute in order to use it. But that’s not quite right.

At its core, CSS is a collection of layout algorithms. There's Flexbox, Grid, Flow layout (the default), and many others. Each layout algorithm can implement each CSS property however it wants.

When we set position: relative or position: absolute, we switch to Positioned Layout, a layout algorithm that implements z-index. It's not the only one that does, though! Flexbox and Grid both implement it as well.

When we stop thinking of CSS as a big grab-bag of properties and start thinking of it as a constellation of layout algorithms, the language starts to make a lot more sense. You can learn more about this alternative mental model in my article, “The Importance of Learning CSS”.

There's a Weird Thing here, and I think it's worth pondering about for a minute or two.

In our Photoshop analogy, there is a clear distinction between groups and layers. All of the visual elements are layers, and groups can be conjured as structural helpers to contain them. They are distinct ideas.

On the web, however, the distinction is a bit less clear. Every element that uses z-index must also create a stacking context.

When we decide to give an element a z-index, our goal is typically to lift or lower that element above/below some other element in the parent stacking context. We aren't intending to produce a stacking context on that element! But it's important that we consider it.

When a stacking context is created, it “flattens” all of its descendants. Those children can still be rearranged internally, but we've essentially locked those children in.

By default, HTML elements will be stacked according to their DOM order. Without any CSS interference, main will render on top of header.

We can lift header to the front by giving it a z-index, but not without flattening all of its children. This mechanism is what led to the bug we discussed earlier.

We shouldn't think of z-index purely as a way to change an element's order. We should also think of it as a way to form a group around that element's children. z-index won't work unless a group is formed.

As we've seen in our tooltip demo, stacking contexts can cause subtle, hard-to-diagnose bugs. Wouldn't it be better if z-index values were compared globally instead?

#2508. Popular cannabis sleep gummies may have a hidden dark side

Cannabinol (CBN) has become a popular ingredient in sleep supplements, often promoted as a natural, gentle way to relax and get a better night's rest. However, new research from the University of Connecticut suggests that this widely available cannabis compound may have risks that consumers should know about.

Scientists found that repeated exposure to CBN caused mice to develop signs of physical dependence. The findings, published in the November issue of the journal Drug and Alcohol Dependence, raise questions about the safety of using CBN regularly, particularly over long periods.

"CBN is everywhere right now. Gummies, tinctures, capsules, and it's marketed as a safe, herbal sleep supplement," says Steven Kinsey, professor in UConn's Elisabeth DeLuca School of Nursing. "Our findings suggest consumers should be aware repeated use may carry risks."

Popular Sleep Aid Shows Effects Similar to THC

CBN is a naturally occurring compound in cannabis, along with THC (tetrahydrocannabinol) and CBD (cannabidiol). Although CBN is generally considered less psychoactive than THC, the researchers discovered that it affects some of the same biological systems and can produce several key signs associated with drug dependence.

In the experiments, mice received CBN twice a day for three consecutive days. As treatment continued, the animals became less responsive to the compound, indicating that they had developed tolerance. When CBN was withdrawn, the mice also displayed physical symptoms resembling withdrawal, including head twitching and paw fluttering. These movements are comparable to the shaking that people can experience during drug withdrawal.

Another experiment revealed a surprising connection between CBN and THC. Mice previously trained to recognize and respond to THC's psychoactive effects reacted similarly when given CBN. This suggests that the two compounds produce overlapping subjective effects, at least in mice.

Together, the results indicate that CBN, despite its reputation as a milder cannabis compound, interacts with some of the same receptor systems that contribute to THC dependence.

Scientists Raise Concerns About CBN Sleep Supplements

The findings are particularly relevant because CBN products have become increasingly common, even though scientists still have limited information about their long-term effects.

The lead author of the study, Olivia Vanegas '26 Ph.D., notes "The marketing of minor cannabinoids is pretty wild right now. There are so many therapeutic claims about these drugs, but we really don't know much about their potential risks, especially with repeated use."

CBN is now widely promoted as a sleep aid, yet important questions remain unanswered. Researchers do not fully understand how CBN affects sleep quality or whether taking it repeatedly at doses commonly found in supplements could lead to addiction in humans.

There is also a historical reason for caution. Over the past century, several medications initially promoted as helpful sleep treatments were eventually found to carry serious risks of dependence and habit formation.

Potential Benefits Come With Unanswered Safety Questions

The researchers also identified potentially useful properties of CBN. The compound demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects, including at lower doses that did not produce psychoactive effects.

However, the experiments revealed another possible concern. During a simulated bacterial challenge, CBN reduced the body's ability to defend itself. This weakening of immune protection could make it easier for an infection to develop, similar to how bacteria can enter through a cut on the foot and cause illness.

These findings suggest that CBN may have medical applications, but scientists need a clearer understanding of its benefits, potential side effects, and appropriate doses before determining how it can be used safely.

Importantly, the experiments were conducted in mice, so the findings do not establish that CBN causes dependence or withdrawal in people. Additional studies are needed to understand how the compound affects humans, especially when taken frequently, at higher doses, or over extended periods.

"CBN may still have therapeutic value," he says. "But until we know more about dosing and long-term effects, the message for consumers is simple: buyer beware."

Can you extinguish a flame without touching it? This fun classic science activity is always a crowd pleaser. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air, so when it’s released near a candle, it replaces the air (which contains the oxygen needed for fire to burn) and puts out the flames!

Simply place a balloon quickly on a bottle of fizzy water when it’s first opened. If you swirl and gently shake the bottle, the CO2 will collect in the balloon and inflate it.

Curious, he photographed the strange markings and posted them in a Thai Facebook group called “What is this creature?”

It didn’t take long to get an answer. Pichai had discovered an extremely rare dinosaur footprint.

On October 20, just three days after Prathetsena’s discovery, Thailand’s Department of Mineral Resources confirmed that the markings were 120-million-year-old dinosaur footprints. And there wasn’t just one. Investigators found six impressions forming a continuous trail left by a large, meat-eating dinosaur.

The discovery, made in the Phu Hin Chom That–Phu Phrabat National Park area of Udon Thani province, is now officially the first confirmed carnivorous dinosaur tracksite in the province.

The footprints belong to a theropod, a group of predominantly carnivorous dinosaurs that walked on two legs and included formidable predators such as Tyrannosaurus rex. The exact species, however, cannot be determined from the footprints alone.

Each three-toed impression measures approximately 29 centimeters (11 inches) long and 26 centimeters (10 inches) wide. The tracks are preserved in fine-grained sandstone belonging to the Phu Phan Formation, which dates to the Early Cretaceous period.

Based on their dimensions and spacing, researchers estimate the animal stood approximately 1.42 meters (4.7 feet) tall at the hip and measured 6.3 meters (21 feet) from head to tail — about as long as a large delivery van, with hips nearly as high as the roof of a typical car.

#2513. Antarctica’s “Doomsday Glacier” Could Raise Sea Levels Twice As Much as Scientists Feared

Even without further warming, Thwaites Glacier could contribute twice as much to sea level rise by 2100 as previously estimated, according to a revised ice sheet model.

Thwaites Glacier in West Antarctica has been retreating and thinning rapidly, while satellites show substantial acceleration in its ice flow. Traditional ice sheet models have depicted slower movement, leaving scientists struggling to reconcile their simulations with the observations. The glacier lies in the Amundsen Sea Embayment, a sector responsible for roughly one-third of the Antarctic Ice Sheet’s mass loss.

A new ice sheet model developed by glaciologists at the University of California, Irvine and NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory projects that melting at Thwaites could contribute up to 30 millimeters, or about 1.2 inches, to sea level rise by 2100. That is twice previous estimates, even without additional climate warming. The study was published in Nature Communications.

“Glaciologists have long based their assumptions of ice sheet dynamics on the ‘grounding line,’ a discreet, narrow and mostly stable demarcation where ice leaves the land and begins floating on water,” said lead author Mattia Poinelli, who was a UC Irvine postdoctoral scholar in Earth system science when the study was conducted. “We have come to acknowledge a different arrangement beneath Earth’s major ocean-terminating glaciers. Satellite observations demonstrate the existence of a ‘grounding zone,’ sometimes kilometers wide, where warm ocean water is reaching deep underneath the ice sheets and causing vigorous melting.”

Synthetic aperture radar readings from the Finnish ICEYE satellite constellation allowed the researchers to detect kilometer-scale fluctuations in this zone along the main trunk of Thwaites. The data indicate that seawater penetrates several kilometers beneath grounded ice and lifts it during tidal cycles. This flushes the underside of the ice with pressurized, warm seawater, which currents altered by climate change push toward the Antarctic coastline.

That contact with the ocean affects how firmly the glacier remains anchored to ridges on the seafloor. According to Poinelli, a NASA JPL research affiliate who is now a project scientist at UCLA, the study showed that grounding zone processes accelerate the glacier’s detachment from these stabilizing features, allowing faster ice flow and deterioration.

“The Thwaites main trunk is being temporarily stabilized by the Mouginot Ridge,” he said. “Once that ridge is unpinned, the glacier will continually retreat across a predominantly retrograde seafloor toward its next topographic high, the South Ridge.”

A retrograde seafloor slopes downward farther inland, so retreat would carry the glacier into deeper water before it reaches the next rise in the terrain.

The researchers calibrated their model against more than 20 years of observations. By including a grounding zone instead of a grounding line, they reconstructed changes in the glacier from the 1990s to the present that were four times as pronounced as those represented in traditional models.

Simulations incorporating a grounding zone 4 to 6 kilometers wide, or about 2.5 to 3.7 miles, predict a 60 percent probability that the glacier will lose its anchoring contact with Mouginot Ridge by the end of the century, Poinelli said. “When you factor in key physical processes and calibrate against historical observations, you gain a reliable pathway for narrowing uncertainty in projections,” he said.

“We have documented and quantified the impacts of climate change on our planet’s sensitive ice sheets for decades, but a mounting realization gained in recent years of what is happening on the undersides of glaciers like Thwaites is leading to an even greater sense of alarm,” said co-author Eric Rignot, UC Irvine professor of Earth system science. “Most projections up-to-present have struggled to match the rapidity of the mass loss because of missing physics; our new model with more complete physics is able to match the record and then produces projections that are twice larger than before. These results predict greater risk of and exposure for coastal flooding than anticipated in the coming decades.”

#2518. Scientists discover how pancreatic cancer rewrites its DNA to escape treatment

Scientists have uncovered new details about how pancreatic cancer constantly changes its genetic makeup, allowing it to become more aggressive, spread throughout the body, and resist treatment. By examining nearly half a million individual cancer cells, researchers identified genetic changes that could reveal new ways to fight one of the deadliest forms of cancer.

The study, conducted by scientists at NYU Langone Health and its Perlmutter Cancer Center, focused on pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, a particularly difficult cancer to treat. Nearly 85 percent of patients die within five years of being diagnosed.

Although researchers have already linked more than half a dozen genes to the disease, much remains unknown about how pancreatic cancer develops and adapts. By examining tumor cells individually rather than studying them in large groups, the team gained a clearer understanding of how different populations of cancer cells change their DNA as the disease progresses.

How Pancreatic Cancer Evolves To Escape Treatment

The researchers discovered that pancreatic tumors contain a variety of distinct cell populations known as clones. Each clone consists of cancer cells with similar genetic characteristics, but their behavior can differ dramatically.

Some clones develop changes that help them withstand cancer treatments, while others become better equipped to invade surrounding tissues and spread to other parts of the body. These findings suggest that the cancer's ability to continually reorganize its genetic material plays a central role in both treatment resistance and metastasis.

Previous studies generally divided pancreatic cancer cells into two main categories: classical (less aggressive) and basal (more aggressive). However, because those studies examined large collections of cells together, scientists could not fully explain how cancer cells move from one category to the other.

The latest findings help explain this process. Genetic changes occurring within individual tumors allow cancer cells to shift between these two states, potentially making the disease more dangerous as it develops.

Scientists Identify a Gene That Could Make Pancreatic Cancer Less Aggressive

Published on October 13 in Nature Genetics, the study identified several genetic changes associated with pancreatic cancer progression. One particularly important finding involved MYC, a gene that showed heightened activity in some of the most aggressive cancer cell populations.

In additional laboratory experiments, the researchers blocked MYC activity in aggressive basal pancreatic cancer cells. Remarkably, the cells shifted back toward the less aggressive classical state.

The change also made the cancer cells more exposed to immune cells, suggesting they could become easier for the immune system to recognize and attack. Although further research is needed, the results point to MYC as a promising target for future pancreatic cancer treatments.

"Our study provides the most granular view to date of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, revealing how ongoing genetic reshaping drives tumor evolution over the course of disease," said study lead investigator Despoina Kalfakakou, PhD, a postdoctoral research fellow at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and Perlmutter Cancer Center. "These findings suggest that targeting the mechanisms underlying tumor evolution, including MYC, may help restrain the cancer's rapid progression."

"Our research also suggests that single-cell clonal analysis offers a better approach to understanding complex cancers, like pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, as it develops over time, by mapping how tumors evolve and adapt at each stage of disease progression," said study co-senior investigator Aristotelis Tsirigos, PhD, a professor in the Departments of Medicine and Pathology at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and a member of Perlmutter Cancer Center.

"More broadly, our study results highlight the critical role of clonal diversity, genetic plasticity, and interactions with the tumor's microenvironment in shaping human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma biology," said Dr. Tsirigos, who is also co-director of the Division of Precision Medicine and director of NYU Langone's Applied Bioinformatics Laboratories.

Why Pancreatic Cancer Can Evade the Immune System

The investigation also provided important clues about pancreatic cancer's ability to hide from the immune system, a characteristic that may help explain why the disease often responds poorly to immunotherapy.

Researchers discovered that certain cancer cell clones showed increased activity of the TIGIT gene, which is associated with proteins that help tumors escape immune detection.

At the same time, these clones exhibited reduced activity of the PD1 gene. This combination may help explain why immunotherapies designed to target PD1 have shown limited effectiveness against pancreatic cancer.

Understanding these differences between cancer cell populations could help scientists develop treatments that interfere with the specific mechanisms tumors use to avoid immune attack.

According to Dr. Tsirigos, clinical trials investigating drugs that inhibit MYC or TIGIT are already underway for pancreatic ductal carcinoma and other cancers. He said the latest findings underscore the need for larger studies examining the different states cancer cells can adopt and how treatments might be tailored to target them.

Nearly Half a Million Cancer Cells Reveal Hidden Tumor Diversity

To investigate how pancreatic cancer changes over time, the scientists examined gene activity (using transcriptomics) in 443,451 individual cells collected from 62 tumors belonging to 56 men and women diagnosed with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

Six of these patients also provided tumor samples both before and after chemotherapy, allowing researchers to examine changes associated with treatment.

Unlike traditional approaches that measure changes in gene activity across large groups of cells, the new analysis examined individual cells. This provided a much more detailed picture of the different cancer cell populations living within the same tumor.

The results showed that individual tumors contained approximately two to 11 distinct clones, consisting of varying combinations of classical and basal cancer cells.

By tracing the genetic origins of these clones and comparing patterns of gene activity, the researchers connected differences in DNA copy numbers with the types of cells present in each tumor.

This approach allowed them to identify which genes, including MYC and TIGIT, were particularly active at different points during cancer progression.

Together, the findings provide a more detailed understanding of how pancreatic tumors evolve, resist treatment, and spread. They also suggest that interfering with the genetic mechanisms driving these changes could offer new opportunities to combat a disease that remains exceptionally difficult to treat.

Have you ever wondered why fizzy drinks make you burp? Read on to find out and try a science activity to demonstrate!

Fizzy drinks are fizzy because they have carbon dioxide gas pumped into them under high pressure. If you have a SodaStream at home, you’ve probably seen how adding the gas makes water fizzy. Fizzy drink bottles are tightly sealed so no air can escape, but when you open the cap, the pressure drops and the carbon dioxide escapes. This is why fizzy drinks go flat over time.

When you drink a fizzy drink, it travels into your stomach. The inside of your body is around 37°C. When gas warms up, it expands and spreads out to fill all the available space. As the gas builds up, it pushes against the top of your stomach. To stop the stomach from inflating too much, you burp to let the trapped gas escape.

We can model burping with fizzy water, a balloon and a bottle! The bottle represents the stomach. As the fizzy water warms up, the carbon dioxide bubbles expand and escape from the water, inflating the balloon!

#2520. Rattlesnake bite venom is based on their personality

Published Oct 22, 2026 1:42 PM EDT

Rattlesnake venom is a complex blend of necrotic toxins designed to quickly disrupt blood coagulation and destroy tissue cells. This effective biological weapon comes at a major premium, however. Snakes must regularly replenish their venom reserves, which means they need to dole out just the right amount with each bite. Injecting too little venom into prey may allow their next meal to escape, while delivering too much can leave a rattlesnake defenseless against its own predators.

But that doesn’t mean rattlesnakes adhere to a one-bite-fits-all policy. According to researchers at California’s Loma Linda University, the amount of venom routinely varies by individual reptile. Their recent findings published in Frontiers in Ethology indicate these dosage ranges aren’t randomly distributed, and that snakes and their venom are much more complicated than many people realize.

“This study gives us a better understanding that reptiles, such as rattlesnakes, are behaviorally complex and have personalities like humans do,” explained Loma Linda University biologist and study co-author Chelsea Martin.

Martin and her colleague, William Hayes, focused on the preferences of the Pacific rattlesnake (Crotalus oreganus helleri). On three occasions over a four-week window, the team offered 48 snakes the chance to sink their fangs into a membrane-covered beaker. Previous experiments mainly focused on the frequency of bites, but Martin and Hayes also measured the amount of injected venom in each instance. They then compared the data across snakes.

“The results were striking in their consistency,” the study’s authors wrote in an accompanying Phys.org rundown.

The team also reported measured behaviors like aggressiveness, bite willingness, and the amount of venom amount were statistically significant and repeatable across many of the snakes. Additionally, their study further debunks the lingering myth that larger snakes actually produce less venom.

“Larger snakes expended more venom than smaller snakes, as expected given their greater capacity for venom storage,” explained the biologists, while noting that actual delivered levels still occasionally varied regardless of size.

The study’s authors emphasize that their findings shouldn’t be held against the rattlesnakes themselves. While often feared, the snakes generally do everything they can to avoid confrontations with larger animals like humans. Bites are almost always the result of an unintentional encounter in the wild rather than an overly aggressive serpent. If anything, it’s people who aren’t paying attention to their surroundings, and not the other way around.

“One practical lesson remains unchanged: No rattlesnake should be approached or handled on the assumption that it will behave predictably,” the authors said. “Consistent individual differences do not make any particular snake safe.”

Schizophrenia affects how people perceive their surroundings, organize their thoughts, and communicate with others. Although these symptoms are often studied separately, new research suggests they may be connected to a common disruption in brain activity.

In a study published in Science Advances, scientists identified changes in a brain rhythm known as beta-band activity that were associated with several symptoms of schizophrenia. Patients with the most pronounced changes also had greater difficulty processing sensory information, more disorganized speech, and more severe clinical symptoms.

Researchers examined brain activity in 25 people with schizophrenia and 25 healthy participants using high-resolution magnetoencephalography, or MEG. The recordings were collected at the McConnell Brain Imaging Centre at the Montreal Neurological Institute-Hospital (The Neuro). Participants completed a task involving the timing of sights and sounds, and researchers separately analyzed their speech for signs of disorganization.

Compared with the control group, participants with schizophrenia showed weaker and slower beta-band activity. These brain rhythms are involved in processes that help the brain interpret sensory information and coordinate responses to the environment.

“What struck us was that the same weakened brain signal kept showing up wherever we looked, whether patients were processing a sound, a sight or completing a movement,” said Hsi (Tiana) Wei, lead author of the study and a postdoctoral scholar in McGill University’s Department of Psychiatry and the Douglas Research Centre. “Seeing the same pattern across these different domains suggested there may be a common underlying process linking the symptoms.”

The results support the possibility that some schizophrenia symptoms arise from disturbances in how the brain constructs and updates its understanding of the world. Rather than representing entirely independent problems, difficulties with perception, behavior, and speech may share an underlying neurological process.

“This gives us early but compelling evidence that measuring the brain’s operations, especially how it builds and revises its own model of the world, may help us appreciate that there is a common thread behind the confusing array of symptoms we see in disorders like schizophrenia,” said Dr. Lena Palaniyappan, senior author of the study, Professor of Psychiatry at McGill University, and director of the Centre of Excellence in Youth Mental Health at the Douglas Hospital.

“This is a first, modest-size study, and the associations with speech and clinical severity will need to be tested in larger independent samples before we can be confident in them,” Wei said.

If future studies confirm the findings, beta-band activity could potentially serve as a measurable indicator of brain dysfunction associated with schizophrenia. Wei also suggested that the signal may be worth investigating as a future treatment target, although its clinical usefulness remains unproven.

#2528. A hidden bias in human language challenges 70 years of science

Researchers at the University of Vermont have discovered a surprising pattern in human language that challenges a scientific theory widely accepted for more than 70 years. Their findings suggest that the meaning of words is shaped by something more fundamental than emotion: our understanding of power, danger, and order.

The study, published in Science Advances, introduces a new approach called "ousiometrics," which uses mathematical methods to measure the essential meaning of language. Its most striking finding is that the words people use show a consistent and widespread preference for safety over danger.

This hidden safety bias could transform how researchers study language, understand human behavior, and develop artificial intelligence systems that interpret what people say and write.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Safety Bias in Human Language

Since the 1950s, much of the scientific understanding of word meaning has relied on a framework developed through pioneering research by psychologist Charles Osgood and others. Known as the VAD model, it describes meaning through three emotional dimensions: valence (positive vs. negative), arousal (excited vs. calm), and dominance (controlling vs. submissive).

This approach became influential in psychology, linguistics, and artificial intelligence, particularly in systems designed to identify the emotional tone of written language.

However, the University of Vermont researchers found evidence that this long-established model overlooks important aspects of how meaning is organized.

Their analysis examined billions of individual uses of more than 20,000 words across a wide range of real-world texts. Using advanced computational techniques, the team identified a different underlying structure. They found that the three dimensions in the traditional VAD model are not independent of one another, as previously assumed.

Instead, their results indicate that word meaning can be more effectively described through three separate dimensions: power (weak vs. powerful), danger (safe vs. dangerous), and structure (ordered vs. chaotic).

The research, supported by the US National Science Foundation, Google, MassMutual, and other funders, found that this new framework accounted for more than 90% of the variation in measured word meaning. The traditional VAD approach explained approximately 72%.

That improvement could be especially important as large language models, automated content moderation, and other AI technologies increasingly depend on understanding human communication.

Why Human Language Favors Safety Over Danger

One of the study's most unexpected discoveries emerged when the researchers examined how words actually appear in everyday communication.

Across books, news reporting, social media, and spoken language, words associated with safety appeared much more frequently than those associated with danger.

This finding offers a new explanation for a familiar phenomenon called the Pollyanna principle, which describes the tendency for human language to lean toward positive rather than negative words.

For decades, researchers generally interpreted this pattern as evidence that people favor positive emotional expressions. The new analysis suggests that something more fundamental may be responsible.

According to the researchers, the apparent preference for positivity largely reflects an underlying preference for safety.

"The Pollyanna principle's positivity bias," the study concludes. "is, in fact, a one-dimensional projection of an underlying safety bias."

"This is a big observation that comes out of this work," said Peter Dodds, director of the UVM's Complex Systems Institute and senior author of the study. "Expressions of safety are crucial to all language."

Could Human Language Be Shaped by Survival?

The discovery raises an intriguing possibility: the structure of human language may reflect evolutionary pressures associated with staying alive.

From this perspective, words do more than communicate feelings. They also help people recognize threats, evaluate uncertainty, establish trust, and coordinate actions with others.

Being able to distinguish between safety and danger is essential to survival. The researchers suggest that this basic human priority may also influence how meaning is organized in language.

Throughout daily life, people constantly exchange information about whether individuals, places, situations, and behaviors are threatening or safe. The new findings suggest that these distinctions may be more central to communication than previously recognized.

They also offer a different interpretation of why positive language is so common. Rather than simply expressing happiness or approval, many positive words may convey a sense of security, stability, or predictability.

Julia Zimmerman, a postdoctoral researcher in UVM's Computational Story Lab and a co-author of the study, said the three newly identified dimensions reflect experiences that are fundamental to human life.

"Power, danger, and structure," she said, "are relevant to every person that's ever lived."

Researchers have also long recognized that people tend to favor language associated with goodness and low aggression. The team argues that these familiar tendencies may arise from the same deeper pattern.

"We now understand," the team writes, that these are "shadows of an underlying linguistic safety bias."

Although the results are consistent with a possible connection between language and survival, they do not establish that evolutionary pressures directly caused the safety bias.

A Discovery That Could Change How AI Understands Language

The findings have implications that extend well beyond linguistics.

Many artificial intelligence systems use sentiment analysis to determine whether language expresses positive, negative, or neutral feelings. Some approaches also draw on the traditional VAD framework to measure emotional meaning.

If that framework does not fully capture the underlying structure of language, systems built around it could overlook important distinctions in what people communicate.

The researchers suggest that incorporating power, danger, and structure could eventually help AI models interpret language more accurately and explain their assessments more clearly. This may be particularly useful when analyzing trust, risk, and decision-making.

In linguistics, the findings offer a different way to understand the foundations of meaning. Instead of treating emotional tone as the primary organizing principle, the new framework emphasizes distinctions related to strength, safety, and predictability.

Psychology could also be affected. Decades of studies have used the VAD model to investigate emotions, perception, and behavior. A different set of fundamental dimensions could encourage researchers to reconsider how some of those findings are interpreted.

The discovery may also interest neuroscientists. The human brain is highly sensitive to threats and signals of safety, and the newly identified linguistic bias suggests that these biological priorities may be reflected in language. This potential connection could help researchers explore relationships between brain activity and symbolic communication, although further research would be needed to establish them.

A New Tool Measures the Hidden Meaning of Words

To investigate these patterns, the researchers created analytical methods designed to measure meaning across enormous collections of written and spoken language.

One of their central innovations is the "ousiometer," a tool that rapidly evaluates the essential meaning of large bodies of text and produces an average meaning score.

(The word "ouisa" comes from Ancient Greek and is a root for the English word "essence.")

The ousiometer builds on an earlier tool developed by the research team called the "hedonometer" (a happiness meter). While that instrument measures the overall emotional tone of language, the new approach examines broader patterns of meaning.

The researchers demonstrated that the ousiometer can analyze material from remarkably different sources, including Jane Austen's novels, Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories, the New York Times, Wikipedia, Twitter, and transcripts of talk radio programs.

The tool can also track how meaning shifts as a story unfolds.

For example, the researchers analyzed an English translation of Victor Hugo's Les Misérables and mapped its changing meaning throughout the novel.

They called this changing pattern an "ousiometric trajectory." The visualization resembles the twisting shape of a multicolored protein, with the narrative moving across a grid based on four contrasting pairs: dangerous/safe, weak/powerful, gentle/aggressive, and bad/good.

By following this path, researchers can observe how the underlying meaning of the text changes from one section of the story to another.

Why Counting Word Frequency Changes the Results

Another important innovation involved distinguishing between words themselves and how often people actually use them.

In linguistics, researchers describe individual words as categories called "types," while each appearance of a word in text is known as a "token."

(For example, as a category "apple" is a type and every time the word "apple" is used in a sentence is a token.)

This distinction matters because a word that appears millions of times contributes much more to everyday communication than one that is rarely used.

Earlier research often gave each word equal importance, regardless of its frequency. The Vermont team instead incorporated how often words appear in real communication, allowing patterns to emerge that might otherwise remain hidden.

This approach was particularly important for identifying the widespread preference for safety-related language.

The ten-member research team was led by Peter Dodds and Chris Danforth, professors in UVM's College of Engineering and Mathematical Sciences. Their collaborators included researchers from the Santa Fe Institute, the Complexity Science Hub in Austria, Howard Hughes Medical Center, the University of California, Berkeley, the University of Adelaide, and MassMutual Data Science.

Rethinking What Human Language Reveals About Us

Understanding this hidden safety bias could have consequences for how researchers study communication across society.

If words consistently reflect a preference for safety, that tendency may influence how stories are told, how information spreads, and how people interpret events and experiences.

The findings could eventually inform research into political communication, mental health messaging, and AI systems designed to interact with people.

More broadly, the study challenges the idea that human language can be understood primarily through emotions such as happiness, fear, and excitement.

Instead, the researchers propose that meaning has a deeper structure connected to power, danger, and order. Their findings suggest that language may reflect the challenges humans face when navigating uncertain environments, maintaining relationships, and identifying threats.

By revealing this previously overlooked structure, the team offers a new way to understand what words communicate and why certain kinds of meaning dominate everyday conversation. Human language, their research suggests, may be more closely connected to our fundamental need for safety than scientists have traditionally recognized.

#2529. Scientists Identify a New Type of Depression That May Feel Like Permanent Jet Lag

A new study suggests nearly one in four depression patients may experience chronic body clock disruption.

For some people with depression, every day feels like waking up with jet lag but without the flight. They sleep longer, wake exhausted, and find themselves most alert when the rest of the world is winding down. Their problem may not simply be how much they sleep or how poorly, but when their internal clock tells them to.

In an analysis of 11,380 Australians with a history of depression, researchers identified 2,604 people, or 23 percent, whose sleep habits and symptoms fit a proposed new subtype of depression called circadian depression. The patients developed depression younger, experienced more severe symptoms, and found common antidepressants less helpful.

In other words, a large portion of the population may experience depression due to their body’s internal clock, which perhaps is not in sync with the tune of modern life, rather than due to anxiety.

Our internal timekeeping systems, or circadian clocks, respond to light and help regulate sleep, appetite, alertness and mood.

“When it’s off, you feel jet-lagged all the time,” Ian Hickie, a University of Sydney psychiatrist and senior author of the study, told ScienceAlert.

Depression can look strikingly different from one patient to another. Some cannot sleep; others sleep excessively. Some lose their appetite; others gain weight. Yet psychiatrists often begin treatment with similar medications, sometimes trying several before finding one that helps.

Only about 30% of patients experience full relief from their very first antidepressant medication. Around one-third of people do not respond to standard antidepressant drugs overall.

Researchers at the University of Sydney wanted to investigate whether a subset of patients might share a particular vulnerability owed to disruption of their internal clocks.

That suggests the two groups are not interchangeable, despite the overlap sometimes.

Previously, research linked evening chronotypes to poorer antidepressant responses. Separately, clinical trials have suggested that bright-light therapy can help some people with depression. “Because circadian depression is linked to people’s internal body clock, treatments targeting the circadian rhythm, such as light therapy and melatonin, may be much more relevant, and clinicians should reach for these much earlier in people with this presentation,” he said. But no trial has yet shown that people selected by these six criteria benefit more from such treatments

“That means these genetic associations do not independently establish a distinct biological subtype of depression,” Ronan Lordan of the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland told the Science Media Centre.

The practical lesson is not to replace antidepressants with melatonin or a light box. It is to take sleep timing seriously when assessing depression, particularly when treatment fails, and to discuss possible interventions with a clinician.

#2530. Scientists Discover That Ovaries May Be Doing Something Unexpected Decades After Menopause

Ovaries often dismissed as inactive after menopause may still shape how women age, with human tissue revealing inflammatory changes and molecular similarities to ovarian cancer.

Ovaries continue to change for decades after menopause, long after their role in reproduction has ended. In a study of ovarian tissue from 28 women ages 50 to 75, scientists found age-related shifts in proteins associated with inflammation, immune activity, and the structure of the tissue surrounding cells.

The research, conducted by scientists at Northwestern University and the Buck Institute for Research on Aging, was published in Aging Cell.

“In the ovary, we reach a point of no return with respect to fertility in which it’s no longer producing eggs, and I would venture to say most people assume those ovaries are just sitting there doing nothing, similar to the appendix,” said co-corresponding author Francesca Duncan, associate professor of reproductive science in medicine at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine. “But that’s what makes this study very different.”

To investigate what changes after menopause, the researchers looked beyond ovarian hormones to examine the tissue’s protein composition. Co-corresponding author Birgit Schilling, professor and director of the Mass Spectrometry Center at the Buck Institute, led the team responsible for that analysis. Spearheaded by Buck Institute staff scientist and Schilling lab member Mark Watson, the work used advanced proteomic technologies that can identify and measure thousands of proteins at once. The researchers analyzed 5,812 groups of proteins in normal ovarian tissue.

Human ovarian tissue is difficult to obtain, making the collection of 28 ovaries unusually valuable, Duncan said. Concentrating on a 25-year age range after menopause allowed the team to isolate differences within the postmenopausal years, rather than comparing young and old ovaries across an entire lifespan. The technology also made it possible to examine thousands of proteins in every sample.

“When we analyzed the data in multiple different statistical ways, what became clear across all these methodologies is that the ovary was not stagnant,” Schilling said. “In fact, over the decades, there were quite dramatic differences in terms of some proteins’ levels would go up with age and some proteins’ levels would go down, which tells us this organ is quite dynamic.”

Ovaries from younger postmenopausal women contained more proteins involved in maintaining cells and tissue structure. Older ovaries had more proteins associated with inflammation, immune activity, and the breakdown and rebuilding of the tissue around cells. The differences were greatest between women in their 50s and those ages 70 and older.

Some of the molecular patterns also resembled those seen in cellular senescence. In this state, aging or damaged cells stop dividing but remain biologically active and can send signals that affect nearby tissue.

“Our data points to progressive aging features in which the ovary appears to lose its original identity and ends up doing something different,” Duncan said. “We do not yet know if there’s ever a point in a woman’s life when this organ is no longer beneficial and it’s actually harmful. These results really point to the need to ask this question.”

Duncan’s group had previously found similar evidence of continued remodeling in postmenopausal ovaries of mice. She began both that research and the human study with expectations that the findings ultimately challenged.

“I was predicting that if we took a 50-year-old ovary, a 60-year-old ovary, a 70-year-old, an 80-year-old ovary, they would all look exactly the same, have no eggs, and basically be non-functional,” Duncan said. “The fact that across species we’re seeing this gives us confidence that this isn’t just some random event, but something truly meaningful is happening in the biology.”

Women now routinely live for decades after menopause, Duncan noted, making the ovary’s role during this long period increasingly relevant to understanding women’s health beyond fertility.

“The ovary is not just a reproductive organ,” Schilling said. “It really influences how somebody ages because it speaks to all these other organs, such as the brain, and also to the bone, and women after menopause experience often a lot of bone problems. So, we think there is a connection between all these organs.”

The changes in tissue structure also connect the new findings to an earlier question about ovarian cancer. In 2016, Duncan’s lab was the first to find that aging ovaries become increasingly inflamed, fibrotic, and stiff, resembling scarring in other tissues. Cancer cells can thrive in stiff, collagen-rich environments, prompting the researchers to investigate whether these changes could make older ovaries more hospitable to cancer cells. The new protein analysis provides a molecular view of that remodeling process after menopause.

All the tissue in the current study appeared normal and cancer-free. Nevertheless, some proteins previously associated with ovarian cancer were more abundant in aging ovaries, with the strongest signal among women in their 60s. This does not mean the ovaries were cancerous or precancerous. It suggests that healthy ovarian aging and ovarian cancer share some biological processes.

“It’s not that all postmenopausal women get ovarian cancer, but we know that that fibrotic inflammatory environment is certainly an environment that cancer cells prefer to home in on,” Duncan said. “This is all consistent with the idea that the aging ovary is a potential third hit for ovarian cancer, with the first hit being genetic and the second hit being environmental. So can we target the aging ovary therapeutically to lower the risk of ovarian cancer, or can we develop novel biomarkers of ovarian cancer?”

Whether aging in the ovary can drive changes elsewhere in the body remains an open question.

“Is the ovary the canary in the coal mine in women?” said Duncan, who is also an associate professor in residence at the Buck Institute. “Could aging in the ovary set off a cascade of changes that continues throughout the body as women get older?”

The findings do not answer whether healthy ovaries should ever be removed after menopause, the researchers emphasized. Much more research is needed to determine exactly what the organ does during these years and whether the changes observed contribute to chronic disease or are simply features of normal aging.

#2537. What Did Denisovans Look Like? Scientists Have Their Best Answer Yet

Ancient proteins are helping scientists put a face to a long-lost branch of humanity.

For years, we’ve mostly known this mysterious human lineage from fossil bits and pieces. Scientists have found fossilized bones, skull parts, they’ve even found traces of their genes in people alive today. But what did the Denisovans look like?

Now, a new study is finally bringing their appearance into focus. By analyzing ancient proteins preserved in fossils from China, researchers have identified more Denisovan remains and pieced together a remarkably detailed picture of their features including massive brow ridges, sloping foreheads, and broad, relatively flat faces.

The findings also suggest something surprising: several ancient human groups that scientists thought were distinct may have been Denisovans all along.

The Denisovan story began in a cave in Siberia. In 2010, scientists analyzed DNA recovered from a tiny finger bone and discovered that it belonged to a previously unknown group of ancient humans. They named them after Denisova Cave, where the bone was found.

Denisovans were close relatives of Neanderthals. They had also interbred with our species, leaving traces of their ancestry in people alive today.

Unlike Neanderthals, whose skeletons have been found at numerous sites, Denisovans were initially known from only a handful of fragments. Scientists could read their genetic history more easily than they could picture their faces.

That began to change in 2025, when researchers showed that a nearly complete skull from Harbin, northeastern China, belonged to a Denisovan. The fossil, nicknamed “Dragon Man” and previously described as Homo longi, offered a rare look at the shape of a Denisovan head.

The latest discovery adds another crucial piece.

At Xujiayao in northern China, archaeologists excavated 20 human fossil specimens between 1976 and 1979. The roughly 199,000–138,000-year-old remains included skull and jaw fragments whose place on the human family tree remained disputed.

The DNA didn’t survive. But the new team, led by researchers in China, turned to something else: proteins preserved inside bone.

They recovered 18 ancient proteins from two fossils. In both specimens, they found a distinctive version of a collagen protein previously identified in confirmed Denisovans, but not in Neanderthals or modern humans. A separate analysis of the protein sequences also grouped the Xujiayao fossils with Denisovans.

Taken together, the Xujiayao remains and the Harbin skull suggest a few traits. Denisovans had long, low skulls, heavy brow ridges and foreheads that sloped backward. Their faces were relatively broad and flat through the cheeks, with little forward projection around the mouth. Their nasal openings were broad, and their skull bones thick.

So, they weren’t just Neanderthals living farther east. They shared some features with Neanderthals but differed in the back of the skull and parts of the jaw. Some features also resembled the much older Homo erectus, while others looked surprisingly modern.

Of course, these facial reconstructions can never be perfect; there’s a lot of information you can’t derive from bones, DNA, and proteins. For example, we can only infer their skin tone, hairstyle or exact facial appearance. Any lifelike image necessarily involves artistic interpretation. But it’s the first time we have a more complete idea of what Denisovans looked like.

“We are finally starting to put a face to the Denisovan,” says Sally Wasef, a palaeogeneticist at the Queensland University of Technology in Brisbane, Australia, for Nature.

The bones now reveal far more than scientists could have inferred from the original Siberian finger bone. And they may explain another longstanding mystery.

For decades, researchers encountered ancient human fossils across China that seemed to fit nowhere neatly. Some were classified as early modern humans. Others were treated as Neanderthal relatives or members of separate species.

The Xujiayao fossils themselves were recently placed in a proposed species called Homo juluensis, partly because of their enormous estimated braincase capacity of about 1,700 cubic centimeters. Meanwhile, the Harbin skull had been assigned to Homo longi.

The new study argues that differences such as skull size may not justify dividing these fossils into separate evolutionary branches. After comparing the bones from these regions, the scientists suggest we’re simply dealing with a varied Denisovan lineage.

This is still far from a settled issue, and the conclusion is stronger for the specimens identified through proteins or DNA than for those linked only by resemblance. But this would explain why scientists just weren’t finding any Denisovan remains; they were, but they didn’t realize they’re Denisovans.

The accumulating discoveries also suggest that Denisovans were remarkably adaptable.

Fossils and molecular evidence now place them across a huge swath of Asia, from Siberia to the Tibetan Plateau, northeastern China and as far south as the Taiwan Strait. The authors suggest they may have occupied parts of East Asia over hundreds of thousands of years, across radically different environments.

They weren’t a small group confined to a Siberian cave. They were a widespread part of humanity’s history.

And their story didn’t end entirely with extinction. Ancient interbreeding left Denisovan DNA in modern humans, particularly among populations in Oceania and parts of Asia. One inherited Denisovan-related genetic variant even helps some Tibetans cope with oxygen-poor air at high altitudes.

We still can’t know exactly what Denisovans were like. But scientists are no longer working with a faceless genome.

A population once revealed by a tiny finger bone is gradually emerging as a group of real people, with distinctive faces, varied lives and a place in our own ancestry. Real people who are now a part of our own genetic heritage.

#2538. Astronomers may have discovered the first moon beyond our Solar System

Astronomers may have found the first moon beyond our Solar System, but the mysterious object is unlike any moon we know. Observations from the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope (ESO's VLT) have uncovered evidence of an enormous moon-like body in the CD-35 2722 star system. Rather than orbiting a planet, this unusual object circles a brown dwarf, a celestial body more massive than a planet but not massive enough to become a star.

If confirmed, the discovery could mark the first detection of a natural satellite outside our Solar System, potentially changing how astronomers define moons and planets.

Kevin Hoy, an ESO student based in Chile and lead author of the study published in Nature, spent months examining the CD-35 2722 system. He describes its unusual arrangement as "super weird" compared with our own Solar System.

At the center of this relatively young system is CD-35 2722, a star with approximately half the Sun's mass. Orbiting the star is a brown dwarf, an object that occupies the murky boundary between planets and stars. Brown dwarfs are generally too small to sustain the hydrogen fusion that powers ordinary stars, yet they are more massive than planets.

The newly identified object orbits this brown dwarf, creating a remarkable arrangement in which a massive satellite circles an object that itself revolves around a star.

"This system is somewhat hard to define using Solar-System-based words like 'planet' and 'moon'," states Hoy, who is also affiliated with the Universidad Diego Portales and the Millennium Nucleus of Young Exoplanets and their Moons (YEMS) in Chile.

The researchers refer to the newly detected object as an exosatellite. It has at least as much mass as Jupiter, while the brown dwarf it orbits is more than 30 times as massive as Jupiter. This makes the possible moon far more massive than any natural satellite in our Solar System.

"The exosatellite is clearly massive enough to be a planet, but it does not orbit a star, though it orbits an object that orbits a star," says Hoy. "Being the third wheel in this system makes us want to call it a moon, even if it is nothing like the small, rocky moons we have in our system."

A Giant Exomoon That Challenges Cosmic Definitions

The unusual discovery highlights a fundamental problem with applying familiar astronomical labels to objects found in distant star systems.

An exosatellite is a natural satellite located beyond our Solar System, while the term exomoon generally refers to a natural satellite orbiting a planet or another celestial body outside our Solar System. However, astronomers have not established a universally accepted definition of an exomoon.

That distinction becomes especially important in the CD-35 2722 system, where the possible moon is itself massive enough to be considered a planet under some classification schemes.

Alice Zurlo, director of YEMS and a collaborator on the study, explains: "The satellite we report is a giant gaseous body orbiting a highly massive companion, itself several times the mass of Jupiter."

The arrangement is very different from the familiar structure of our Solar System, where planets orbit the Sun and moons orbit planets.

"We have a clear delineation between the planets and the Sun in the Solar System, so defining things like moons is simple. In the CD-35 2722 system, where we are blurring the lines between stars, planets, and moons, the whole thing becomes more complicated to describe," adds Zurlo, who is also an astrophysicist at Universidad Diego Portales.

Scientists Search for the First Confirmed Exomoon

Astronomers have spent years looking for moons around objects beyond our Solar System, but no candidate has yet been detected with enough certainty to establish a confirmed discovery.

That makes the latest findings particularly significant. Although scientists have identified more than 6,000 exoplanets, only a handful of possible exomoons have emerged from observations, and the evidence supporting them remains limited.

Earlier in 2026, a research team led by Quentin Kral reported possible signs of a satellite in the HD 206893 star system. Their observations, obtained with ESO's Very Large Telescope Interferometer, suggested that a moon-like companion might exist, but they did not establish a definitive detection.

The CD-35 2722 system now offers another promising opportunity, supported by measurements of how the suspected moon affects the motion of its larger companion.

How Astronomers Detected the Possible Alien Moon

To investigate CD-35 2722, Hoy, Zurlo, and their colleagues used the CRIRES+ instrument installed on ESO's Very Large Telescope.

The team applied a technique known as the radial velocity method, which was also used to discover the first exoplanet orbiting a Sun-like star.

This approach works by measuring extremely small changes in an object's motion. When two celestial bodies orbit one another, their mutual gravitational attraction causes both to move around a shared center of mass. Even a much smaller companion can produce a detectable wobble in the larger object's motion.

By examining subtle changes in the brown dwarf's motion, the researchers identified what they consider strong evidence that another massive body is orbiting it.

"As exotic as it is, this system is truly unique and represents a breakthrough: the first plausible detection of an exosatellite," says Zurlo.

Confirming moons beyond our Solar System would do more than add another category of objects to astronomical catalogs. These discoveries could help researchers understand how planetary systems form, how their components interact, and why some systems develop structures dramatically different from our own.

Future observations could also reveal whether massive satellites like the one proposed in CD-35 2722 are rare exceptions or part of a much broader population of unusual celestial objects.

ESO's upcoming Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) is expected to play a major role in that search. Equipped with an enormous 39-meter mirror and advanced scientific instruments, the telescope will give astronomers the ability to detect smaller exomoons that are currently beyond the reach of existing observatories.

As scientists begin identifying more satellites in distant planetary systems, they may discover that familiar distinctions between stars, planets, and moons are not as universal as once assumed.

A satellite is any object that travels around another object. Satellites can be natural (like our own moon) or artificial (like a spacecraft).

An exosatellite is a satellite located outside our Solar System. The term exomoon generally describes a natural satellite orbiting a planet or another object beyond our Solar System, although astronomers have not agreed on an official definition.

The possible companion in CD-35 2722 illustrates why that definition matters. With a mass comparable to or greater than Jupiter's, it resembles a planet in size and composition, yet its orbital relationship with the brown dwarf makes it a candidate for an entirely different classification.

#2545. Scientists Turned Shiitake Fungi Into Experimental Computer Memory

Your next computer might grow in a compost heap.

You can grow a computer component on your kitchen counter. Well, sort of. That’s the radical implication of 2025 research from The Ohio State University, where scientists used shiitake fungi to make experimental electronic devices that can remember information.

In their study, research scientist in psychiatry John LaRocco and colleagues describe a fungus-based memory system intended for brain-inspired computing.

“Being able to develop microchips that mimic actual neural activity means you don’t need a lot of power for standby or when the machine isn’t being used,” said LaRocco in an October 2025 university release. “That’s something that can be a huge potential computational and economic advantage.”

Memristors — short for “memory resistors” — are electronic components whose resistance depends on their electrical history. This makes them useful candidates for neuromorphic computing, which takes inspiration from the brain. Traditional versions are made of silicon or metal oxides, using specialized fabrication processes. The Ohio State team decided to explore whether fungi could offer an alternative. Because why not?

Specifically, they turned to shiitake mushrooms (Lentinula edodes), known for their resilience and peculiar sensitivity to electrical stimuli. So, the researchers grew the fungi in Petri dishes filled with farro, wheat germ, and hay until the mycelium formed a dense white mat. Then they dried the samples in sunlight, rehydrated them just enough to restore conductivity, and connected them to an Arduino-powered circuit. What they got was a memristor — a sort of tiny bioelectronic brain cell — that could remember and respond to electrical patterns.

“We would connect electrical wires and probes at different points on the mushrooms because distinct parts of it have different electrical properties,” said LaRocco. “Depending on the voltage and connectivity, we were seeing different performances.”

The team reported roughly 90% accuracy in memory tests at up to 5,850 cycles per second. A separate test found a 95% match to ideal memristor behavior at 10 cycles per second and 1 volt. These percentages measure different things; neither establishes parity with commercial computer memory.

This isn’t the first time scientists have tried to merge mushrooms and machines. Mycologists and engineers have long been fascinated by mycelium, the network of filaments that makes up much of a fungus. Researchers are exploring how its electrical responses might serve both sensing and computing.

#2547. NASA’s asteroid Bennu samples reveal a 4.5-billion-year-old secret

Samples collected from asteroid Bennu by NASA are revealing surprising new details about how our Solar System formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago. A new chemical analysis challenges earlier theories about the asteroid's birthplace and suggests that Jupiter played an important role in shaping the materials from which Bennu and other ancient objects formed.

Bennu completes one orbit around the Sun every 1.2 years and makes a relatively close approach to Earth about every six years, coming within roughly 300,000 kilometers (186,000 miles). NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission traveled to the asteroid, collected material from its surface, and successfully delivered the samples to Earth in 2023.

On September 23, 2023, a capsule carrying approximately 120 grams of asteroid material landed in the Utah desert. Scientists around the world subsequently received small portions of the precious cargo for detailed study.

Among them was Maria Schönbächler, Professor of Isotope Geochemistry at ETH Zurich, whose laboratory received half a gram of Bennu material. Her team immediately began examining the samples for clues about the asteroid's history.

The research, now published in Science Advances, has uncovered a distinctive chemical signature that not only helps identify Bennu's origins but also provides new evidence about the processes that shaped the early Solar System.

Bennu's Chemical Fingerprint Reveals Its Cosmic Relatives

To investigate Bennu's origins, the ETH Zurich researchers examined isotopes of three elements: iron, titanium, and chromium.

Isotopes are different forms of the same chemical element that contain varying numbers of neutrons, giving them slightly different masses. By measuring the relative amounts of these isotopes, scientists can identify a material's chemical fingerprint. These patterns provide clues about where an asteroid's ingredients originated and, in some cases, when they formed.

The analysis revealed that iron and titanium are distributed remarkably evenly throughout Bennu's material. It also uncovered a close chemical relationship between Bennu, the asteroid Ryugu, and a rare group of primitive meteorites known as CI meteorites.

CI meteorites are carbon-rich remnants of the early Solar System that have undergone relatively little alteration over billions of years. They are exceptionally uncommon among meteorites recovered on Earth.

Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites share strikingly similar isotopic fingerprints, suggesting that their original materials came from the same reservoir of ancient cosmic dust. Their chemical signatures also differ substantially from those of other known asteroids, meteorite groups, and planets.

These similarities offer an important clue about where these objects formed and what conditions existed during the Solar System's earliest stages.

Scientists Discover Bennu's Unexpected Birthplace

For years, researchers generally believed that primitive asteroids like Bennu originated in the outer Solar System, perhaps in the same distant regions where comets formed. They also suspected that Bennu's original material came together relatively late in the Solar System's development.

The new findings challenge both assumptions.

Instead, the researchers propose that the material that became Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites accumulated near a boundary known as the water-ice line.

This boundary marks the region in the disk surrounding the young Sun where temperatures were low enough for water vapor to freeze into ice. Around 4.5 billion years ago, it may have been a particularly important meeting place for material traveling from different parts of the developing Solar System.

Dust originating in both the inner and outer regions mixed near this boundary. Water ice helped the smallest particles stick together, allowing them to gradually accumulate into larger bodies.

"Bennu is a hybrid: the material does not clearly match either the inner or the outer Solar System," says Schönbächler.

Its unusual chemical composition suggests that Bennu formed from a combination of materials originating in both regions rather than from a single, isolated source.

Jupiter May Have Played a Crucial Role in Bennu's Formation

The researchers also developed a scenario explaining how this mixing could have occurred, with Jupiter emerging as a key player.

Jupiter began forming extraordinarily early in Solar System history, within approximately one million years of the Sun's birth. At that time, the young Sun was surrounded by a rotating disk of gas, dust, and ice.

Within this disk, countless small particles gradually collided and stuck together, building larger objects through a process called accretion. Over time, some of these growing bodies became asteroids and planets.

As Jupiter rapidly increased in mass, its immense gravitational influence began reshaping the distribution of material throughout the disk.

The researchers suggest that the growing gas giant acted somewhat like a structural barrier, preventing most larger particles from crossing its orbital region while still allowing finer dust to move around it.

This fine material, originating from different parts of the disk, could then mix extensively near the water-ice line.

According to the proposed scenario, the parent bodies of Bennu and Ryugu, along with the objects that produced CI meteorites, formed from this thoroughly mixed material.

Jupiter may therefore have helped determine not only where these ancient asteroids formed but also which ingredients became incorporated into them.

Ancient Dust and Ice Reveal the Ingredients That Built Earth

The proposed formation scenario could also explain two of Bennu's most intriguing characteristics: its abundance of water-bearing material and its chemical resemblance to the Sun.

Near the water-ice boundary, ice could evaporate as temperatures changed, releasing water vapor that later condensed again in cooler regions. This process would have helped concentrate water in the material from which Bennu formed.

Meanwhile, Jupiter's influence may have limited the movement of larger particles, leaving the asteroid's parent material dominated by fine dust.

Because these tiny particles could circulate and mix efficiently throughout the disk, they preserved a chemical composition that closely resembles the overall elemental mixture found in the Sun.

The process is somewhat similar to the way fine household dust spreads and settles across many different surfaces rather than remaining in one location.

This extensive mixing could explain why Bennu's material retains such a representative sample of the ingredients present during the Solar System's earliest history.

"Bennu may offer our best glimpse of the original mix of chemical elements from which the terrestrial planets were ultimately built," as Schönbächler states.

Bennu is considered an exceptionally primitive asteroid because its material formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago and has experienced relatively little chemical alteration since then.

That makes its samples particularly valuable for reconstructing the conditions that existed before Earth and the other rocky planets finished forming.

By studying the asteroid's chemistry, scientists can better understand how the Solar System developed and which processes determined the composition of its planets.

Bennu's water-bearing minerals and organic compounds also make it important for investigating how early Earth obtained some of the chemical ingredients necessary for life.

Although these findings do not establish exactly how those materials reached our planet, they provide valuable evidence about where such ingredients originated and how they were distributed throughout the young Solar System.

A New Mission to Mars' Moon Could Reveal More

Despite the new discoveries, several questions remain unanswered.

"We are now wondering whether other asteroids have the same isotopic signature as Bennu and Ryugu," Schönbächler goes on to state.

Researchers also want to determine more precisely how Jupiter influenced the movement of dust and whether its gravitational effects were responsible for favoring the accumulation of particularly fine particles.

Additional asteroid samples could help scientists test the proposed formation scenario and establish whether Bennu's chemical signature is shared by other ancient bodies.

With the Bennu analysis now complete, Schönbächler is looking ahead to another opportunity to examine material from the early Solar System.

Japan's space agency, JAXA, is preparing a sample-return mission to Phobos, one of Mars' two moons. The mission is scheduled to launch at the end of October 2026, with the goal of collecting material from the Martian moon and bringing it back to Earth.

"It would be exciting to also obtain this material to analyse it in my laboratory," she says.

Schönbächler plans to apply to JAXA for access to the samples. However, the scientific community will need to be patient. The capsule carrying material from Phobos is not expected to return to Earth until 2031.

In CSS, adjacent margins can sometimes overlap. This is known as “margin collapse”, and it has a reputation for being quite dastardly.

The good news is that once we understand the rules behind this notoriously-confusing mechanism, it becomes a lot clearer, and a lot less surprising ✨.

In this tutorial, we're going to dive deep into the details and figure it out. No more being bewildered!

When margin-collapse was added to the CSS specification, the language designers made a curious choice: horizontal margins shouldn't collapse.

In the early days, CSS wasn't intended to be used for layouts. The people writing the spec were imagining headings and paragraphs, not columns and sidebars.

So that's our first rule: only vertical margins collapse.

Negative margins allow us to reduce the space between two elements. It lets us pull a child outside its parent's bounding box, or reduce the space between siblings until they overlap.

#2549. Why does rain smell so good?

Have you ever been outside on a warm day after it rained and wondered what the lovely, earthy smell is? The smell is created by a compound called geosmin, a by-product of soil-dwelling actinobacteria. The smell was given the name petrichor in 1964. It’s not the smell of rain falling. It’s the smell of the earth coming back to life after a dry spell.

As the ground dries, actinobacteria produce spores, generating geosmin in the process. The human nose is extremely sensitive to geosmin and can detect it at concentrations as low as 5 parts per trillion!

When the weather is dry for a period of time, plants release oils into the soil around them. The oils stop roots from growing and prevent neighbouring seeds from germinating, reducing competition for nutrients in tough times.

When it rains on dry ground, tiny bubbles of air are trapped underneath the rain. They rise through the water droplets and burst at the surface, releasing a fine mist of plant oils and earthy scents into the air. We recognise these scents as Petrichor!

You can see the release of tiny air bubbles after rain hits the ground in this video from MIT.

Geosmin is a metabolic by-product produced by soil microorganisms such as Streptomyces.

Joy Bear and Richard Grenfell Thomas first used the term petrichor in the science journal Nature.

Humans can smell geosmin at extremely low levels.
