#1451. 9 Unique Apps to Try on the iPhone Duo

Despite seven years of Android folding phones, Apple's iPhone Duo launch has spurred developers to create quirky, weird, and fun apps that experiment with the folding design (leaving Android owners feeling like second-class citizens yet again).

I've reviewed almost every folding phone in the past decade, and my inbox has never seen this many app pitches from indie developers wanting to show off their app for Apple's first foldable.

Some of these apps aren't necessarily new but use the phone's hinge to offer fresh experiences. You'll also find a “Made for iPhone Duo” badge on apps that make best use of the larger screens on the App Store. From a game that has you closing the phone to grill a piece of toast to a clever way to memorize flash cards, here are nine unique apps to try on your new Apple handset.

Board games will have a field day on the iPhone Duo, and Battlefleet Duo is the best example. On the larger 7.6-inch screen, you get a sprawling canvas, but in tabletop mode, you can view your ships on the bottom screen and the enemy's fleet above. That's especially great if you have a friend with the iPhone Duo, as you can play local multiplayer and sit across from each other just like you would with the old-school Battleships. You can play two free battles every day, or unlock unlimited battles with a one-time $5 purchase—no ads or subscription.

Reminiscing about the long-lost days of the pre-smartphone era? Flipcorder offers simple pleasure, turning your iPhone Duo into a camcorder, so you can make new memories that feel like the old days. Open the app, and the outer display will show a “closed lens”—open the Duo to “take the cap off,” then start recording. The user interface is retro, and video clips have a filter to emulate VHS (it has a MiniDV filter too). I really like that you can view your “tapes” and everything is cataloged by day, so you can see 30 minutes of footage in one stitched-together clip.

As you’re recording, the person on the other end can see a preview of the video on the outer display—that kind of dual-display experience is only available to camera apps, so if you were hoping to read a book and have the outer display appear as the book cover, that's sadly not possible at the moment.

Flashcard apps are a dime a dozen, but nothing quite captures the physical experience of flipping around a flashcard like RectoVerso. This $13 app lets you import your own text—your classroom notes or even a PDF—and uses Apple Intelligence to automatically create flashcards on the subject matter. Alternatively, you can import decks from Anki, another popular flashcard app, or write your own. The magic is when you're actually studying. Don't know the answer? Slightly open the iPhone Duo, and the answer will gradually appear—fully open, and you get the full answer. That way you can bang your hand on the desk and say, “Ah, I totally knew that.”

Who doesn't love a rhythm game? Pop your iPhone Duo into a 90-degree angle and let the blocks in PianoFold reach the middle crease—that's when you tap the corresponding column. You need to time your taps perfectly, unless you enjoy listening to Bach get butchered in real time. There are 40 levels across eight chapters, and the game rates your performance (out of three stars) at the end of each song. You can retry as many times as you'd like. The first two chapters are free, though you can also play one song for free every day. For full access, you'll have to pony up $5. There are no ads and no subscription.

When I showed my wife Toastie, she remarked that it might be the first time she ever wanted an iPhone (she uses a Pixel). That's high praise! This delightful $3 app asks you to construct a sandwich on the iPhone Duo's inner screen. Each recipe has specific instructions you have to follow, and when you're done, you'll need to close the iPhone for a few seconds—however many the recipe says—to “grill” your sandwich. Open the phone back up, and it's ready to serve! That is, if you haven't burned it. There are only 10 recipes, so you'll probably get through this quickly. But hey, at least you can say you've used the world's most expensive toaster.

Want to focus? Or relax? Portal lets you play relaxing soundscapes—listen to a volcano's bubbling lava or waves crashing on a beach—but you can also see these sights on the Duo's 7.6-inch canvas, in tabletop mode, or on the outer screen in tent mode. It's very relaxing and an excellent companion for travelers who rely on a sound machine to fall asleep. You can also follow calming breath exercises, or set focus timers to get stuff done. The app boasts more than 100 vistas, from Iceland's black beaches to enchanting UK woodlands. The app is free, though you can only access three portals refreshed monthly. Full access costs $3 a month, $30 per year, or $100 for a lifetime membership.

If you like the idea of Portal, you might want to check out Fold Focus, though it's designed to help you get work done rather than relax. Close the Duo to focus on a task, and the pixel animation on the outer display will start building a city. Open your phone before the timer ends and everything will come crashing down.

Want to try a new calendar app? Maybe your current one isn't optimized for the Duo (ahem, Google Calendar). Try FirstSeed Planner. The developer has fully enabled Duo support, letting you view the calendar on one screen and a daily planner on the other. It supports drag-and-drop actions between the two halves of the screen, and I also love the space at the bottom for writing notes for the day. And when Apple enables stylus support on the Duo later this year, you can handwrite in FirstSeed, making your Duo feel more like a paper planner. It's free, but the Pro version costs $3.49 per month, $30 per year, or $90 for lifetime access. The paid versions unlock features like automatic backups and weather forecasts.

If you’re big on retro gaming, you’ve probably heard of Delta. The popular emulator just rolled out its biggest update yet, headlined by support for the folding iPhone Duo. Prop the Duo at a 90-degree angle, and custom skins transform the device into a classic clamshell like the Nintendo DS—complete with automatic sleep mode when you close it. Just remember: It’s strictly an emulator, so you’ll still need to source your own ROMs responsibly.

Bring your favorite PCs and Macs back to life with Tiny Computers. This $4 app houses a library of 10 computers, from the Commodore 64 to the iMac G3. Fold the Duo into tabletop mode to bring the museum of machines to life—a vintage screen up top and a functional, clickable keyboard below. The operating systems are interactive, so you can run retro paint apps, secret terminal commands, or kill time with classic desktop games like Solitaire.

#1452. Suspected pneumonic plague in Siberia: What we know about the disease

After a worker at a Russian plague research institute in Siberia died October 2, 2026, nearly 200 of her contacts were under medical observation. But authorities there denied pneumonic plague had caused the death.

UPDATE: Following initial reports raising concerns that a lab worker had died accidentally of pneumonic plague, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) said it was now also "closely monitoring the situation."

However, in a statement published October 6, 2026, the ECDC said that "based on the limited information currently available, there are no reports of secondary cases and no evidence of sustained human-to-human transmission that would cause further spread of the disease."

Following the death of a Russian laboratory worker, there is speculation about an outbreak of pneumonic plague, a highly contagious bacterial infection of the lungs. Russia denies there is a broader risk to the community.

Russian officials have said the laboratory worker's death was not caused by a plague pathogen. The 28-year-old died of "pneumonia of unknown origin," according to Russia's Interfax news agency, citing Rospotrebnadzor, Russia's disease-control agency. According to that agency, samples taken from the woman showed no microorganisms "related to her professional activities." This contradicts earlier information.

Local media had previously reported that the woman, who worked at an institute dedicated to fighting plague, had dropped a test tube.

She reportedly died on Friday, October 2, 2026, at the district hospital in Shelekhov, a town near Irkutsk in Siberia.

According to official Russian figures, authorities placed two hospitals and a children's clinic under quarantine. Local reports said staff at the institute were being quarantined at their workplace. The US news site Axios has reported that around 200 people who had been in contact with the woman had been placed under observation. There were no further reported infections.

Even so, distrust of how Russia handles information persists. On one of DW's Russian-language social media channels, a user wrote: "It wouldn't surprise me if this turns into a disaster now, both in terms of the spread and the quarantine measures."

All that is known for certain, at the time of writing and first publication (October 5, 2026), is that pneumonic plague is highly contagious.

Pneumonic plague is a form of plague that infects the lungs. Like every form of the disease, it is caused by Yersinia pestis, a bacterium usually found in small mammals, such as rodents and in the fleas that feed on them.

The World Health Organization describes the pneumonic form as the most virulent type of plague.

Its incubation period can be as short as 24 hours, and it is the only form that passes easily from person to person through respiratory particles released when a sick patient coughs. This is why, according to the WHO, it can trigger severe epidemics.

Symptoms appear quickly: high fever, headache and weakness, followed by a rapidly worsening pneumonia with shortness of breath, chest pain and a cough that often brings up blood.

A person can catch pneumonic plague by breathing in the bacteria directly, or by developing it when bubonic and/or septicemic plague go untreated and spread to the lungs.

Pneumonic plague is not a separate disease. It is one of three forms of plague: bubonic, septicemic and pneumonic.

What sets them apart is where the infection settles in the body, and that shapes how the disease spreads and how deadly it is.

Bubonic plague is the most common form and the one most people think of when they hear the word "plague." It usually follows a flea bite. The bacteria travel to the nearest lymph node, which swells into a painful lump known as a bubo. According to the WHO, it rarely spreads between people.

If bubonic plague goes untreated, the bacteria can move further through the body. If they spread into the bloodstream, the disease can become septicemic plague, which causes bleeding under the skin, tissue death in the fingers and toes, and organ failure. If the bacteria reach the lungs, the illness becomes pneumonic plague, the form that can pass from person to person through the air. Both septicemic and pneumonic plague can also develop directly, without a bubo appearing first.

Left untreated, bubonic plague kills between 30-60% of patients. The septicemic and pneumonic forms are always fatal without treatment.

Pneumonic plague is curable, but only if treatment starts quickly. The WHO warns that without treatment it can kill within 18 to 24 hours of the first symptoms.

Common antibiotics can cure the infection, and recovery rates are high when patients are treated within about a day of falling ill.

Speed is what decides the outcome. Doctors are advised not to wait for laboratory confirmation and to start antibiotics as soon as plague is suspected, according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

The drugs themselves are not rare or specialized. They are standard, widely used antibiotics, and patients usually need a course of up to two weeks. To stop the infection spreading, patients are kept in isolation, and wearing masks helps protect those around them, the WHO says.

People close to a patient are treated as well, even if they have no symptoms. Both the WHO and the CDC recommend a seven-day course of preventive antibiotics for close contacts of anyone with pneumonic plague.

The WHO does not recommend a plague vaccine for the public. It reserves vaccination for high-risk groups, such as laboratory staff who work with the bacterium and health workers.

You’re about to perform a sensitive action, such as making a payment, changing your password, or sharing your account details. Apple will analyze certain information related to an interaction to generate the risk level, but it never receives the data itself. The company doesn’t analyze your content in such apps as Mail, Messages, and Photos. But Apple does learn the type of action you attempted when an app requests a risk assessment. Impersonation Risk Detection is turned off by default because a supported app requires a certain level of access to your actions and activities.

#1461. Ronaldo stays on under cloud as Messi exits internationals

Just hours before Lionel Messi played his last match for Argentina, Cristiano Ronaldo posted his latest salvo in a row with Portugal. Ronaldo wants to play on despite the troubles, while Messi bathed in adulation.

They are the two players who have defined the last two decades. As football became ever more popular, commercial and dominant in the cultural and sporting landscape, Cristiano Ronaldo and Lionel Messi remained its twin peaks.

Along with that has come endless social media posts, stories and discussions about who is better. Does Messi's World Cup and two Copa America titles with Argentina trump Ronaldo's Euro 2016 win and two Nations League titles? Or does the Portuguese's greater number of goals mean more? How about their club honors?

It is, essentially, a subjective argument and not one that can ever be resolved. It remains to be seen whether that's also true of the public spat between Ronaldo and his Portugal coach Jorge Jesus.

On Tuesday, Messi scored a 126th goal in his 208th game for Argentina. Shortly before he did, Ronaldo posted a 2,000-word statement in which he stopped short of quitting the national team but said there was an "absolute breakdown of trust" between him and the coach.

Ronaldo's comments refer to Portugal's recent match against Norway in which he said he was initially to be rested before being asked to warm up as a substitute without ever being brought on. The striker, 41, walked away from the team's camp for the remainder of the international break, but said the story is not over.

"I can promise all my fans, and naturally especially all Portuguese people, that we will have one or more farewell matches. There will be time for that," said Ronaldo, who plays for Al-Nassr in Saudi Arabia.

"Despite the absolute breach of trust in the coach's word, as anyone who witnessed these conversations can attest, I should not have done it," Ronaldo said. "The responsibility is entirely mine. I apologize to the Portuguese people once again, but the breaking of one's word has, for me, a defining impact.

"I felt that, from that point on, my staying would only harm the group, and I did what I have always said I would do if I didn't feel useful: I picked up my bags and left," he continued before suggesting he accepted there may be a need for the coach or federation to punish him.

It was a long way from the emotional scenes in Rosario, Argentina. Instead of anger and recrimination, the overriding emotions there were gratitude, nostalgia and sadness.

"Not coming anymore is going to be deeply painful. I swear I'd like to play for the rest of my life, because playing for the Argentina national team is the most beautiful thing that can happen to you," Messi, who now plays his club football for Inter Miami in the US, told the 80,000 fans who had packed in to see him score a penalty in a 3-0 win over Benin.

For some, the contrasting evenings offered a neat synopsis of the pair. Ronaldo, less naturally gifted than his counterpart, has made the most of his talents with a singular focus which many believe often makes him prioritize himself over the team. He has had similar fallouts with managers and head coaches before, including Jose Mourinho at Real Madrid, Erik ten Hag at Manchester United and Portugal's 2022 World Cup coach, Fernando Santos.

Ronaldo's much-discussed aim to reach 1,000 career goals is held up by some as further evidence of where his motivations lie.

"You know what my goal is. I want to win trophies, and I want to reach that number (1,000 goals) that you all know. I will reach the number for sure, if no injuries," he said last December. He is currently on 979.

For others, that is simply what is required to be the best. While Messi's quiet character and general refusal to play out his life in public suggest a more humble character focused on helping the team, his genius has undoubtedly changed the teams around him. In the 2026 World Cup, he barely broke into a run but still led an otherwise fairly limited Argentina to the final with eight goals.

There will be no more Messi goals for Argentina and, despite his promises, no absolute assurances that the world will see Ronaldo in a Portugal shirt again. For now, both will head back to their club sides to play out their twilight years away from the intensity of Europe's top leagues. But wherever they go from here, the spotlight will keep following.

#1472. Nobelitis — How winning a Nobel Prize can give you delusions of grandeur

After being chosen for one of the world's most prestigious awards, some Nobel laureates lose touch with reality and suddenly start spouting unscientific nonsense. They're afflicted by what some have called "Nobelitis."

Linus Pauling won not one, but two Nobel Prizes — the first, for chemistry, in 1954 for his research into the nature of the chemical bond, and the Peace Prize in 1962 for his work in the anti-nuclear weapons movement.

The American chemist not only won two Nobel Prizes, but won them on his own, a rare feat for any scientist. Such an honor often turns laureates into global superstars overnight, with millions hanging on their every word.

That instant fame can be dangerous, said Nils Hansson, a professor in the Department of History, Theory and Ethics of Medicine at Heinrich Heine University in Düsseldorf. Especially, he said, when these world-renowned experts "start spreading crazy, unproven scientific theories" — ideas that, when disseminated by Nobel laureates, risk being taken seriously.

That's what happened with Pauling. Beginning in the mid 1960s he began claiming that vitamin C was a universal remedy, a cure for everything from cancer to cardiovascular conditions and degenerative diseases like Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis or osteoarthritis.

Pauling developed an alternative treatment he called orthomolecular medicine, based on specific doses of vitamins and minerals for each patient. To this day, some 60 years later, evidence for his methods remains inconclusive.

Pauling's case is a classic example of "Nobelitis," or "Nobel disease" — not an official diagnosis, but an informal term used to describe how some laureates begin to suffer delusions of grandeur later in life. It was first coined by Paul Nurse, president of The Royal Society, the UK's national academy of sciences, and himself a winner of the Nobel Prize.

"Be skeptical of everything, including yourself and your own ideas," Nurse said in a 2019 interview with KU Leuven University in Belgium, when asked what made someone a good scientist. In cases of Nobelitis, that self-skepticism is the first to go.

That was also the case with virologist Luc Montagnier, who was jointly honored in 2008 for his discovery of the human immunodeficiency virus, HIV, with colleague Francoise Barre-Sinoussi in the early 1980s.

Montagnier would later go on to support homeopathic medicine, another alternative treatment of disease and claim that HIV transmission in Africa could be diminished through better hygiene and nutrition.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Montagnier said the SARS-CoV-2 virus had been created in a lab with parts of the HIV genetic code. "It's difficult for a practicing virologist to say that a Nobel laureate in virology is spreading nonsense," said German virologist Christian Drosten on NDR's Coronavirus Update podcast in May 2020. "But that is utter nonsense."

James Watson, who was never known for his modesty, also let fame go to his head. In 1962, he was awarded the Nobel Prize along with Maurice Wilkins and Francis Crick for their discovery of the structure of the DNA molecule. But in the early 2000s, Watson repeatedly spread racist theories that Black people were less intelligent than white people.

For medical historian Hansson, Watson is a prime example that the Nobel Prize is not automatically awarded to a person who should be considered as a role model.

"You may have made a fantastic discovery, but that doesn't make you a universal genius," he said.

But the great expectations of an admiring public may also contribute to the development of Nobelitis. Paul Nurse, who won the Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine in 2001, soon found himself getting daily requests for interviews on all sorts of topics.

"As if suddenly I was acknowledgeable about all workings of the world and of human beings," he told DW.

Nurse had this advice for researchers: "Be careful quite what you say. Talk about things that you do know something about that would be fine. And if you don't know so much or you're not sure about something, be really clear that you're not sure."

Hansson has also noticed that the media eagerly report whatever Nobel laureates say — even when prize winners stray far from their area of expertise.

Nobelitis sufferers are leaders in their fields and rightly honored for their work with the Nobel. But they're also regular individuals, not immune to megalomania and other behavioral problems — meaning that homeopathy, orthomolecular medicine and other pseudosciences don't automatically become credible just because they're backed by a Nobel Prize winner.

#1482. Emerging economies are embracing solar power and skipping fossil fuels much faster than Europe or the US

Renewable electricity is cheaper than ever. While the West got rich on coal and oil, some of the world's poorest countries are banking on solar, wind and water instead.

As emerging economies and global living standards rise, is the world in for another fossil fuel boom? The data says not. Countries from Southeast Asia to Sub-Saharan Africa aren't investing in fossil fuels as the West did; they're investing in electricity powered by renewables.

When the US and Europe industrialized in the 19th and 20th centuries, they did so using fossil energy. From coal ovens, gas heating and combustion-engine cars to electricity produced in coal and gas power plants, entire societies relied on polluting fuels.

Yet while burning fossil fuels allowed industrialized countries to boom, it also caused the climate crisis that is linked to extreme weather events around the world today.

Many of the same countries that became economically powerful off the back of fossil fuels are now trying to transition to renewables, but centuries of legacy infrastructure hold them back.

Because a gas boiler won't run on wind power and a diesel truck cannot be solar-powered, societies looking to shift to green energy need to use more electricity and increase their share of renewables.

That is where many middle- and lower-income countries have an advantage. With less existing energy infrastructure built around fossil fuels, they are better placed for a faster rollout of renewable electricity sources.

In many instances, solar power in particular is now cheaper to produce than fossil electricity. And unlike large coal or gas plants, which take years to construct and millions in investments, solar and wind farms can be built quickly and with much less upfront cost.

At the same time, according to an analysis by renewables think tank Ember, electronic devices like refrigerators, electric stoves or air conditioners have all become much cheaper in the last decades, making electricity a more affordable option as an energy source for cooking, heating and more.

While electric cars are still a luxury item in most places, their prices are coming down too. And a UN study found that in India, Indonesia and Vietnam electric scooters and motorbikes already cost the same as petrol models. Yet a lot less to fuel.

All this means that renewable electricity is quickly becoming the best choice for the wallet — not just for the climate. And countries are taking note.

"We're seeing a leapfrog take place right across the emerging markets,” said Kingsmill Bond, energy strategist at Ember. "The whole of Asia is electrifying quickly, and the whole of Europe and the United States is stagnant."

In 2023, only around a quarter of global electricity was generated by renewables. But that figure is rising fast, and almost all new capacity added these days is based on solar or wind power.

Lower-income countries already use less fossil fuels and more renewables than higher-income ones.

And large middle-income economies are outpacing richer ones as well. In 2023, solar power alone made up at least six percent and ten percent of electricity in India and Vietnam, respectively. The global average was five percent.

This boom has meant middle- and lower-income countries are electrifying fast, consuming less energy directly from fossil fuels than richer countries. Vietnam, for instance, is already using more electricity than the US, Europe and even China, and India is following the same path.

But the potential for energy leapfrogging might be particularly high in low-income countries, especially across Sub-Saharan Africa.

Countries such as Kenya, Ethiopia and Namibia are rapidly expanding their energy infrastructure to meet the demands of a growing, young population. But rural areas in many countries are not connected to electricity grids.

Until a few years ago, he says, people defaulted to diesel generators to meet their energy needs. That came at a cost because importing fuel is particularly expensive in many African countries, meaning energy crises and oil price fluctuations hit much harder.

"Some businesses can make back their investment in just two years, so why would you want to deploy anything else?” he said. "Solar has rapidly become the de facto default technology that everyone falls back on.”

RAM: We recommend 16 GB of RAM. That’s become the new standard, even in more affordable laptops. Graphics: Want to play AAA PC games, edit video on your laptop, or run local AI models? You’ll want some powerful graphics on hand. At this point, Nvidia is the primary option. And because I grew up Southern Baptist in Alabama, I also contended with menacing subgenres of these panics. Rooted in religious strictures against secularism, they rhymed with what was happening in the culture at large if you were seeing it narrowly though a pinhole. At the time and place I grew up, I witnessed how culture warriors viewed Salman Rushdie’s novel The Satanic Verses (which they’d obviously never read) as a handbook of occult practices, repudiated the Catholic artist Andres Serrano’s Piss Christ as blasphemous, and reprised rumors from the 1960s suggesting that if you played certain rock songs backwards, demons would talk to you—only this time via cassette tape instead of vinyl.

This vivid array of scares and campfire stories had one key through line: an ostensible concern with the horrors that an amoral and predatory culture were wreaking on the youth of America. There was a far-from-complicated animus at the heart of these complaints, particularly when our Boomer elders were wielding them: A generation that had been thrust onto the center stage of mid-century consumer capitalism was now seething with resentment that a successor demographic was storming the leading edge of culture via art, changing social norms, and especially politics, which, as the right-wing Gen Xer Andrew Breitbart famously argued, is always downstream from culture.

It’s hard to avoid viewing the current generational fractures within the Democratic Party through this lens. Where older Democrats differ from much younger ones, it’s generally along the lines of attitudes toward sexuality and gender and trust in institutions and authority figures (especially the police, the military, and religious and business leaders). These positions on culture and character in public life also readily translate into differing views on the role of government in society and the role of the United States in global affairs.

Nowhere are these fractures more apparent than in the growing, well-chronicled divide between the centrist and progressive wings of the party, the latter of which skews younger. So we’re not quibbling about definitions here, I’m going to use the Pew Research Center’s terminology for the two poles of the liberal coalition: The “leftward progressives” are the farthest left (and the youngest), while the members of the “order and opportunity left” are the farthest right.

Leftward progressives support trans rights and think billionaires are bad for society. They care about US involvement in global affairs but believe we mostly tend to make things worse. The members of the order-and-opportunity left are concerned about safety and are “moderate” on issues of sexuality and gender. This is a nice way of saying they’re happy with some immigration restrictions, generally trust law enforcement, think gender is mostly determined by sex assigned at birth, and tend to whine if anyone asks about their pronouns.

Leftward progressives overwhelmingly vote Democratic (97 percent went for Kamala Harris in 2024) but express some dissatisfaction with the party. In a survey published in 2026, Pew found a clear correlation between higher age and lower progressive sentiment within the party, with leftward progressives accounting for 26 percent of Democrats and Democratic leaners under 30 and 15 percent in the 30-to-49 cohort. From there, the drop-off is steep: Leftward progressives make up just 6 percent of the Democratic-aligned 50-and-older group. Not surprisingly, the picture for the order-and-opportunity left is a virtual photographic negative, comprising 15 percent of Democratic and Democratic-leaning voters under 30, 22 percent of those in the 30-to-49 cohort, and 31 percent of those in the 50-and-older group.

These categories are not perfect, but they still give a strong account of the generational disparities in voter attitudes. They are decent proxies for the younger, more progressive left and the older, more centrist liberals. The issues these two groups disagree on constitute the fault line that runs through the Democratic Party right now, presenting real challenges for a party that’s long been assailed as a stodgy gerontocracy—and, tellingly, the ascribed responsibility for this growing schism tends to increase as one moves down the life-cycle chart. According to much of the discourse coming from Democratic officialdom, the problem—as always—is kids these days.

The leftward progressives align closely in spirit and demography with the Democratic Socialists of America. Despite its relatively small national membership (in the low six figures), the DSA is a ready stand-in for the wide-ranging establishment lamentations about leftist overreach. The DSA’s status as the de facto brand for a political youth movement is not incidental to the critique; it’s a load-bearing wall.

And the DSA has gotten younger over the past decade. In 2013, the average age of a DSA member was 67. After Donald Trump was elected in 2016, it was 33—a trend that, like the overall growth of the organization, largely stems from the insurgent presidential candidacies of the outlying progressive octogenarian Bernie Sanders. Thirty-three isn’t that young, but given the median age of a member of Congress (57.5 in the House and 64.7 in the Senate as of January 2025), DSA members are practically tweens.

The fact that younger members of the DSA—the people most responsible for having increased the national membership so quickly, from 5,000 just before Trump’s first election to more than 120,000 today—are playing a more prominent role, as well as leadership fights within the organization, have led to some generational fractures even in the DSA itself, particularly over the issue of Israel’s genocide in Gaza. Maurice Isserman, who had been a member of the DSA for over 40 years, wrote in The Nation in 2023 about quitting the organization, claiming that it had become too harsh in its broadsides against Israel and wasn’t criticizing Hamas harshly enough. At its core, this is a rehash of the old antisemitism argument: that a particular concern for Palestinian lives is effectively a pointed attack on Jewish ones. Isserman cited “the singularly important role of Palestine in DSA’s rather short list of international concerns, completely overshadowing the attention paid, for example, to Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, or to the rights of other oppressed peoples, like the Kurdish Muslims, or China’s Uyghur Muslims.”

This has become a common refrain among older Democrats: that the situation in Gaza is of interest to young people only because it allows them to criticize Israel and, by extension, Jews. (This argument relies on a disingenuous and dangerous conflation of Israeli nationalism and Judaism that’s been weaponized by the right, but that is a sermon for another time.) It’s telling that Isserman elides the very obvious reason why younger progressives might be more vocal about the Gaza genocide: The United States has provided more economic and military aid to Israel than any other country since the founding of the Israeli state—an outlay of at least $21.7 billion since October 7 alone. We (and here “we” includes DSA members, who pay taxes just like the rest of the Democratic coalition) are not providing billions in aid to Russia, China, or the persecutors of Kurdish Muslims in Syria or Iran.

This posture, in other words, isn’t a consequence of undue hostility toward Israel so much as it’s a reflection of moral injury: the feeling that something must be done, but that structural forces block effective moral action. Older generations remember a very different set of attitudes toward Israel, and many assume that the relationship is or should remain the same as it has always been. Younger generations are more likely to view the US–Israeli relationship through the lens of Netanyahu’s regime and its attendant barbarisms, which they have more direct evidence of thanks to social media and a 24/7 news cycle. Of course they feel more strongly about it than conflicts in which the United States is either ostensibly on the right side (as in Russia’s war with Ukraine) or has far less involvement.

But that doesn’t stop high-profile centrists from insisting that anyone below a certain age who is critical of Israel’s actions in Gaza is antisemitic, especially if they happen to be a brown person. Case in point: The Atlantic’s Jonathan Chait, who wrote a column about Michigan Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed’s relationship with Hasan Piker, whom Chait deems antisemitic for noting that it’s dangerous to conflate Israel and Judaism—a point echoed by many other Jewish people in the political center.

This brings us to another divide between the older generation and the younger: technology. If you don’t know who Hasan Piker is, he’s a political streamer on social media whose views are progressive. He is not the left’s Joe Rogan, because Rogan has said many more offensive things and no one is telling Republicans or California Governor Gavin Newsom to disassociate themselves from him. Nor is Piker formally connected to the Democratic Party apparatus. But he is popular with the youth. He has 3.1 million followers on Twitch and 1.9 million on YouTube. This level of influence is largely why centrist Democrats have determined that he is not just a problem but a “crisis,” in Chait’s alarmist formulation.

Determined to outdo Chait, the Democratic strategist James Carville has threatened to leave the Democratic Party entirely if Piker (who, again, is not a candidate, an operative, a consultant, or an officer of the DNC) becomes more of a “force.” “I am not going to be in the same party with Hasan Piker,” Carville proclaimed during one performance in his endless regress of kids-these-days cable hits.

Such tantrum-throwing may play well on television, but it makes for terrible political judgment. The ’90s-bred cult of studious difference-trimming and capitulation to the right that Carville helped pioneer continues to reinforce the Democrats’ image as a party terminally dedicated to preserving the status quo. In one recent, all-too-typical case, Representative Hakeem Jeffries, who will likely be the next House speaker if the Democrats do well in the midterms, faced a massive backlash after he met with Jared Kushner to discuss ways the Democrats could work with the Trump administration. Jeffries is on the younger end of the establishment (he’s 56) but is politically synched with his mentor Nancy Pelosi and Senate minority leader Chuck Schumer.

Our seniority-heavy caste of democratic leaders have been slower to acknowledge that we are in extraordinary times. In 2019, I wrote about Pelosi’s reluctance to impeach Trump (she said he was self-impeaching) and her simultaneous bigfooting of the newly elected leftist congresswomen who made up “the Squad.” Pelosi came around on impeaching Trump, but she went on to block Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez from the senior position on the House Oversight Committee in favor of Gerry Connolly, a 74-year-old who died of cancer shortly after his appointment. Pelosi’s generation of leaders is quick to protect the management cohort of the party—which is now a full-on gerontocracy—from any encroachment by younger elected officials, even at the expense of the party’s future.

And they are incapable of acting as an opposition party; they don’t have the stomach for it. Their political instincts were formed during an era when elections were not determined, as they are now, almost entirely by negative partisanship. During the ’90s heyday of most of the figures who now make up the party’s leadership, engaging in civility theater was a sign of political maturity. Now voters view it mostly as a sign of weakness.

The former New York City comptroller and current Democratic nominee for Congress Brad Lander, a DSA-aligned progressive who jointly campaigned with Zohran Mamdani for mayor, has described the divide among Democrats as “folders” versus “fighters.” This line breaks down generationally, too. In this political environment, writing “very strong letters,” taking closed-door meetings with Trumpists, adopting Republican talking points about gender expression in the guise of “just asking questions,” and dismissing the concerns of women and people of color as “identity politics” looks like folding. Pushing for big systemic reforms that will trigger formidable political challenges (universal healthcare, tax reform that puts more of the burden on the rich and less on the poor, ambitious housing goals) looks like fighting. Standing up for trans people, criticizing the United States’ role in providing weapons to Israel, and biting back at GOP insults looks like fighting.

Younger Democrats are simply better equipped to act like an opposition party—in part because they don’t have the experience of being in power, either as a party or as an age cohort. They are less willing to wait patiently until the next election—or the next one or the next one—for Democrats to gain new seats or get around to exercising whatever power they already have.

I’m 49 years old and taught at New York University for six years and the School of Visual Arts for six years before that. I’ve worked on Democratic campaigns of all stripes, some with DSA candidates (including one with a younger Zohran Mamdani) and some with older, more moderate candidates in red states. I have been around many young Democrats; I’ve taught them, hired them, and also witnessed directly the pushback they get from Boomers and Gen Xers who resent their efforts to exert real political power instead of just sitting on the sidelines until they’re old and rich enough to own a house and have kids.

Yet the relevant divides remain as stubbornly cultural as they are material. It’s generally the case that older generations oppose student protests on the apparent assumption that students should earn their right to dissent, as if the First Amendment is something you graduate into.

When I defend younger activists and candidates, I always prepare for a barrage of facts about the alleged erratic levels of political engagement among this cohort that are technically true but often willfully misinterpreted. The first of these cherished data points is the one showing that younger people don’t vote as reliably as older people. But the people who cite this statistic assume it’s because the youths are too self-involved, or lazy, or possibly hungover from last night’s party. They almost never acknowledge that young people’s voter participation is heavily dictated by systems that fundamentally work against them.

Voting is a habit, and it’s instilled over many years as people get used to the cadence of elections—particularly in traditionally low-turnout contests such as primaries and downballot races. In addition, campaigns can’t afford to target everyone, so they often seek to identify likely supporters among those who have consistently voted over a period of years, which obviously leaves out younger people. Plus, the political participation of college students is actively suppressed by the right, which assumes that they’re more liberal than the general population. And it’s astonishingly easy to impose voter-suppression measures at universities and colleges where students are living away from home.

As a simple matter of self-preservation, elected officials (many of whom are much, much older if we’re talking about Congress) are less responsive to the needs of people who don’t have children or own homes. Younger voters are much less likely to fall within this charmed circle of the electorate than their grandparents—in part because of the political and economic windfalls that the older generation received.

Despite all of this, young people have been going to the polls in greater numbers over the past decade. As of this writing, the 35-year-old mayor of New York City is the seventh-most-popular Democrat in the country, and AOC is the fourth. Both are dues-paying members of the DSA.

If Democrats are in crisis, it’s not the fault of the young. They should not be held collectively responsible for a badly worded comment from a popular influencer who is not running for office. By contrast, Boomer Democrats have done nothing to hold their own cohort responsible for its ties to an ex-president and an ex–treasury secretary who palled around with Jeffrey Epstein. (James Carville is fine with Bill Clinton remaining a power broker in the Democratic Party, but a streamer he’d never heard of before last week is a bridge too far.) At 18, young people are deemed old enough by the US government to die for their country, sign up for hundreds of thousands of dollars in student debt, and be forced to bear children if they become unexpectedly pregnant. Many of them have to pay the same effective tax rates as older Americans who are far richer. They deserve political participation—and yet are continuously vouchsafed less than their fair share of it.

Leftward progressivism, as Pew calls it, is not just a youth movement (plenty of us on the left are old!). But it is a youth movement culturally, even as it’s built on the work of earlier generations. Because of that, older elements in the Democratic coalition find it threatening; it marshals a political movement around changing social norms, concerns motivated by a sense of moral injury during a formative period of life, differing expectations about what government can and should do, and a justifiably righteous conviction that it’s better to fight and lose on hard issues than not to fight at all. Which makes it very much like virtually every youth movement that came before it.

The idea that progressive young people are sabotaging the party from within with pronouns, keffiyehs, and wild, irrational ideas about what the government should be able to do for people that governments in other developed countries routinely manage to do is just another moral panic. I expect that the younger members of the party who emerge as leaders will prove their elders wrong. You can indeed see this process unfolding right now in Mamdanistan, where we have less violent crime, faster pothole repairs, a handful of city-owned grocery stores slated to open, and no open revolts.
