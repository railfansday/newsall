#3052. Burnham to reveal plans for AI unit to tackle wildfires after scorching summer

PM will also announce funding for specialised protective gear and the deployment of military aircraft

The UK government is to create an AI Cobra unit to spot wildfires after this year’s record-breaking summer saw devastating blazes across the country.

The country experienced its hottest-ever summer this year, with prolonged dry periods and extreme heat fuelling wildfires that triggered an emergency alert to mobile phones in England and Wales. Figures released on 8 October showed a clear upward trend in the number of fires recorded each year.

The Met Office found that the climate crisis made this extreme heat about 130 times more likely.

At the government’s first wildfire resilience summit on Friday, Andy Burnham will announce plans for a new wildfire AI unit in the National Situation Centre, which is part of the Cobra briefing rooms.

Businesses including Google, Airbus and VodafoneThree are expected to attend the summit to set out how satellite monitoring and early warning systems can help fight fires. The government said its new AI unit would enable it to “spot and fight fires faster”.

The prime minister will also announce £45m for fire crews to buy wildfire-specific personal protective equipment (PPE).

This comes after the Guardian revealed that more than half of the 29 fire services that responded to requests for information had no wildfire PPE.

They included the Suffolk fire and rescue service, which had to deal with an “apocalyptic” wildfire earlier this summer that burned close to the Sizewell B nuclear power station and destroyed vast areas of a protected nature reserve.

Standard particle-filtering respirators do not protect against dangerous gases such as carbon monoxide, which are heavily present in wildfire smoke.

Standard PPE can also be too hot and heavy for wildfire conditions. This can cause crews to overheat, or remove their PPE altogether, exposing them to greater danger from heat, smoke and radiation.

The prime minister will also announce that military planes and helicopters will be made available to fight wildfires on remote moorland, steep hillsides and other hard-to-reach terrain.

The defence secretary, Wes Streeting, said: “As the threat of wildfires in the UK grows, defence will step up to provide greater support to our emergency services and protect military training areas across the country.”

The government will also look at tougher local restrictions or product bans to stop people starting fires when the risk is high. In August, Burnham announced a temporary ban on sales of disposable barbecues. He has also been criticised for failing to address the climate emergency in his wildfire response.

Farmers will also be given funding to make landscapes more resilient to fires. This announcement comes after the government quietly scrapped plans announced under the Conservative government to prepare the countryside in England for wildfires.

This revelation led Alistair Carmichael, a Scottish Liberal Democrat MP and chair of the Commons environment, food and rural affairs committee, to raise “serious questions about whether this summer’s wildfires could have looked different with more resources on the ground”.

The government also said it would allocate £25m to restore dried-out peatlands in England so they hold more water and act as natural firebreaks, as well as provide financial support for farmers to fireproof their land.

This new grant scheme is expected to run from April 2027 to April 2029.

Environmental campaigner and author Guy Shrubsole said: “For decades, wealthy grouse moor owners have drained our peat bogs, turning them into a tinderbox. This grant will help reverse that destruction, but must be followed by a new round of landscape recovery funding, and duties on landowners to repair the damage done. It’s time to make England wet again.”

The prime minister said: “I stood in Stourbridge and watched families lose everything. I will never forget it. Our firefighters were heroes that day, but heroes shouldn’t have to fight with one hand tied behind their backs.

“Crews will now have the kit they need to stay safe and military aircraft will stand ready to help tackle wildfires from the sky. We cannot put people through another summer like this one.”

#3053. The Golden Rules of Airplane Etiquette—Because We Have Simply Seen Enough

It is my firm belief that everyone—no matter their age, gender, race, or Delta SkyMiles Medallion Status—needs to learn proper airplane etiquette, and the earlier the better. As a freelance travel writer, I get on lots of flights to and from different parts of the world, and I’ve seen the same silly mistakes, rude behavior, and even downright harmful stunts enacted by all types of travelers. But by that same token, I’ve also witnessed acts of generosity and helpfulness that all people universally appreciate.

So what, exactly, are the rules at 35,000 feet? To draft such a constitution, I tapped three of Condé Nast Traveler’s Top Travel Specialists who are experts in air travel, in addition to my own first-hand experience: Sophie Caulcutt, co-founder of The Voyager Club; Vikram Seshadri, co-founder of In Residence; and Paul Tumpowsky, chief revenue officer of Fora. And to get the real insider take on how we should behave in the air, I spoke with two working flight attendants: Rich Henderson, a flight attendant for a US-based airline and co-host of the podcast Two Guys on A Plane; and a US-based flight attendant and content creator (@lifeinlayovers on TikTok) named Liam.

In general, we agreed that there’s no environment where etiquette (or the lack thereof) matters more than when you’re sitting shoulder-to-shoulder with dozens of other seatbelt-strapped passengers inside a metal tube hurling through the skies. And while commercial air travel may not always be kind to us, we can choose to be part of the solution. As Tumpowsky of Fora pleads,: “Perhaps we can look inside ourselves to make the overall traveling experience better for us and our fellow travelers.”

In the spirit of doing unto others as we would have them do unto us, below are the golden rules of airplane etiquette. Keep these principles and good habits in mind, and you’ll find that, even when everyone else around you is behaving badly, you’ll feel all the better for being a kind and considerate traveler. A true professional, some may even go so far to say.

Among travelers, especially those seated in economy, no one is dying to spend more time on the plane than they have to. So instead of crowding the gate—a rookie move that will earn you the unattractive nickname of “gate lice”—be patient and wait to board last. Your assigned seat will still be there at the end of boarding. (After all, a queen is never late. Everyone else is simply early.)

Jostling to get on board early to secure overhead bin space? The crew will almost always find a spot, even if it’s not directly above your seat; or they’ll check your bag into the hold at no extra cost. To mitigate the inconvenience, pack light and put together your “seat bag” so you have all your essentials (more on these points below).

Are you flying in business or first class? Fabulous. “Linger in the lounge, enjoy the restaurant, and board last,” says Seshadri of In Residence. “Your overhead bin space is dedicated and waiting for you.”

For parents traveling with kids, you might actually want to be among the last passengers to board. “Why spend an extra 30 or 40 minutes sitting on a plane with young children if you don’t have to?” Tumpowsky says. “But I completely understand why new parents, in particular, may want to board early to make sure they have enough storage within reach for everything they’ll need during a long flight.”

Make your personal item count: Before getting on the plane, prepare a “seat bag” that contains all your in-flight must-haves and keep it close to you in your seat. For me, that’s usually my fanny pack with my passport, boarding pass, phone, AirPods, eye mask, lip balm, hand cream, phone charger, and battery pack. Maybe my Kindle or a copy of The New Yorker; a toothbrush and toothpaste for overnight flights. This will eliminate or reduce the need to rummage through the bag under the seat in front of you, or step out into the aisle to reach for your luggage in the overhead bins.

Turns out, doing this is also helpful for the people around you, according to Henderson of Two Guys on a Plane: “Boarding is basically a giant traffic jam, and one person standing in the aisle reorganizing their entire suitcase can hold up a hundred people behind them. Have what you need for the flight ready before you board, find your seat, put your bag away, and step out of the aisle as quickly as possible.”

First off, acknowledge the flight attendants when you board with eye contact, a smile, a quick hello. These are the people here to make sure you stay safe on the plane and everything goes smoothly, first and foremost. Be a good human and treat them with the dignity they deserve, and you’ll get the same treatment in kind. At the end of the flight, do the same thing and thank the crew—you all made it!

Next, carry-on bags. People tend to overpack heavy carry-on bags because most US airlines do not weigh them, explains Liam of @lifeinlayovers, so boarding becomes even more hectic and slow when you can’t lift your own stuff into the overhead bins. Try to pack only what you can literally carry. Consider too your own physical abilities, height, and strength, and pack accordingly. And if you see someone struggling with putting away their carry-ons and you can help, do so. It helps everyone out, and makes the boarding process go more smoothly. The flight attendants will thank you. Speaking of: Note that many airlines discourage flight attendants from helping with your overhead luggage because of the risk of injury, says Henderson, so don’t expect that every FA can or will lend you a hand.

While we’re on the topic of overhead bins, Henderson says, “Please don’t put your small purse or jacket in the overhead bin when people are still looking for space for their actual suitcases.” Save putting them away in the overhead space until boarding is mostly complete; there will always be space on the plane for your stuff, Liam says.

As for deboarding the plane, a few more best practices: Do not get into the aisle while the plane is still taxiing to the gate. Once the plane comes to a complete stop, that’s when deboarding can begin. Wait for the rows in front of you to get up and head to the exit, rather than scrambling to snatch your items from the overhead bins and just crowding the aisle. As space opens up in the aisle, that’s when you stand up, get your bags, and exit.

It’s possible someone behind you will react exasperated, believing you should have gotten your stuff earlier—when there was no room to do so. If that happens, give them a smile (or a sly wink, if you’re feeling cheeky), and retrieve your luggage calmly, knowing that you’re in the right.

When you’re transiting and have limited time to get from Flight A to Flight B, kindly inform a flight attendant prior to landing. “I believe the appropriate protocol is to speak with a flight attendant rather than attempt to negotiate your way to the front of the aircraft,” Tumpowsky says. “The crew can assess just how tight your connection really is, determine whether special consideration is warranted, and, if appropriate, make an announcement asking other passengers to remain seated. That request carries considerably more authority coming from the flight crew than from a passenger who has decided that their connection is more important than everyone else’s.”

Caulcutt of The Voyager Club agrees, saying that the crew may even consider it essential to move your seat closer to the exit—or at least closer to the aisle—if your connection is extremely tight or if they anticipate a delayed arrival. When approaching the crew, Caulcutt says, “I often find that a trip to the galley, during boarding or shortly after, to politely explain the situation one-on-one works best.”

My seat, my choice! When we select our seats on a plane, especially when we pay to get assigned places or use status benefits to do so, we are well within our rights to keep it. Able-bodied spouses, families, and friends can reunite after the flight. You can politely and firmly refuse to swap. (Exercise caution if using the correct but risky phrase: “Your lack of planning is not my problem.”)

“But just apply common sense,” says Caulcutt. “You don't want to separate an elderly couple,” who may physically need each other for assistance, or “a mother from a weeping child.” Because did you really want to sit next to that wailing five year old anyway? Swap with the parent who can calm them down.

Some of us love making friends wherever we go, but when you’re going to be stuck on a plane with hundreds of other people in a steel projectile hurtling through the air, the rules of social engagement get a touch more delicate. So what’s acceptable, and where do you draw the line?

“A brief conversation and a little cordiality are perfectly appropriate,” says Tumpowsky. It’s not a bad idea, though by no means required, to establish some rapport with those around you—but don’t force anything. “If your seatmate engages and the feeling is mutual, by all means, enjoy the conversation,” Tumpowsky continues. “But no one should feel obligated to continue a conversation longer than they're comfortable with. A polite exchange of pleasantries is not a binding commitment to discuss your respective life stories until the landing gear is employed.”

If you’re traveling with a friend or friends and you’re all prone to yapping, keep a few things in mind: “Keep the tone of the conversation low,” says Caulcutt. “People in planes tend to talk louder than they realize because of engine noise. Try to keep your voice at a level that your seatmate can hear comfortably but the rows around you can’t easily follow.” Try to keep topics light and disagreements to a minimum, she adds. If you get into a fight, you won’t have much room to put some distance between you on a plane.

And on night or red-eye flights, the lights in the cabin are usually dimmed and people are trying to sleep, Caulcutt says, so keep conversations to a whisper whenever possible. Otherwise, save them for later on the ground.

Be patient, and take deep breaths. “No one hates it more than the parents,” says Caulcutt. Period!

OK, well, it really is for our own safety in the air. As Henderson says, “I promise we flight attendants are not doing the safety demonstration for our own entertainment!” Do as advised and check where your nearest exits are; and see where the closest bathroom is, while you’re at it. Brush up on the brace position appropriate for the cabin you’re in. Enjoy the safety video, if there is one (the Philippine Airlines “Safetynovela,” depicted as a soap opera, is my current favorite). If the FAs are doing a live demo, give them your attention and a smile.

This is all the more important when you’re seated in an exit row. When the FAs check in with you about the responsibilities you have in the case of an emergency, answer truthfully regarding whether or not you're physically, mentally, or even spiritually able to carry out what needs to be done. This is the cost of the exit-row legroom. (Also, the exit-row legroom comes with the price of not being able to stow luggage in front of you, as there’s no seat to keep them under, so bear that in mind when picking your place on the plane.)

The fasten-your-seatbelt sign is not a suggestion. “Turbulence can happen without warning, even when the sky looks perfectly clear,” Henderson says. Liam explains that the flight deck turns on the sign when there's a forecast of bad weather up ahead, either from radar or aircraft ahead of the plane you’re on. So keep your belt fastened whenever you’re seated, Seshadri says, “even if loosely! You’ll never regret it.”

All our experts and FAs agree that reclining your seat is fair game. That’s what it’s there for. As Tumpowsky says, “Whether it’s the overhead light, window shade, or reclining, I generally believe you have the right to use the product as it was designed.” And everyone is also on the same page when it comes to meal time: Don’t do it while the person behind you is eating. It’s just good manners.

“A little courtesy goes a long way,” Seshadri says. “Recline slowly, be mindful if the person behind you is using it—like working on a laptop—and put your seat upright when the crew asks, especially for takeoff and landing.” On a plane, as in life: Be considerate of others.

This is another point on which all our experts were in agreement. The flight attendant call button is absolutely there to be used, especially if you need assistance, have a medical concern, or there’s something that requires a crew member’s attention—like if your phone fell into the cracks of the seat. (A very real concern! If the machinery ends up cracking your phone and the battery leaks, it could be a fire hazard.)

“But if the FAs are visibly in the middle of beverage service and you just want another soda, it’s usually easier to ask us when we reach your row,” Henderson says. And don’t use the button to summon them every five minutes for things that can wait. There are sometimes hundreds of other passengers and only a few of them. Liam says, “Staffing has decreased and cabins have expanded. Please be patient with us, especially when we’re busy with service or there’s turbulence.”

Again, among our experts, this is a universally acknowledged truth: “The middle seat passenger deserves both middle armrests,” Henderson says. After all, the window passenger gets the window, and the aisle passenger gets a little extra breathing room. “The poor person in the middle deserves something!” he says. (Liam agrees: “As someone who often gets the middle seat when flying standby on benefits, please, for the love of all things good, give me both middle armrests.”)

“No bare feet on display ever!” Caulcutt says. And rightly so. You can remove your shoes, especially on long-haul flights; our feet do swell in flight. Keep your socks on (try compression socks); swiftly and discreetly swap into a fresh pair if you’ve lost the ability to smell yourself. Do not ever prop your feet up on the armrests, seatbacks, or tray tables. (Quick trick: Use your backpack or duffel bag, under the seat in front of you, as a foot rest.)

Henderson’s advice: “Never walk into an airplane bathroom barefoot or even just in socks. That mysterious liquid on the floor is probably not water.” And Seshadri’s: “Please never clip your toenails or file them on a flight!”

For better or worse, Wi-Fi connectivity is getting better on planes, meaning that what often used to be a relatively quiet airborne sanctuary is now another space in which the cacophony of modern life can invade. So, again, don’t be a jerk about it.

Our experts agree that headphones and earbuds are a must, whether you’re listening to music, watching a movie on your own device, or scrolling through Instagram Reels. Do not take your high-level meeting from 35,000 feet in the air. “Voice and video calls are usually prohibited by airline policy or regulations,” Henderson says. “An airplane is a shared space, not your personal living room or office.”

Expect these principles to be sticking points in the coming years. “High-bandwidth internet—and potentially the ability to make calls more easily in the air—is going to create the next battleground in flight etiquette,” Tumpowsky says. “Just because technology allows you to do something doesn’t necessarily mean 200 other passengers want you to do it.”

Alas, from personal experience, it has to be said! And think about your surroundings when you’re watching a movie and there’s a graphic scene in the story, whether sexual or otherwise. Consider speeding through if there are, say, children around. That said, it’s really up to you—especially if the movie or show in question is offered through the in-flight entertainment system. I was once on a flight watching an episode of And Just Like That… and they got to the infamous Snapple scene in Carrie’s apartment and, well, I didn’t skip it since I was pretty much alone in the business class cabin.

As long as you’re not doing a full-on downward dog in the aisle, Caulcutt says, stretching in the aisle is fine. Keep it quick and respectful. Do some standing spine twists, lymphatic taps, and gentle walking, then return to your seat. Don’t do it during meal service or when the FAs are working. If an FA tells you to return to your seat, don’t make a fuss and sit back down.

“We encourage passengers to get up and move around when it’s safe, especially on long flights, but the aisle is a workspace for flight attendants and an important pathway for everyone on board, especially in the event of an emergency where we must move quickly,” Henderson says. So don’t block the aisle, congregate around the bathrooms, or interfere with the crew doing their jobs. “A little stretching is one thing, but turning the plane into a yoga studio is another,” he says.

There’s nothing wrong with politely waking someone if you need to get out of your row to reach the toilet. Just be considerate (there it is again, basic kindness). Liam says, “I’d hate to be woken up after I’ve just gotten comfortable; for me, that’s having an eye mask and headphones on. Watch out for those signs. But if you have to go, you have to go.” Wake someone up the way you’d want to be woken: Respectfully.

The flip side is that if someone in your row gets up to use the bathroom and disturbs you, it might be a good opportunity for you to go as well. “Think of it as airplane efficiency,” Tumpowsky says. “You may save everyone another round of climbing over one another later in the flight.”

Whether they physically need to or not, many people allow their intrusive thoughts to win and grab the top of the seat in front of them to pull themselves up. This is awful and rude. If you really need support, Tumpowsky says, “the proper etiquette is to use your own row of seats for support whenever possible. Pulling on the seat in front of you can jolt the person sitting there—and if they’re sleeping, you’ve just unnecessarily woken them up.”

I was gonna say, “leave the bathroom the way you leave yours,” but the mileage may vary on that one. Keep the place tidy, wipe anything you made wet as a courtesy to the person who comes in after you, and dispose of any waste properly, as the laws of common decency intend. “Just because you don’t want to push down the flap to discard your trash doesn’t mean you can just start a pile of it on the ground or counter,” Liam says. “And for the love of aviation, wear shoes in there!” Henderson adds.

Maybe there was once a time, in a faraway land, when dressing nicely for a flight might have improved your chances of an upgrade. No longer. “Today, upgrades are much more likely to be determined by status, algorithms, bidding, or other systems, so I don’t think your wardrobe is moving you up the list,” Tumpowsky says. “Dress comfortably. Being comfortable will have a much bigger impact on your flight experience than looking like you belong in first class.”

You can do so comfortably! May I interest you in a cashmere jogger set or a wrinkle-free traveling suit? Figure out a travel uniform that works for you—something comfortable that also makes you feel put-together. It’s a proven fact (by me, anecdotally) that looking good helps you feel good, like you can handle any travel hiccup like flight delays or lost baggage with dignity and grace. But hey if it’s not your literal style, no one says you have to.

“Always be polite to the plane crew,” Caulcutt says. Flight attendants aren’t responsible for the weather, delays, baggage fees, or most airline policies, so don’t take your frustrations out on the people who are there to keep you safe and comfortable to the best of their abilities.

Patience and kindness can go a long way, Henderson says, and Seshadri reminds everyone to extend that respect to the airport staff and your fellow passengers. Moving with respect and courtesy, he says, “will earn you not just a better flight but a good dose of karma and happiness too.”

Yes, cabins are getting stuffed with more seats, and those seats are getting smaller, while crew staffing keeps getting cut. And yet, even in the face of all this, on top of everything going on in our world, we get to travel. So it’s more important than ever to cultivate a sense of solidarity as travelers. As Liam puts it, “We’re truly all in this metal tube together.”

#3056. A London Scheme to Cut Car Pollution Restored Lung Growth in Children

A controversial scheme aimed at slashing car pollution in Central London was a boon to children in the area, whose lungs grew faster after the program was launched, researchers found.

Established in 2019, the Ultra Low Emission Zone initially covered around 8 square miles in Central London. To drive in the zone, owners of older, more polluting cars, buses, and trucks would have to pay a daily fee, equal to around $17 for most drivers, with fines for those who skirted the fee. The scheme led to a steep drop of nitrogen dioxide pollution, which is known to impede lung growth.

To study the impact on children, researchers followed more than 3,400 students, between the ages of 6 and 9, at schools in Central London and in the town of Luton, roughly 30 miles away. Between 2018 and 2022, they regularly measured the lung volume of each child.

Before the emissions scheme was introduced, researchers found, children in London had stunted lung growth. But within five years of the program’s launch, they saw a turnaround. While exposure to pollution fell in both Central London and Luton over that period, in Central London it fell by more than twice as much. And as pollution dropped, the lungs of the children in London grew faster, until they had caught up with children in Luton.

The findings have significant health implications, as children without fully developed lungs are at greater risk of asthma and other health disorders. As pollution fell in Central London, researchers found, the proportion of children with impaired lung function also dropped, from 14 percent to 9 percent. The research was published in The Lancet Public Health.

U.K. Health Minister Karin Smyth said the study “is a powerful reminder of the impact that air quality has on children’s lung health, and will help to improve our understanding of how reductions in air pollution can restore normal lung growth among children, reduce the risk of respiratory illnesses in adulthood, and support our goal to give every child the best start in life.”

The Ultra Low Emission Zone was expanded in 2021 and again in 2023, and now covers all of Greater London. Under the scheme, officials estimate, levels of roadside nitrogen dioxide have fallen by 27 percent across London, while deaths linked to toxic air have dropped by 40 percent.

Despite its effect on pollution, the zone has proved highly controversial, sparking widespread protests over the fees and vandalism. Londoners have damaged or stolen hundreds of the more than 3,700 cameras installed citywide to monitor traffic.

Even long-term travelers who talk about how they “aren’t tourists” do the same – just at a slower pace and while trying to spend less money.

And there’s nothing wrong with that. That’s just the nature of travel. While we all imagine dropping into a place, befriending locals, and having this crazy time like we’re in some movies. #3058. Invisible Beaker STEM Challenge

The invisible beaker is a classic science activity and a fantastic example of “science magic” that’s sure to provide a wow moment to children (and adults) of all ages! It’s inexpensive and very easy. You need a large and a small glass beaker and enough clear cooking oil to cover the small beaker when it’s placed inside the larger one.

The beakers must be made of borosilicate glass, such as Pyrex, or the demonstration won’t work. The secret to the small beaker disappearing is because of something called the Refractive index.

Optional challenge – draw a picture on the smaller beaker and watch as it seems to float inside the big beaker.

Usually, when light travels from one material to another, it changes speed and bends. It is the bending of light which allows us to see the edges of a transparent object.

Oil and glass have the same refractive index (approximately 1.47), which means that light passes through them as if they were a single solid substance. Light doesn’t bend as it passes from one to the other, so our eyes cannot detect the small beaker!

You can see the edges of the larger beaker as the refractive index of air is 1.0. Light bends as it passes from the glass to the air, and so the edge of the beaker is visible.

#3062. Experts cast doubt on Anthropic report detailing how AI has altered work at the company

Anthropic claims AI will shift how work gets done and labor demand – but some experts are skeptical of the vision

Last year, the Anthropic CEO, Dario Amodei, warned that artificial intelligence could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs and that in six months it would be writing 90% of code. Neither of those predictions came to pass.

Now, in a report Anthropic published on Thursday, the company said it was hiring fewer junior technical workers and that human attention has become the new “bottleneck” as it increasingly relies on its Claude AI agents.

Anthropic says AI has changed the way work gets done, and as a result, restructured employment at the company. The report suggests its changes foreshadow the future of work more broadly: as more companies adopt AI agents, software engineers could face a tougher job market while entry-level tech workers struggle to build necessary skills and experience.

“As AI diffuses and improves, the organization of work could shift,” Anthropic’s report reads before offering a potential future. “One possibility is single-person startups, each with a human founder steering swarms of agents to rake in billions in revenue.”

In its report, Anthropic touts its “deep adoption of AI”, which it claims resulted in “producing more code per person, closing more sales per rep, and processing more contracts”. By outsourcing some coding and sales-related tasks to AI agents, Anthropic said it raised productivity within a few months.

From May 2026 onwards, AI agents logged more work hours than human employees, the report said. Employees now spend about 22% of their time managing Claude agents, more than double the amount a year ago, based on Anthropic’s analysis of their chat summaries.

It also says since March 2025 – soon after the release of its agentic AI coding tool Claude Code – entry-level technical hires dropped from nearly 30% to 9%, and headcount dropped from about 21% to 14%. It attributes the shifts to AI usage, and described its workers as merely “a thin layer atop a growing contingent of AI agents”.

While the report provides a small glimpse of the inner workings of the AI heavyweight, some AI researchers are skeptical that it will herald big changes across companies or industries. Instead, they say the findings are probably Anthropic’s attempt to drive its valuation ahead of its initial public offering – similar to how SpaceX boasted about proposed space datacenters to boost its market debut in June.

Anthropic’s claims seem to back some tech executives’ view that AI is behind recent waves of corporate layoffs, but research complicates that.

Morgan Frank, a Case Western Reserve University researcher on how AI and technological changes affect workers, recently posted a paper that’s in peer review showing that AI-exposed job markets deteriorated well before the arrival of ChatGPT in November 2022 and Claude a few months later. State unemployment claims reveal that job markets tanked starting at the beginning of 2022, months before ChatGPT’s release, according to the paper.

“It doesn’t appear that the launch of ChatGPT corresponds to any major increase in loss or worsening outcomes,” he said.

Frank said several factors could explain recent job losses, including cutting pandemic-era hires and “AI-washing”, where some bosses credit AI for layoffs they might have made anyway.

Julián Posada, a Yale social scientist, also questions Anthropic’s motives for publishing its report.

“It is written from the perspective of a company that wants to sell shares, a company that really wants people to invest in it,” said Posada, author of the book Platform Extractivism: Data Work and the People Powering Artificial Intelligence.

Unlike an economics study, the report lacked an independent assessment by outside auditors, Posada said. Its figures and graphs only tell part of the story, without revealing how Anthropic’s workers feel about their work or how they are actually engaging with the models, he said.

Dylan Baker, lead research engineer at the non-profit Distributed AI Research Institute, was also curious about how Anthropic’s developers feel about the changes. Shifting some software development to AI, while hiring few entry-level workers and having the remaining engineers wrangle AI agents seems “shortsighted”, they said.

Baker is also surprised by Anthropic’s human “bottleneck” characterization.

“That’s such a strange way to put it,” they said. “It fundamentally misunderstands how software is written, architected and designed. It’s such a social, interpersonal and human process.”

Anthropic’s report quotes an unnamed engineer, who described facing “an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention”. That doesn’t sound like a fruitful work environment, Baker said. “I wouldn’t think really thoughtful, well-designed products are coming out of that team.”

As generative AI products proliferate, a majority of Americans are wary of AI, with particular concerns about AI-related job losses, according to Pew Research Center. In its report, Anthropic suggests that tech employers should offer their workers stock ownership, posing this as a potential solution to rising concerns. “It is difficult to imagine a version of pro-worker AI adoption in which workers do not hold a financial stake in successful rollout,” it said.

Anthropic did not respond to the Guardian’s request for comment.

Anthropic, which could debut on the stock market as soon as November, released its employment report one day after pledging to commit 5% of its shares to the “public good”, though it did not provide details. Critics noted that philanthropy has long doubled as a reputation builder used by tech titans like Bill Gates and Mark Zuckerberg.

But Anthropic, and its claims that it is improving how work gets done could face tougher scrutiny as it nears its IPO. A draft of the company’s S-1 filing revealed that Anthropic had an $80bn net loss over the first half of this year, and that it received a junk credit rating in June. Considering Anthropic’s slowing revenue growth, investors now project a valuation of $1.5tn, down from earlier estimates of $2tn or more. At the same time, Anthropic’s cloud computing partner, CoreWeave, has become swamped in AI debt and its stock is plummeting, fueling concerns that the AI bubble may begin to deflate.

Regardless of when or whether the bubble pops, software engineers and other tech workers will surely be affected, Baker said. The question is how that will play out.

“My hope is that there is a reckoning, a reassessment or re-evaluation of how much this has always been about human work,” they said.

#3065. Collapse of Atlantic Currents Could Drive a Surge in Warming

A vast system of Atlantic currents appears to be weakening, and scientists say its collapse could lead to dramatic cooling in northern Europe. A new study suggests an additional risk: a surge in warming across the rest of the globe.

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, or AMOC, carries warm water from the tropics toward Europe. As the water flows north, it cools, becoming more dense, and descends to the depths, where it flows back south. But as oceans warm, those waters are not so cool, nor so dense, as they once were, and measurements suggest the system of circulation is slowing down.

Complicating matters further, the melting Greenland ice sheet is unleashing into the North Atlantic large amounts of fresh water, which is less dense than salty water, further slowing circulation. If the AMOC were to collapse, temperatures in northern Europe would drop by 9 to 27 degrees F (5 to 15 degrees C), according to one estimate.

The weakening is not a new phenomenon. The AMOC has undergone hundreds of weak periods over the last 2.7 million years as the climate shifted. And scientists have long assumed that each time the North Atlantic cooled, southern latitudes warmed by an equal amount. The total amount of heat remained roughly constant, they thought. It merely shifted from place to place.

But the new modeling study reveals a more dramatic change, showing that when AMOC previously weakened, the Earth grew hotter overall.

“The AMOC works like a heat valve that controls the energy budget of the planet,” said lead author Christo Buizert, of Oregon State University. With a strong AMOC, warm water flows to the North Atlantic, where the heat is lost to the atmosphere. But with a weak AMOC, the study found, heat instead builds up in the ocean.

Buizert said that past weak periods produced the same amount of warming as 10 years of human emissions would today. The research was published in Nature Geoscience.

Buizert noted that studies of past shifts in the AMOC are not a perfect comparison for changes underway today, but they can help scientists make sense of what may be in store as temperatures rise.

#3066. Don’t Visit Ko Lipe

After 19 years, I finally went back to Ko Lipe, the Thai island I spent close to a month on in 2006. Back then, it was one of those super off-the-beaten-path destinations that few but the most intrepid travelers visited, where electricity only ran a few hours a day, basic bungalows right on the beach cost something like $2 USD, and there really was a last boat for the season.

There was much to do here but that was the point. You hung out on the beach, read a book, went snorkeling, went back to the beach, drank beers at the one beach bar on the island, rotated meals between the five restaurants there, and went to bed early.

It was paradise – and a place a lot of people got stuck. Days easily turned into weeks here.

If you asked me what the highlight of all my travels was, I would be the time I spent on Ko Lipe. I made incredible friends, lounged around, got to know the locals, learned a bit of Thai, and, overall, lived that idyllic backpacker life we all dream about.

Over the years, I’ve avoided going back to Ko Lipe because the memory of my time there is so strong that I didn’t want to ruin it. Any re-visit would simply be trying to recreated a magic that couldn’t be recreated because the people that it special wouldn’t be there. I’d be chasing travel ghosts. And, since I know my sleepy paradise has been developed greatly over the years, I was also too afraid seeing that would make me sad.

Tourism in Thailand tends toward the unsustainable. No island really develops in a good way. It’s all build, build, build.

And I didn’t want to see my Ko Lipe like that.

But as I was planning my recent trip through Southeast Asia, returning to Ko Lipe made sense. I was heading down the Indian Ocean side of Thailand on my way into Malaysia and I’d pass by it.

And, since I was looking for a lively place for New Year’s Eve, it seemed liked the best choice. I knew there would be travelers there and there were no other nearby islands that would work, especially since Ko Lipe has a boat to Langkawi, which was my next stop.

And I’m sad to report that Ko Lipe took the Ko Phi Phi model of tourism and is now extremely overdeveloped.

Most of the island is now paved over, the old dirt footpaths having become concrete for the cars and construction trucks. Swaths of palm trees are now the sites of high-end resorts with pools (on an island with no natural water supply). Construction of more resorts continues at a fast pace. The coral around the island is dying, a victim of all the boats, anchors, pollution, and overfishing. Beaches are now lined with boats, their exhaust spilling into the ocean, leaving a shiny film you can see as you swim. And the restaurants cater to tourists looking for bad Western food, not great Thai cuisine.

The island’s boom has displaced many locals, who were forced to sell to mainland developers, and much of the island’s workforce is now from the mainland. They see little of the benefits this tourism boom.

So lies Ko Lipe, another victim of Thailand’s all too common overdevelopment and exploitation of limited resources.

I met lots of people there who loved the island. If it’s your first time, I can see why you would love it. After all, the area is postcard perfect, the water is perfectly an azure blue, the sand is a beautiful white, and since you’re surrounded by a national park, a lot of tours take you to some secluded islands.

And, in comparison to Ko Phi Phi, Krabi, or Phuket, it is less developed so I can’t fault someone stepping here for the first time going “wow!”

But, as I reflect on the island and its overdevelopment, I have come to the same conclusion I have about Ko Phi Phi: people shouldn’t visit.

I’m not against growth, but I’m against this kind of growth. It’s not sustainably managed and going there will only tax the island’s limited resources even further. You can’t put the genie back in the bottle and no local is going to say “sure, I’ll stay broke so you can an idealized vision of the world.”

And, with so many other islands to visit that are well managed (Ko Lanta, Ko Jum, and Ko Mook, to name three nearby), I think you should skip Ko Lipe.

A visit there will only make things worse.

It pains me to say that, because it was such a beautiful place, and my original visit had a huge impact on my life. But if we’re going to be good stewards and travelers, sometimes you just have to say enough is enough.

And Ko Lipe is a place where enough is enough.

Go somewhere else that is better managed.

Riding elephants in Thailand went away when consumers became more conscious of it. Eco lodges got big because of consumers. Overtourism is talked about by consumers as much as it is by locals.

Maybe if enough people start to do something, Ko Lipe will change.

But, at the very least, by not going you are at least no contributing to the problem.

#3067. How are storms named?

Have you ever wondered why and how storms are named? It may feel like a random process, but storm names follow a strict international system. Naming storms has been found to increase awareness and encourage people to prepare if needed. It’s also easier for news reporters to use a single name rather than a longer, less memorable name composed of scientific terms.

The naming process depends on the type of storm and its location.

Tropical storm and hurricane names are maintained by the World Meteorological Organisation, which uses six alphabetical lists that rotate every six years.

The first storm of the year starts with an A, the second wth a B and so on through the year. Names alternate between male and female and have done so since 1979.

Q, U, X, Y, and Z are usually missed out because there aren’t enough names starting with those letters.

Names of particularly severe storms are retired to avoid confusion in subsequent years and as a sign of respect.

The Atlantic list of storm names for 2025 has been released and includes Dexter, Jerry and Melissa.

Storms in the UK are named only when they are expected to have a medium to high impact, typically associated with an Amber or Red Weather Warning.

Financial markets are wrong to predict the Bank of England will raise UK interest rates by a full percentage point or more by next summer, City economists believe.

According to market-implied interest rate expectations, the Bank is likely to raise interest rates at least four times over the next year, lifting the bank rate from 3.75% today to 4.75% by the end of July, and possibly to 5% – meaning five rises – by December 2027.

But a number of City experts believe such a sharp tightening cycle is unlikely, given there is little sign that the rise in energy costs is pushing up other prices sharply.

“If you look at the swaps market, and you look at what’s priced in for the Bank of England, you’ve got four rate hikes and then no cuts ever. And I think you’d be hard-pressed to find anybody that really agrees with that,” said James Smith, a developed markets economist at the Dutch bank ING.

“By next spring, I think they [the Bank] could be sounding the all clear, and by the end of next year they could be cutting rates again,” Smith told a briefing in the City last week.

Market-implied interest rates are calculated from the prices of a range of financial instruments, including derivatives such as “swaps”, to establish where traders think interest rates will be in future.

Economists cite several reasons why market-implied interest rates expectations are high. One is that these expectations have been closely correlated with oil prices since the Iran war began.

The higher oil prices have risen, the more rate increases have been priced in. An investor could be right that interest rates won’t rise as far as markets think, but if oil prices keep increasing in the meantime, they would face mark-to-market losses and potentially margin calls.

“It can get pretty painful, so a lot of traders are quite reluctant to take this risk until there’s greater certainty about energy supply and the extent of inflation this winter,” said Smith.

Many traders got burnt at the start of the current Middle East crisis, when most were positioned for further rate cuts this year, he said.

Sanjay Raja, the chief UK economist at Deutsche Bank, also doubts that the Bank will raise rates as fast as the market pricing suggests.

“I do not believe that any mainstream forecaster thinks the Bank of England will hike to 5% by the end of next year with the information set we have today,” Raja said. “It’s not to say it won’t happen. But based on current projections, it seems less likely.”

He has calculated that a lot of the forecast interest rate rises is actually “baked-in risk premia” – where investors require an extra return to protect them from uncertainty or instability.

I was growing a bit tired of the NYC dating scene, the rising cost of living, and felt like, creatively, I was in a rut. With AI coming for creators like me and the industry changing, I was a little lost on what, career-wise, would come next.

And Paris seemed like the best place to go.

It’s cheaper than NYC, I had friends there already, I’ve always wanted to learn French, it would make a great base for exploring Europe, and I could start writing my next book there. There’s nothing like a change in scenery to get the creativity flowing!

But the question remained: how do you move to France?

After all, they don’t have a digital nomad visa, and you’re limited to three months if you visit on your regular Schengen tourist visa.

Well, it turns out that it’s not actually that hard. (And while I can only speak for Americans, I suspect the criteria are similar for Canadians, Aussies, Kiwis, and other “developed” nations.)

If you want to move to France, you have four main options:

The student visa is pretty straightforward. You need to enroll in a full-time university program and show you have enough funds to support yourself (around 600 Euros per month). A friend is currently there on this visa, as she is doing a nine-month intensive French program to become fluent. You still have to apply for the visa, but if you’re enrolled in an accredited school, you’ll likely get approved. Another benefit to this visa is that you can work part-time on it!

But this visa is contingent on being in school, so if you leave or quit, it’s voided. However, when you are done, you can change this to a “job seeker” visa, which can give you up to another six months in France (or more depending on how long your program was).

I thought about applying for the entrepreneur visa, but the application process is pretty complex and can take months to be approved. You have to set up your business in France — and that is a lot of paperwork. You have to show that your business makes money, has clients (at least one of them being French), and can support you full-time (you have to make at least the French minimum wage). They will scrutinize your assets and financials a lot and you’ll need to register your business in France and pay French taxes.

Additionally, France offers a talent visa. If you’re an expert in your field (with verifiable accreditations and accolades), this could be a good visa to apply for, especially if you plan to stay in France for a long time and want to physically work there. But, again, you’ll need to have some source of income and plan to do physical business in France. If you’re not an academic but someone in the arts, you have to show how you are going to add to the “culture of France” in some way.

While I would meet the requirements for both those visas, the process for either would have been time-consuming, and, since I’m not sure France is going to be my forever home, I decided not to go through that process.

So I went with the long-term visitor visa, officially called VLS-TS visiteur. This allows me to stay up to 12 months in France and is renewable in the country. It does come with a lot of restrictions, however: I can’t physically work here and I am not allowed into the French social welfare system.

It’s also the visa a lot of Americans (and most retirees) are coming in on. If you have passive income or retirement savings and just want to live in France, this is a good option for you. It allows you to set up a bank account and it can be renewed pretty much indefinitely.

The paperwork for this visa (which I’ll get into in a bit) essentially comes down to whether or not you can support yourself. French authorities want to make sure you won’t be a burden on the system.

My biggest question centered around whether or not I could “work” on this visa. A lot of creators and digital nomads are applying for — and getting — this visa.

But remember: you aren’t allowed to work on this visa so how are you going to work if you can’t work?

So let me take a moment to talk about “work” from a legal standpoint.

As I mentioned, there’s no digital nomad visa that allows you to work in France. And the French tax office said last July that remote work is considered taxable — but there are no official laws that say so. That’s just their opinion and there’s been no progress in codifying that opinion. In fact, my visa doesn’t even come with a tax number, so I can’t be taxed. I couldn’t pay taxes even if I wanted to.

The visa office asks how you’ll support yourself when you are in France and one of the sources of income they accept is “income from work.” I was very open that my means of support would be the income from this blog and my books.

The system there really hasn’t caught up to remote work. It still defines “work” as something you do physically that could take away a job from a French person.

So could I run walking tours in Paris? Nope. Could you work at a café? Nope.

But does writing this blog post count as work? After all, no one is paying for it. It’s a free resource. Does going to a café to work on the next great American novel count? What about my weekly newsletter that has affiliate links (that generate income) in it? Is writing that considered work?

The gray area is considerable, so the best thing to do is to talk to a lawyer. I recommend Daniel Tostado (yes, that’s his real name), one of the biggest American-French immigration lawyers in Paris. He has a huge law firm and regularly meets with government officials to clarify the rules on this. He gave me some solid advice.

For this long-term visitor visa, the application process is pretty easy. You go to the French government website, fill out your application, take the application number to the TLScontact website, and make an appointment. (France outsources the appointment process to this company, which then sends everything to the French consulate for approval.)

The main things you’ll want to demonstrate are that you have accommodation for 90 days on your application and enough income to support yourself. They just want to make sure you aren’t going to be a burden on the system. I went overboard. Here’s what I brought to my appointment:

And another friend who got this visa didn’t bring anything beyond what was asked in the application form. But I wanted to take no chances!

At the appointment, the staff double-checks your paperwork, takes your biometric data, collects the fees, and then sends your passport to the embassy for processing. Officially, it takes up to 15 days to process your visa, but everyone I know seems to get it back within a week.

The worst part was not knowing if you are approved until your passport is returned. While you can track the progress of your application, you don’t know the verdict until your passport is back in your hands. While I was waiting (and anxiously spiraling), I looked on Reddit and found that most denials were for lack of income or issues with their accommodation, which seem to be the two most important things to have perfect on your application.

Once you get your visa back, you can enter France any time after the start date. After you enter, you have 90 days to register your visa with the state (they give you a little QR code with instructions). This allows you to “officially” be on this long-term visa. (It’s a more paperwork kind of thing.)

Once that is done, there’s nothing else to do and you can stay in France (and Europe) until your visa expires!

Dr. Mario Vega Croker, Medical Director of Panama Fertility located in Panama City, Panama, has noticed more Americans knocking on his door. #3097. When is the Earth closest to the sun?

Did you know that the Earth is about 3 million miles closer to the sun in winter than in summer?

This year (2026), Earth is closest to the Sun on Saturday, 3rd January. This specific point in the Earth’s orbit is called perihelion.

The point in Earth’s orbit at which the Sun is farthest from Earth is called aphelion.

In January, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun, which means sunlight hits the ground at a shallower angle than when the Earth is tilted towards the Sun. The shallow angle means that the sun’s heat is spread out as it travels through the atmosphere, making the weather colder.

The Southern Hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun in January, which is why it experiences summer during perihelion in the Northern Hemisphere.

Fun Fact: The Earth moves faster when it’s closest to the Sun (because of gravity), so winter in the Northern Hemisphere is about 5 days shorter than summer!
