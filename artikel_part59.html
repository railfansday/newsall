#2901. Parachute Egg Drop Experiment – Gravity and Air Resistance

This fun parachute egg drop experiment is a great demonstration of the forces acting on parachutes. If you drop something, it falls to the ground. This is because it is pulled by the gravity of the Earth. You’ll notice that some things drop faster than others. This is because of air resistance. Try dropping a piece of paper and a lego brick. Which drops the fastest?

We are going to try dropping an egg on its own, dropping an egg attached to a parachute and an egg in a basket under a balloon, to see which falls the slowest.

Other investigation ideas are to experiment with different size parachutes or using different materials for a parachute.

Remember to only change one variable and keep all oher conditions ( for example, height the egg is dropped from, the same ).

Drop an egg on its own, the egg in the basket and the egg in the parachute from somewhere high up. Make sure an adult is around to help with this part.

If you tried dropping a piece of paper and a LEGO brick, the paper should have fallen more slowly than the brick. This is because the paper has a larger surface area, so it pushes against more air as it falls, resulting in greater air resistance and a slower descent.

An egg dropped without anything to slow it down will fall quickly and break. A parachute and a balloon add air resistance, slowing the fall and preventing the egg from breaking.

We found that the parachute fell much more slowly than the balloon. This is because the parachute has a larger surface area than the balloon, so it slows the egg’s descent more.

If we dropped a hammer and a feather, we would expect the hammer to fall faster. However, if we did this on the moon, where there is no air resistance, they would hit the ground at the same time!

As we explained above, two forces act on an object as it falls. Gravity pulls the object down, and air resistance slows the fall.

Parachutes are used to slow the fall of an object by increasing air resistance, which reduces the effect of gravity!

Record the time taken for all three to drop and see how much slower the parachute is.

Experiment with different sizes of parachutes and see which drops more slowly.

#2904. People are taking an unapproved weight-loss drug. AI found something surprising

An experimental weight-loss drug that has not yet received regulatory approval is already attracting users willing to obtain unapproved versions online. Now, scientists are using artificial intelligence to investigate their experiences, uncovering unexpected symptom reports that differ from those documented in clinical trials.

The drug, retatrutide, belongs to a new generation of GLP-1 medications designed to help regulate appetite and body weight. Although it remains under investigation and is not approved for use, people are purchasing unapproved formulations that are not intended for human consumption and discussing their experiences on internet forums.

Researchers at the University of Pennsylvania analyzed nearly 150,000 Reddit posts and comments from approximately 40,000 users to investigate these accounts. Their study, published in JAMA Network Open, provides the first systematic examination of potential side effects reported by people claiming to use unapproved retatrutide.

One finding stood out. Among users whose reported symptoms could be classified, increased appetite was the most common complaint. That was particularly unexpected because retatrutide is designed to suppress hunger, and its Phase 2 clinical trials primarily identified digestive problems as the most frequent side effects.

AI Uncovers Unexpected Weight-Loss Drug Reports

"Our goal is to identify hypotheses in online conversations that we could test. AI helps us find recurring patterns in people's self-reports, including experiences that may be missed by conventional drug-safety monitoring," says Sharath Chandra Guntuku, Research Associate Professor in Computer and Information Science (CIS), Director of Penn's Computational Social Listening Lab and senior author of the study.

However, the researchers emphasize that the findings should not be interpreted as proof of previously unknown side effects. Reddit users represent a very different population from clinical trial participants, and their accounts cannot establish whether the drug caused the symptoms they described.

There is also considerable uncertainty about what these individuals are actually taking. Products sold outside regulated medical channels may differ from the genuine experimental medication in their ingredients, purity, and dosage.

"We can't say that retatrutide is causing these symptoms, or even that Reddit users are actually taking genuine retatrutide, since the drug is not legally available, and gray market formulations vary," says Lyle Ungar, Professor in CIS and a co-author of the study.

Rather than confirming new risks, the research offers scientists an opportunity to identify questions that warrant more rigorous investigation.

"This is the first glimpse into the experiences of a population that is otherwise outside conventional drug safety monitoring because they are taking unapproved versions of a drug that has yet to finish clinical trials," says Neil Sehgal, the study's first author and a doctoral student in CIS.

How Researchers Analyzed 150,000 Reddit Posts

The project builds on previous research by the University of Pennsylvania team. In that earlier investigation, scientists used large language models (LLMs), the type of artificial intelligence that powers many modern chatbots, to examine more than 400,000 Reddit posts discussing semaglutide and tirzepatide.

That analysis revealed reports of possible side effects, including menstrual irregularities, that may not have been fully documented in clinical trials or official drug safety records.

"We wanted to apply the same methodology to gray-market retatrutide," says Sehgal.

Retatrutide is particularly interesting because it works differently from existing GLP-1 medications. Semaglutide acts on one hormone receptor involved in blood sugar control and appetite regulation, while tirzepatide targets two. Retatrutide activates three receptors, potentially producing a different combination of effects throughout the body.

To investigate what people were experiencing, the researchers used AI to examine nearly 150,000 Reddit posts and comments. They identified approximately 14,000 users who claimed to have personally taken an unapproved version of retatrutide.

The AI systems then extracted descriptions of symptoms that users associated with the medication and translated those descriptions into standardized medical terminology. This allowed researchers to compare reports even when different people used different words to describe similar experiences.

After excluding expected effects such as weight loss and reduced appetite, the team identified 7,823 users who reported at least one symptom that could be medically classified.

"A researcher might be able to examine a few thousand Reddit posts by hand, but looking at nearly 150,000 would be an enormous undertaking," says Sehgal. "AI makes it possible to analyze these conversations at scale, identifying patterns in what people are sharing that would otherwise be difficult to recognize."

The most striking difference involved hunger.

In retatrutide's Phase 2 clinical trials (the drug is currently in Phase 3 trials, the final step before FDA approval), participants most commonly experienced gastrointestinal complaints, including nausea. These effects are familiar concerns with medications that influence appetite and digestion.

But among Reddit users who reported taking unapproved retatrutide, the pattern looked different. Increased appetite emerged as the most frequently described classifiable symptom, despite the substantial weight-loss effects demonstrated in clinical testing.

The researchers also compared the new findings with their earlier analysis of semaglutide and tirzepatide.

Compared with those earlier Reddit reports, discussions involving unapproved retatrutide contained proportionally more mentions of appetite-related changes, sleep disturbances, increased heart rate, and muscle-related symptoms. Reports of nausea and other digestive complaints were proportionally less common.

These differences could reflect how retatrutide interacts with the body. However, they could also result from differences between the groups of people discussing the medications, their other drug use, or the contents of the unapproved products.

"Retatrutide works differently from existing GLP-1 drugs, so it's possible that its effects on the body are different, too," says Jena Shaw Tronieri, Senior Research Investigator at Penn's Center for Weight and Eating Disorders and a co-author of the study.

"But the people discussing it on Reddit also may be different," she notes. "Users willing to obtain an unapproved medication on the gray market likely differ from other clinical populations. Some may be taking multiple experimental peptides, and we can't verify exactly what they're taking. We need more research to understand what, if anything, these differences in self-reported symptoms mean."

Can AI Detect Drug Safety Concerns Earlier?

Beyond the findings about retatrutide, the study demonstrates how AI could potentially improve the early detection of drug safety concerns.

Traditional monitoring systems depend heavily on clinical trials, reports submitted to regulators, and information collected by healthcare professionals. People obtaining experimental medications through unapproved channels may not be represented in those systems.

Social media offers another source of information, allowing researchers to identify recurring experiences that might otherwise escape attention. However, these reports must be independently investigated before they can be considered reliable evidence of a drug-related problem.

The Penn team has already seen an example of how this approach can help guide further research.

In their previous investigation of semaglutide and tirzepatide, researchers identified online reports of menstrual irregularities. Those findings subsequently encouraged another scientific team to examine similar symptoms using the FDA's database of reported adverse drug events.

The follow-up study, published in Obstetrics & Gynecology, also detected disproportionate reports of certain menstrual changes. Although those findings did not establish causation, they provided additional reasons to investigate the potential connection.

The researchers hope their retatrutide findings will lead to similar independent studies, particularly as the drug advances through clinical development.

"That's what we hope will happen with this study," says Sehgal. "Other researchers can take what we find on Reddit and look for the same patterns in other datasets, using entirely different methods. Whether those signals hold up or not, the goal is to build a better understanding of what people are actually experiencing."

#2906. Whitsundays Long Weekend: What Filipinos in Australia Should Plan

Most Filipinos in Australia save their annual leave for one trip, usually in December. Three weeks home, a long list of relatives, and by the time you land back in Sydney or Melbourne, the leave balance is empty and long weekends are all you have left for the rest of the year.

The three days never feel like enough to plan a proper trip around, so most of those weekends get spent at home. The Whitsundays answer that better than almost anywhere else in the country. A group of 74 islands off the north Queensland coast, with water clear enough to see coral from the surface. No passport or visa complications. You book a flight, a room and one boat trip, and the planning is finished.

Three days also happens to be the right length for a Whitsundays tour. Fly out Friday morning, spend Saturday on the Great Barrier Reef, and you are home by Sunday evening with a holiday behind you and your annual leave still sitting there untouched.

Because Hamilton Island holds the whole trip. Three days is a tight window, and none of it goes on transfers, because nothing here is further than a short walk or a free shuttle.

There is nothing foreign to manage either. No currency to exchange, and your phone plan and Medicare card still work. A three-day Whitsundays holiday does not feel like a rushed version of a real one. And the best part? You only have to do minimal planning.

Any long weekend works, but some are better value than others. School holidays push flights and rooms up. Skip them if your dates can move.

Fly to Hamilton Island. It is the only island here with a commercial airport, so the whole journey is one flight and a ten-minute shuttle to your room.

There is a cheaper option. Fly to Proserpine on the mainland, take a coach to Airlie Beach, then a ferry to the island. That adds about three hours each way, so it is not worth it on a long weekend.

There are two bases. Hamilton Island is the easier one, Airlie Beach is the cheaper one, and for a long weekend the easier one usually wins.

Hamilton Island is a resort island, so there is no travel left once you land. Rooms run from apartments with kitchens up to resort rooms at Catseye Beach. There are no private cars here, so everyone walks, takes a golf buggy, or catches the free shuttle that loops every ten to fifteen minutes. Hamilton Island cruises leave from the marina, a short walk or one shuttle ride from your room. With kids or older parents along, this is the one to book. Rooms, beaches, and the marina sit close together, so you don’t need to pack and travel all day. If your parents are flying in from the Philippines, our guide on how to get an Australia tourist visa for Filipinos covers their application.

Airlie Beach costs noticeably less, with more hostels, self-contained units and restaurants per block. The catch is a ferry every time you want to be on an island, which eats into three days.

With Hamilton Island as your base, a Whitsundays island tour needs no transfers. A day to arrive, one for the reef, one for the island itself. Book the reef day first, since it leaves at a fixed time, then fit the other two around it.

You will land mid-morning or early afternoon, so keep this day light. Drop your bags and spend the afternoon close to where you are staying.

This is a long day, and it runs from early morning to early evening. Eat a proper breakfast, apply sunscreen and bring a hat.

In-water guides go out with each group, so first-time snorkellers are never on their own. Children from five years old can come. There is no alcohol on board, since the vessel is not licensed. Save that for dinner.

The last day belongs to the island itself. A hike, a long breakfast and a buggy. Work backwards from your flight time. The airport is ten minutes away; the hike up Passage Peak is not.

Put the two room rows side by side and Airlie Beach comes out a couple of hundred dollars a night cheaper, which seems practical till you add a ferry crossing each way, every day. On a three-day trip, that saving buys less than it looks like, because the thing you run short of here is time, not money.

Most long weekends end in a small regret for the beach you never reached, the lookout you ran past and all the other spots you missed in the rush. Hamilton Island is small, but there is more on it than its size suggests and three days cover it properly. You fly home knowing the weekend paid you back.

A fire in the Brazilian Amazon. Carl de Souza / AFP / Getty Images

According to new research, such feedback loops could amplify human-driven warming by 20 to 30 percent.

#2914. Astronauts’ hearts just passed a surprising test for Mars

A journey to Mars may be less challenging for the human heart than scientists once feared. A new study suggests that astronauts can adapt remarkably well to months of weightlessness, offering encouraging evidence that humans could endure the long trip to the Red Planet and function under its weaker gravity.

Researchers from Northwestern Medicine, UT Southwestern, and UT Houston monitored the hearts of 13 astronauts who spent approximately six months aboard the International Space Station. Their findings showed that although spaceflight initially affected heart performance, the astronauts gradually adapted, with intensive daily exercise playing an important role in maintaining their cardiovascular health.

One of NASA's major concerns about sending humans to Mars is how astronauts' hearts and circulatory systems will respond when they leave the weightless conditions of spaceflight (known as microgravity) and encounter Martian gravity, which is only about one-third as strong as Earth's.

Encouragingly, the researchers found that the astronauts showed no significant signs of cardiovascular strain when exposed to simulated Martian gravity after returning to Earth.

"With our findings, there is now reason to think that we would be able to traverse to Mars, get there and actually work on the surface of Mars," said study senior author Dr. James Thomas, professor of medicine in the division of cardiology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine.

The study, published last month in the journal Circulation, is the first to investigate how the heart responds to simulated Martian gravity following an extended period in space.

How Daily Exercise Protects Astronauts' Hearts in Space

Living without gravity creates unusual challenges for the human body. On Earth, the heart constantly works against gravity to circulate blood, while muscles must support the body's weight. In space, those demands decrease substantially, allowing muscles and even the heart itself to shrink over time.

Returning to a gravitational environment can also be difficult. Astronauts on previous missions have frequently experienced dizziness or lightheadedness when standing after landing. This happens because gravity pulls blood toward the lower body, sometimes causing blood pressure to drop suddenly. The condition is called orthostatic hypotension. (If you've seen Sandra Bullock crawling after returning to Earth in "Gravity," you get the idea.)

However, the astronauts involved in the new study generally handled this transition well, potentially because they had maintained demanding exercise schedules throughout their missions.

As part of NASA's Exercise Countermeasures program, astronauts exercised for up to two hours each day. Their routines included 30 to 45 minutes of endurance training, which strengthens the cardiovascular system, along with 60 to 75 minutes of resistance training to help preserve muscle mass and strength.

"You lose muscle mass and cardiac mass when you're in a weightless environment because you are working less up there," explained Thomas, who also is a Northwestern Medicine cardiologist. "You're floating around in the air, and you give a little tap with your foot, and you can sail over to say hello to your buddy."

"To counter that, exercise is crucial when you're in space," Thomas added.

Heart Scans Reveal How the Body Adapts to Weightlessness

To understand what happens to the heart during prolonged spaceflight, the researchers collected echocardiograms before, during, and after each astronaut's mission. These ultrasound scans use sound waves to produce moving images of the heart, allowing doctors to examine its structure and measure how effectively it pumps blood.

While aboard the International Space Station, astronauts were trained to perform these scans on one another every few weeks. The recordings were transmitted to Earth, where researchers could monitor changes in heart function throughout the missions.

Thomas worked alongside Dr. Benjamin Levine of UT Southwestern Medical Center and Dr. Michael Bungo of the McGovern Medical School at UTHealth Houston.

"It would be typically about 3 a.m. for me because the space station runs on Greenwich Mean Time," Thomas said. "I would watch the recordings come in live and coach the astronauts in real time to improve the images."

The scans revealed that the astronauts' hearts initially struggled to adjust to their new environment. During the first two weeks in space, researchers observed declines in cardiac filling, stroke volume, and several other measures of cardiovascular performance.

Cardiac filling refers to how much blood enters the heart between beats, while stroke volume describes the amount of blood pumped out with each contraction. Both measurements decreased early in the missions, indicating that the astronauts' hearts were temporarily moving less blood with each beat.

As the astronauts spent more time in space, their cardiovascular systems adapted. Cardiac filling and stroke volume gradually improved, approaching the levels recorded before launch.

These results suggest that the heart can adjust to prolonged weightlessness more effectively than early measurements might indicate, particularly when astronauts maintain regular physical activity.

Astronauts' Hearts Pass a Simulated Mars Gravity Test

The researchers also wanted to answer a particularly important question for future Mars missions: What happens when an astronaut who has spent months in weightlessness suddenly encounters gravity again?

Mars has roughly 38% of Earth's surface gravity. Although this is considerably weaker than what people experience on Earth, it would still force astronauts' hearts to work differently after months of floating in space.

To reproduce some of these effects, the researchers conducted a simulated Martian gravity test after the astronauts returned to Earth.

Each astronaut was tilted upward by 22 degrees from a lying position. This posture caused blood to shift toward the lower body, approximating the circulatory effects of Martian gravity.

The results were encouraging. After spending about six months in space, the astronauts' hearts performed just as well under simulated Martian gravity as they had when standing upright on Earth before their missions.

This suggests that astronauts who maintain their cardiovascular fitness during spaceflight may be capable of transitioning to the weaker gravity of Mars without experiencing major cardiovascular difficulties.

Although the findings come from a relatively small group of astronauts and a simulation rather than an actual Mars landing, they provide valuable evidence for planning future missions.

Combined with the benefits of daily exercise, the heart's ability to adapt to weightlessness and respond well to simulated Martian gravity offers another reason to be optimistic about sending humans to the Red Planet.

#2918. Zone Fun Training

There’s lots of talk about Zone 1, 2, 3, and 4 cardio, with each “zone” based on the level of aerobic or anaerobic energy you’re utilizing. The lower the zone, the more aerobic you are and the more fat you’re burning as percentage of total energy. The higher the zone, the more anaerobic you are the more glucose and glycogen you’re burning. Lower zones are easier and slower. Higher zones are harder and faster.

This is a sound way to think about “cardio,” but it doesn’t cover every zone. There’s one big training zone missing from the conversation: Zone Fun.

In Zone Fun, intensity doesn’t matter. It can be high or low. You can go fast or slow. Intense or easy. Zone Fun oscillates across all the heart rate zones. Because the prime concern and determinant is whether or not you’re having fun, the other stuff melts away. Not in importance—all the aerobic zones still matter, intensity still matters—but in what you have to consciously pay attention to.

You can just keep going as long as you’re having fun and moving your body.

I’d go so far as to say that hitting Zone Fun is the most important metric for your cardiovascular training. If you reach Zone Fun, if you’re moving fast and slow and medium and moving with intensity and then slowing things down, you are doing the right thing. You are improving your health and fitness. Having fun is a good barometer for doing the right thing health wise. Your body intrinsically “knows” when you’re improving skills, increasing VO2max, building an aerobic base and your ability to tolerate anaerobic discomfort, strengthening muscle—fun is the tell.

We have evidence that Zone Fun training is even more effective than other kinds. When you subject rodents in the lab to forced exercise—sticking them on a rodent wheel, making them tread water, tying weights to their tails and making them drag them and climb ladders—you see moderately beneficial effects, but sometimes negative effects. When you give rodents the opportunity to engage in voluntary exercise like free access to a wheel, and they choose to do it, the benefits are greater. For instance, one study found that mice doing a ten week voluntary running program had more favorable shifts in muscle tissue fiber development than mice doing ten weeks of forced running.1 Another even found that mice with colitis had higher mortality and inflammation when they were forced to exercise and lower inflammation and mortality when they chose to exercise.2

Okay, so what are some ways to practice Zone Fun training?

My favorite way to get into Zone Fun is with the greatest sport of all time: Ultimate Frisbee. Sadly, I don’t get to do this as often as I’d like to. But when I lived in Malibu, I had a weekly game set aside with the same group of guys. It was a mix of running, jumping, throwing, jostling for positioning, sprinting, and jogging. Most importantly, it was fun. It was a game. It was a game requiring strategy, planning, and intense concentration. Because when you’re concentrating or when you’re thinking ahead and thinking in the moment, you’re having fun. You’re not thinking about the hard work that you’re doing. You don’t even realize that you’re breathing hard, that you’re starting to get sore. You don’t notice the burning. And therein lies the beauty of Zone Fun.

I’ve taken fitness influencers out on the sand that are half my age and they always tell me it’s the hardest workout of their lives and can barely keep up. No joke. But the thing is, you keep going because riding through sand next to the beautiful water, seeing all the lovely sights that one sees, invigorates and makes the work worth it.

Paddling is my other method of Zone Fun training. Up on the paddleboard, I get into my rhythm and flow, and the hours just melt away. I'm never quite breathing hard. My serratus anterior muscles are getting an incredible workout (there's no better ab exercise). My heart is working, the blood is flowing, and the fat is burning. This is a gentle form of training.

The perfect way to access Zone Fun training is to play sports.

Pick a sport that you used to really enjoy when you were younger. The trick to training is that it shouldn’t feel like work. You also shouldn’t feel like you’re learning to play. You should come into it fairly naturally. So, if you’re playing basketball, you should be able to dribble the ball, make a layup, and generally feel comfortable with the flow of the game. If you’re trying to pick up a game of soccer, make sure that you’re comfortable handling the ball, passing, shooting, and generally move with the way the game moves. And that’s true for anything. You don’t want to be learning the fundamentals when you’re trying to engage in Zone Fun. You don’t have to be an all-star, but you shouldn’t be a total beginner.

Another way to use Zone Fun training is to take what might normally be a boring form of movement and make it more interesting and fun. For example, instead of running on a treadmill, go for a trail run. Changing topography, textural shifts, rising and climbing the trail, plus all the plants and animals and beautiful smells around you, will make what’s normally a boring plod around the track much more interesting and fun. Deep into the endurance world, I did most of my training on trails and mountain passes just because it was so much more interesting than just running around the track or on a treadmill. It was the only way I could make the incredibly uncomfortable training bearable.

Another substitution might be mountain biking instead of road biking. Insead of zoning out on a road, you have to constantly stay alert for roots, rocks, jumps, drops, and trees. You have to stay immersed in the moment—you can’t just bear down and suffer like you can on the road. This gives you just as good a workout without feeling like it.

Rock scrambling is my absolute favorite way to traverse the wild. I grew up in a small fishing town in Maine, scrambling all over the rocks that lined the coast. I think this was how I got most of my movement and training foundation. There's something so satisfying about scrambling on all fours up extremely steep rock faces. It’s just pure fun and I think it triggers something primordial within us.

Here's what I mean by loaded partner carries:

Get a group of at least two people together. Choose a walking path, ideally with an incline. Up a mountain is best. Then, pick a heavy object, at least one per person. If you have two people, choose 1-2 objects. If you're a group of three, choose 2-3 objects. And then you go on the walk while carrying the objects and switching off with each other.

A kettlebell and a weighted sandbag. A big log and a heavy rock. A pair of dumbbells. A barbell and a rucksack. You get the idea. Just go on the walk with a friend or friends, taking turns carrying the objects you brought. This is very hard work, but because you're working together, it's fun. You have each other to lean on and nobody wants to let the other people down. Turns out that camaraderie is extremely motivating.

Those are my favorite ways to train Zone Fun. What about yours? How will you incorporate more Zone Fun training into your regimen?

#2921. Sargassum Superbloom: Seaweed Scourge or Ecological Boon?

Sargassum seaweed off the coast of St. Martin in the Caribbean. VELY Michel via Wikipedia

Stretching from West Africa to the Gulf of Mexico, a massive bloom of sargassum seaweed is swamping beaches and killing coastal wildlife. But scientists have discovered the floating algae also shelters a vast array of ocean creatures, and some are now calling for its protection.

Many of America’s favorite beaches in Florida and the Caribbean have been piled high with foul-smelling seaweed again this summer, often in record amounts. What disappointed tourists may not know is that the inundations, which also smother coral reefs, mangroves, and seagrasses, are just the most visible sign of a vast new marine ecosystem — an expanse of floating sargassum that since 2011 has seemingly come from nowhere to spread in a 5,500-mile-long belt from Mexico to West Africa and Florida to Brazil.

The dense mats of offshore weed grow up to 20 feet thick, and this summer reached a record weight of 38 million metric tons — more than the total weight of all Americans.

The seaweed scourge is wrecking coastal economies in countries heavily dependent on tourism and fishing. The coastlines of 30 countries across the Caribbean and beyond are being visited by the brown mats piling up on beaches, where the weed decomposes, releasing gases such as hydrogen sulfide, with its distinctive rotten-egg smell, and ammonia, which trigger asthma attacks and exacerbate other respiratory conditions. Thousands of victims have been reported by doctors in the Caribbean, especially among workers clearing beaches.

The weed also blocks water intakes and prevents fishers from taking their boats to sea, as well as clogging their nets, engines, and propellers. It has caused mass deaths of marine life on coastlines, while preventing sea turtles from laying their eggs on beaches and blocking hatchlings trying to return to the ocean. The weed also accumulates heavy metals such as arsenic, which leaches out as the weed decomposes on land and can poison water supplies.

For such reasons, most ecologists regard the belt as a menace.

But, while acknowledging its onshore harms, some researchers are now recognizing the Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt as a significant new habitat for marine life in the open ocean, and a valuable carbon sink for the planet. According to Jonathan Jung, a marine ecologist at the Max Planck Institute for Chemistry in Mainz, Germany, the weed and its growing army of inhabitants now form “the largest interconnected floating biome on Earth.”

Sargassum seaweed in the Atlantic was once mostly confined to the Sargasso Sea, an area of sluggish waters off Bermuda largely cut off from the rest of the ocean by rotating ocean currents. It has been well-known to mariners since Christopher Columbus reported sailing through “so much grass that it looked like the sea was curdled with it.” A vital nursery for fish and turtles, the sea is now confirmed to be the breeding and spawning ground of both American and European eels.

A map of the Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt in June 2026. NASA / Adapted by Yale Environment 360

But in 2011, weed from the Sargasso Sea abruptly spread south, driven by anomalous winds generated by an unusually long negative phase of an atmospheric cycle known as the North Atlantic Oscillation, or NAO, in which changes in relative air pressure in the Atlantic alter wind directions. The floating mass was then sustained by upwelling nitrogen and phosphorus. These nutrients usually lurk at depths below a thousand feet but were brought to the surface by ocean mixing created by the unusual winds.

The belt of weed swiftly spread across huge swaths of the tropical Atlantic. And there it has stayed, despite the end of the NAO anomaly and the abnormal ocean mixing.

Many mechanisms have been proposed for this unexpected persistence. Perhaps deforestation along the path of the two great rivers flowing into the tropical Atlantic — the Amazon in Brazil and the Congo in Africa — provides a continuous flush of nutrients from soils. Saharan dust is another potential source.

But in a paper published in April, Annalisa Bracco, an oceanographer at the CMCC Foundation, a research center in Milan, and colleagues modeled nutrient flows in the ocean and concluded that such theories did not fit either nutrient fluxes or the timing of events. Instead, their modeling showed how the rich ecosystem that swiftly developed around the sargassum continuously recycles the nutrients originally brought to the surface by the mixing.

“The ecosystem is now feeding on itself,” Bracco says. The belt “is a striking example of how the ocean can reorganize itself very quickly. What started as a wind-driven event has become a self-sustaining biological system.”

Scientists remain divided about whether climate change played a role in the belt’s emergence. Some climate models suggest a warmer world is pushing the NAO to more extreme phases, such as those in 2010 and 2011. In any case, it is likely that ocean temperatures will matter in the future. Optimal sea temperatures for sargassum growth are around 79 degrees F, but temperatures above 86 degrees F can hinder growth. Average sea-surface temperatures in the tropical Atlantic currently range between 81 and 85 degrees F. “Ultimately, the future of sargassum in the tropical Atlantic will depend on… global warming,” argues Alfredo Martínez-Garcia, a geochemist at the Max Planck Institute for Chemistry. So future warming could ultimately doom the tropical sargassum.

Even so, what has happened in the past 15 years appears unprecedented. There are records of seaweed blooms in the Gulf of Mexico during the Mayan civilization a millennium ago, but they were far smaller and less prevalent. “There is nothing like [today’s vast bloom] in these latitudes anywhere in the historical records,” says Bracco.

A loggerhead sea turtle hatchling on sargassum seaweed in Florida. Mark Conlin / VW PICS / UIG via Getty Images

There are many species of sargassum, some found in the Pacific Ocean and elsewhere. But while most cling to the seabed, it is the floating species Sargassum fluitans and Sargassum natans that predominate in the Atlantic. While the ecosystem they have created in the Sargasso Sea is well-researched, to date, very little research has been done on the make-up of this new Atlantic biome. But it is very different from the previously clear ocean it has replaced, which had a low supply of nutrients, limiting marine life. Today, numerous species live on the leaves, stems, and air bladders of the floating sargassum, including bacteria and other algae supporting populations of invertebrates such as crabs and shrimp, seabirds, and the endemic predatory sargassum fish. Some species are common to sargassum, while others are specific to the warmer tropical waters where the seaweed belt has formed.

A surge of marine heat waves threatens to scramble ocean life. Read more.

The sargassum also helps maintain wider tropical marine ecosystems by providing shelter for juvenile fish species, as well as new habitat for endangered loggerhead and green turtles, and food for larger fish. It “acts as a floating ecosystem for marine life, providing essential food and shelter for important species, such as tuna, marlin, turtles, and birds,” according to an international study led by Jung last year.

As the new ecosystem develops, it is causing scientists to reassess the invasive sargassum. Despite the damage it causes when inundating shores, a growing band of enthusiasts says that it should be celebrated and not condemned. Just as there are international calls led by Greenpeace, the IUCN, and the Bermuda and U.K. governments to designate the long-standing sargassum mats in the Sargasso Sea as a marine sanctuary under the U.N. High Sea Treaty, so some ocean scientists would like the same protection accorded to its giant new offspring.

Researchers at NOAA have already spoken up for the importance of the new sargassum biome. They have designated areas of the tropical sargassum belt in the waters of the Gulf of Mexico and U.S. Caribbean as “essential fish habitat” and “critical habitat” for loggerhead turtles under the Endangered Species Act. To protect this habitat, NOAA currently prohibits the commercial harvest of sargassum in all U.S. federal waters south of North Carolina.

Blooms of sargassum in the tropical Atlantic peak in September on the African coast and in July in the west, where Mexico currently spends more than $300 million annually cleaning up the mess. More than 500 workers in protective gear have this year picked up more than 100,000 metric tons of sargassum weed from the beaches of the southeastern state of Quintana Roo.

The state boasts that its famous resort cities such as Cancun, Tulum, and Playa del Carmen offer palm-lined white sandy beaches and coral reefs. But for much of the year, those beaches and reefs are often smothered by piles of brown seaweed that float ashore each day on the tides. Hotels are emptying as spooked tourists look instead to the country’s Pacific coast.

Workers remove sargassum from the shore of Playa del Carmen, Mexico. Elizabeth Ruiz / AFP via Getty Images

In July, at the height of the current infestation, the Mexican government announced emergency measures to fight the menace, including deploying 30 miles of containment barriers to intercept mats before they reach land and buying amphibious weed harvesters to patrol offshore.

Efforts to collect encroaching sargassum have fueled interest in finding economic value for the harvested weed, especially now that modelers say they can predict sargassum hotspots up to seven months ahead, opening up the prospect of cost-effective harvesting.

Plans include converting the weed into biofuel or fertilizer, or using it as an organic replacement for cement in construction. Start-ups are keen to join in. Carbonwave, a sargassum refining company launched in 2020 in Boston but operating in Mexico and Puerto Rico, extracts biopolymers to make textiles, plastic, and emulsifiers.

The weed could also be harvested to capture its carbon and sell carbon credits. “Sargassum absorbs large amounts of carbon dioxide as it grows,” says Bracco. Its dry weight is 30 percent carbon. “When it reaches the coast and decomposes, much of that carbon is released back into the atmosphere. If we can intervene before this happens, this system could instead be part of the solution [to climate change].”

But care must be taken, says Danielle Hatt, a marine ecologist at Florida International University, in a study for the French territory of Guadeloupe, one of the most affected Caribbean islands. “Chemical screening for toxic metals, particularly arsenic, should be mandatory before approving the use of sargassum biomass for agricultural, food, or feed applications,” she says.

Many ideas for using sargassum will be discussed at an international conference on sargassum exploitation, organized with the European Union, in Cancun in October. #2924. Massive study of 460,000 people challenges ultra-processed food cancer fears

A massive study involving more than 460,000 Americans is challenging assumptions about ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer. Researchers found that eating more ultra-processed food (UPF) was not associated with a higher overall incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC). However, the findings revealed an important distinction: Some types of ultra-processed foods were linked to lower cancer rates, while others were associated with higher rates.

The research, led by Erikka Loftfield of the National Cancer Institute and published October 15 in the open-access journal PLOS Medicine, suggests that the health effects of ultra-processed foods may depend heavily on what those foods actually contain.

Ultra-processed foods account for more than half of the calories consumed by American adults. These products have attracted increasing scrutiny because of their potential connections to chronic diseases, including colorectal cancer (CRC). Yet the category includes a remarkably diverse assortment of foods, making it difficult to judge their health effects as a single group.

For example, some ultra-processed whole grain and dairy products have been associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer, while processed meats have been linked to a higher risk. This raises an important question about whether the level of processing alone is a useful indicator of a food's potential health effects.

A Massive Study Tracks Cancer Risk for Nearly 19 Years

To investigate these differences, researchers examined health and dietary records from 461,682 Americans who were between 50 and 71 years old when they joined the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study in 1995 and 1996.

At the beginning of the study, participants completed questionnaires describing how frequently they consumed different foods. Researchers used these responses to estimate each person's intake of ultra-processed foods.

Participants were then tracked through 2018, with a median follow-up period of 18.8 years. This allowed the team to examine how dietary patterns were associated with the development of colorectal cancer over nearly two decades.

The results showed no overall association between total ultra-processed food consumption and colorectal cancer incidence. In other words, participants who consumed more ultra-processed foods did not, as a group, experience higher rates of colorectal cancer.

However, a closer examination of individual food categories revealed a much more complicated picture.

Some Ultra-Processed Foods Linked to Lower Cancer Risk

The researchers found that higher consumption of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium from ultra-processed food sources was associated with a lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

Meat from ultra-processed sources showed the opposite pattern, with greater consumption associated with higher cancer incidence.

The team also examined what might happen when ultra-processed foods were replaced with less processed alternatives. Their analysis found that substituting minimally processed foods for 10% of ultra-processed food intake by weight was associated with a 2% lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

These findings suggest that the nutritional characteristics of individual foods may be more informative than simply classifying everything as ultra-processed. Foods placed in the same broad category can have very different associations with cancer risk.

Why the Findings Come With Important Limitations

Despite the study's large size and lengthy follow-up period, the researchers caution that its conclusions may not apply equally to everyone.

Most participants were older, white Americans, meaning the findings may not accurately reflect the experiences of younger adults or more racially and ethnically diverse populations.

Another important limitation involves how dietary information was collected. Participants reported their eating habits only once, at the beginning of the study. Researchers therefore could not account for changes in individual diets over the following decades.

The availability and variety of ultra-processed foods also increased during that time. As a result, the initial dietary assessments may have underestimated participants' actual consumption during the study period.

Because the research was observational, it also cannot establish whether particular foods directly increase or decrease cancer risk.

Rather than treating all ultra-processed foods as equally harmful, the findings support evaluating foods according to their nutritional content and specific health associations.

The results also align with existing dietary recommendations for colorectal cancer prevention, which encourage eating whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium-rich foods while limiting processed meat.

"In this large population of older US adults, we did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence; however, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention such that higher whole grain and dairy but lower meat intakes were associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence. "

Ultimately, the study highlights why the label "ultra-processed" may not tell the whole story. When it comes to colorectal cancer, the kinds of foods people eat appear to matter more than their processing category alone.

Once you broke free of carb dependency and developed metabolic flexibility, you could trust your body to tell you what it needs. Over time, you would naturally fall into a pattern of eating that suited your lifestyle.

However, I find that most people, when they make a commitment to following the Primal Blueprint, want to see how far they can take it. Once they discover how good they feel just from choosing Primal foods, it’s natural to ask whether there are additional levels of health and vitality waiting to be unlocked. This is where practices like intermittent fasting and experimenting with macronutrients come in.

Although the Primal Blueprint doesn’t prescribe specific macronutrient ratios, there are some best practices to consider here. Let’s dive in.

First, some basic terminology. Macronutrients, or “macros,” are the protein, carbohydrates, and fats in the foods you eat. These are the building blocks and energy substrates, separate from the water, vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and other compounds, including artificial ingredients, present in food.

The calories in your food come from the macronutrients, with protein and carbohydrates each providing 4 calories per gram and fat providing 9 calories per gram.

Now I have a real problem with this. The body wants to burn fat and glucose (from carbs) for energy, but protein will always go first towards muscle protein synthesis and repair, or building cells, hormones, and enzymes. In that context, it hardly seems fair to assign it a “burn rate” of 4 calories per gram. It’s like saying the 2×4 studs that support the walls of your house can burn nicely if you run out of firewood. They will, but that’s hardly the point.

Nevertheless, that’s how the math is done.

So let’s say you’re interested in conducting some sort of n=1 nutritional experiment. Maybe you want to up your protein intake during a hypertrophy cycle or drop your carbs to go keto. To determine how many grams of protein, carbs, and fat you’re consuming—and, therefore, how many calories you’re taking in—you’ll need to input all of your food into a tracker like Cronometer or My Fitness Pal. Tracking your food is tedious if you do it right, but the data can be eye opening. Most people don’t have a clue how much they’re actually eating.

Once you’ve established a baseline, you can proceed to adjust your intake of certain macros up or down. As I said, the Primal Blueprint doesn’t specify exact macros to aim for, but here are some things to consider.

Of the three macronutrients, protein takes priority in my book. So much of the work I do comes down to one goal: maximizing healthspan. In other words, staying fit, healthy, and cognitively sharp for as long as possible. A cornerstone of that is maintaining mobility and strength and staving off age-related muscle loss and frailty. To do that, you need two things: building blocks (amino acids from protein) and stimuli that tell your body to invest in bone and muscle (exercise).

For the average person, 0.7-1.0 grams of protein per pound of body weight (1.6-2.2 g/kg) is a good target.

You can go higher than that if you want, and there may be some benefit, but this is a good starting point. Taking in a lot more than that isn’t dangerous, but you’ll soon start to see diminishing returns.

Whenever I write about protein, I always get a bunch of follow-up questions: Should I use current body weight, lean body mass, or ideal body weight? How do age and gender factor in?

I’ve written extensively in other posts about how much protein you should be eating if you’re an athlete or a person trying to lose weight, for example. The numbers vary somewhat, but if you’re in the range of 1 gram per pound of body weight, you’ll be fine. Likewise, if you do your calculations based on lean body mass instead of total body mass, you’ll fall in a similar range, especially if you’re already on the leaner side. Take a 180-pound individual who carries 20 percent body fat. The math works out to 126-180 grams per day based on total body weight or 100-144 grams per day based on lean body mass. Not terribly different.

Also, don’t stress about getting the exact same amount of protein every day. Look at your protein intake across the past three to five days. Are you averaging around 1 gram per pound per day? Then you’re in good shape.

I’ve said many times that collagen should be classified as a macronutrient all its own. Collagen is a protein, being composed of amino acids, but it’s not a complete protein since it lacks tryptophan. As a general rule, you’re not supposed to count collagen toward your total protein intake when tracking your food, but I don’t fully agree with that. Collagen is protein. The amino acids are the same amino acids you’d get from a steak, though in different proportions.

I doubt too many people are using collagen peptides as a primary source of protein in their diets, but it’s probably worth stating: most of your protein needs should be met using complete protein from whole food sources.

The Primal diet falls naturally on the lower-carbohydrate end of the spectrum compared to a typical modern diet. A person consuming a Standard American Diet can easily top 300 or 400 grams of carbs by dinnertime thanks to grains, sugar-sweetened beverages, and processed snack foods. In contrast, it’s tough to exceed 100 grams of carbs eating Primal foods, even with tons of colorful above-ground vegetables. Even if you eat a fair amount of fruit or starchy vegetables, you’ll be hard-pressed to exceed 150 grams of carbs on average per day.

I’ve found that under 150 grams of carbs per day is most folks’ sweet spot for easily maintaining body composition and having plenty of energy.

However, if you specifically want to get into ketosis, you’ll need to drop below 50 grams per day, and you’ll need to be strict about it.

On the other hand, endurance athletes who consistently burn a lot of calories and dip into glycogen stores on a regular basis might benefit from eating more carbs from additional fruits, tubers, and root vegetables, perhaps even some rice or oats. This isn’t mandatory, mind you. Plenty of athletes feel and perform great on moderate-carb or ketogenic diets. But super-active folks are also “earning” their carbs and shouldn’t be afraid to experiment with higher intakes, especially around tough workouts and competitions.

I don’t. Net carbs are just the total carbs in your food minus the fiber. The great thing about eating Primally is that you don’t have to fuss about these kinds of details.

Net carbs are really only relevant within the context of a keto diet, where you might want to distinguish between digestible carbohydrates, which are converted into glucose, and fiber, which does not impact insulin levels. In The Keto Reset Diet, I suggested that you don’t have to count carbs from above-ground non-starchy vegetables and avocados since their glucose/insulin impact is minimal thanks to their fiber content. Even that is optional, though. For tracking purposes, it’s easier to just cap your total daily carb intake at 50 grams per day and be done with it.

Once you’ve established your protein and carbohydrate intake, fat becomes the macro you leverage to increase or decrease energy (caloric) intake.

Let’s say you typically eat 150 grams of protein a day and 100 grams of carbs. Each provides 4 calories per gram, so that’s 1,000 calories per day so far from those two macronutrients. The rest will come from fat at 9 calories per gram.

A primary goal of the Primal Blueprint is to turn you into a fat-burning machine, meaning that your cells are highly efficient at using fat for energy instead of relying heavily on glucose. That fat can come from your plate or your own adipose tissue. If you’re a smaller, moderately active woman who is trying to burn through some excess body fat, you might aim for an additional 500-800 calories per day, or 55-90 grams of fat, roughly. But if you’re a larger man who is aiming for a caloric excess during a bulking phase, you might need an additional 2,000 calories. Perhaps you bump your protein up by 50 grams, but that’s only 200 calories, and remember, protein isn’t the most efficient fuel anyway. In any case, this guy will need to consume closer to 200 grams of fat per day to hit his goal.

You never have to bother with macros on a Primal diet, particularly not if you are happy with your current body composition, performance in the gym, and general health. But, if you’re struggling to reduce those stubborn fat stores, you’re having a hard time gaining muscle, or you otherwise feel that your health could be better, AND you’re already eating Primally, playing around with the macronutrient composition of your diet could be a worthwhile endeavor.

That last caveat is important. For folks coming from a standard modern diet, ditching grains, sugars, and sketchy oils is going to move the needle more than ratcheting fat intake up or down by 20 grams or whatever. Now, if your blood sugar is through the roof and you’re on a litany of meds for type 2 diabetes, getting carb intake down is also going to be a top priority. (In that case, make sure your doctor knows that you’re changing your diet, as you’ll likely need to adjust your medications concurrently.)

In short, you’ll get more bang for your buck by getting the major Primal pillars of nutrition, movement, and lifestyle dialed in first before experimenting with meal timing, fasting, macros, etc. Lay the right foundation first, and then these details can turn your results from great to outstanding.

#2933. Rejuvenation breakthrough: Scientists reverse 60 years of aging in human cells

Scientists in Germany have found a way to dramatically reverse the molecular age of human cells, transforming blood cells from an 80-year-old person into stem cells with an age signature of less than 20 years.

Researchers at the University Hospital of Bonn and the University of Bonn achieved this remarkable transformation by reprogramming immature red blood cells into neural stem cells, which can eventually develop into brain cells. During the process, molecular markers associated with aging were effectively reset, making the cells more than 60 years younger at the molecular level.

Unlike earlier approaches, the rejuvenation happened gradually, allowing scientists to follow the changes over an extended period. This slower process could provide important clues about how cellular aging can be reversed and potentially help researchers investigate treatments for diseases associated with aging.

The findings were published in the journal Aging Cell.

How Scientists Turn Blood Cells Into Brain Stem Cells

The human body contains hundreds of specialized cell types, all of which originate from a single fertilized egg and carry essentially the same genetic instructions. As cells develop, however, they take on specific roles. Under normal circumstances, a skin cell cannot transform into a liver cell, and a blood cell cannot become a nerve cell.

Modern laboratory techniques have made it possible to overcome these biological restrictions. Scientists can now reprogram certain mature cells to adopt entirely different identities. For example, skin cells can be converted into cells capable of producing nerve tissue, an approach that could eventually contribute to treatments for neurodegenerative diseases.

The process relies on a combination of proteins called transcription factors, which regulate how genes are activated. By changing which genetic instructions a cell follows, these proteins can redirect its development and give it a new cellular identity.

"Using this method, we have directly converted red blood cell precursors into neural stem cells," explains Prof. Dr. Oliver Brüstle, director of the Institute of Reconstructive Neurobiology at the University Hospital of Bonn.

Earlier experiments by the Bonn team had already demonstrated that nerve cells generated through this approach could successfully connect with existing neurons after being transplanted into the brains of mice.

Scientists Reverse More Than 60 Years of Cellular Aging

The latest investigation focused on an especially intriguing consequence of cellular reprogramming: the dramatic reversal of molecular aging.

"In our current study, however, we focused on a different phenomenon: namely, the observation that cells become significantly rejuvenated during reprogramming," emphasizes Brüstle, who is also a member of the Transdisciplinary Research Area (TRA) Life & Health at the University of Bonn.

To determine how much the cells had rejuvenated, the researchers examined their molecular clocks, particularly biological markers known as epigenetic clocks. These clocks estimate a cell's biological age by analyzing chemical modifications to DNA that tend to accumulate or change as organisms grow older.

Importantly, these modifications do not alter the DNA sequence itself. Instead, they affect how frequently certain genetic instructions are used (epigenetics). Because these patterns change predictably with age, scientists can use them to estimate how old a cell appears at the molecular level.

The results were striking. After reprogramming, cells taken from an 80-year-old individual displayed molecular age signatures corresponding to someone younger than 20. In other words, the process had reversed more than six decades of cellular aging.

"We also know that this epigenetic rejuvenation ensures that the cells actually behave like young cells," says the scientist.

A Slower Rejuvenation Process Reveals New Clues

Scientists have previously observed that reprogramming cells can reset their biological age. However, earlier demonstrations of this effect involved a more complicated process requiring two distinct stages.

First, researchers would convert an existing cell, such as a blood cell, into a pluripotent stem cell. These versatile cells have nearly unlimited developmental "career paths" because they can give rise to many different cell types throughout the body.

The pluripotent stem cell would then be guided into becoming a neural stem cell, which is more specialized and can produce cells found in the nervous system.

This conventional approach produces rejuvenation relatively quickly, making it difficult to examine all the changes taking place.

The Bonn researchers instead bypassed the pluripotent stage entirely.

"In contrast, we converted blood cells directly into neural stem cells -- that is, without first passing through the pluripotent cell stage," said Brüstle. "In our approach, rejuvenation occurred gradually and could be tracked for over 100 days."

The researchers observed a dramatic reduction in molecular age, with the study highlighting rejuvenation of approximately 60 years after 50 days. Because the process continued over a much longer period, scientists could examine the biological changes as they unfolded rather than observing only the final result.

What Cellular Rejuvenation Could Mean for Alzheimer's Research

The ability to watch cells gradually become younger could be particularly valuable for scientists trying to understand the mechanisms that control biological aging.

By studying this extended transformation, researchers can investigate which molecular pathways are involved and test whether particular substances can speed up or slow down the rejuvenation process.

"Rejuvenation that extends over such a long period of time is ideal as an experimental model: Since the epigenetic clocks are slowly reset over several weeks, we can use this model to investigate which factors and active substances accelerate or slow down the rejuvenation process."

The findings could be especially relevant to neuroscience, where aging is closely connected to the development of serious neurological conditions. Although the work does not establish a treatment capable of reversing aging in people, it offers a laboratory model for investigating how cells regain youthful characteristics.

This could eventually help scientists better understand the cellular changes associated with conditions such as Alzheimer's disease.

"After all, age is the most important risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's!"

#2936. How to Stay on Track While Traveling

Staying on track with your fitness and wellness goals can feel extra tough when your routine gets thrown off. Whether you’re hopping on a plane, heading out for a weekend getaway, or just dealing with a packed week that doesn’t leave much room for your usual habits, consistency gets harder. But the good news is you don’t need perfection. You just need a plan that works with your lifestyle. 

Here’s how to make it happen, even when you’re out of your regular routine:

1. Set Mini Goals

Don’t try to hit every single goal perfectly. Instead, focus on 1–2 things that will make you feel your best. Maybe it’s getting 20 minutes of movement in each day, drinking a certain amount of water, or hitting your protein goal. Choose what matters most and commit to that before diving in all at once.

Tip: Use your LSF planner or a simple note in your phone to track these. Seeing that visual reminder each day will keep you grounded.

2. MOVE Your Body 

You don’t need a full gym or perfect setup to move your body. Walks, workouts in our MOVE App, stretching, or a quick ab burner before heading out can all count + can all be done at home. 

Even 10 minutes counts. I promise.

3. Increase Hydration

When you're dehydrated, energy drops, digestion slows, and cravings creep in. Traveling? Bring a reusable water bottle so you can refill it wherever you go. Busy week? Set reminders on your phone to sip throughout the day.

And if you’re flying? Drink even more. Planes can be very dehydrating! 

4. Improve, Don’t Restrict

Trying to be “perfect” while traveling usually leads to stress, not success. Instead of cutting things out, look for easy upgrades. Add veggies where you can. Choose lean protein when it’s an option. Enjoy the fun foods without guilt but also fuel yourself in ways that support your energy and mood.

It’s not all or nothing. 

5. Stick to your Morning Routine 

Even one consistent part of your day can help keep you grounded. Maybe it’s a morning walk, journaling with your coffee, or a quick stretch before the day begins. Having something that starts your day with intention helps everything else fall into place even when you’re off schedule.

Staying on track isn’t about rules. It’s about showing up for yourself, no matter where you are or what your day looks like. Progress happens in the small moments, and it all adds up. You’ve got this babe!

#2937. How to Embody a Primal Lifestyle

If I stopped you on the street today and offered you a million dollars to improve your health or fitness, you’d probably immediately think diet and exercise. Of course you’d be right—but only to a degree. Those two factors are fundamental to health, fitness, vitality, and longevity. However, if you only focused on diet and exercise, you wouldn’t reach your full potential. Not by a long shot.

As important as food and movement are, they are only two of the inputs that affect gene expression. There is a bigger picture to consider, another pillar encompassed within the Primal Blueprint—one that is often overlooked even by individuals who have made a sincere commitment to building healthy habits and taking care of their bodies for the long haul. These are the Primal Blueprint Lifestyle Laws.

If I had to nominate the Primal Blueprint Law that is most neglected in the modern world—and it would be hard to pick just one—it would probably be sleep. At least with diet and exercise, most people pay lip service to how important they are, recognizing that they should care about them even if their day-to-day choices say otherwise. Yet how many times have you seen uber-successful people boasting about how little sleep they get on a nightly basis?

Too many folks glorify an “I’ll sleep when I’m dead” mentality, ignoring the fact that too sleep is, ironically, pushing them toward an early grave.

Our genes expect and need consistent, restful sleep, and plenty of it. Our ancestors got lots of sleep. Even after the discovery of fire, it wasn’t as if they stayed up all night partying. From sunset to sunrise it was safer to huddle together and rest. Long days of hunting and gathering and otherwise working hard for every bite of food also required sufficient time to repair and recover. Studies of modern hunter-gatherers suggest it wasn’t necessarily always an uninterrupted nine or ten hours, either. It’s likely that they slept together as families or as small tribes, keeping a watch out for predators, breastfeeding the baby or just dozing in and out throughout the night.

Of course, a restorative afternoon nap was also available when the urge hit, with no guilt about what else they really should have been doing. Our modern tendency toward poor quality, insufficient sleep has catastrophic consequences for physical, cognitive, and emotional health. Yet better sleep is almost always within reach with some simple (and sometimes not-so-easy) behavior modifications.

(Primal Law #7 is Play. You can read about it in the Primal Movement post.)

Cavemen weren’t really men (or women) who lived their lives in caves all the time. Most of the day, they were in the great outdoors pursuing their various survival tasks. Regular exposure to sun provided lots of vitamin D, an all-important vitamin (really more like a hormone) which they could not easily obtain from food and which their bodies could not manufacture without direct sunlight.

Fast forward to today, and most people spend almost all their time inside—at home, at work, in the car or subway, at the grocery store, mall, movie theaters, bowling alleys, and on and on. Even most of exercise is now done inside gyms, dance studios, or garages. The net result is that an alarming proportion of the population does not get enough sun exposure. Couple that with widespread public health messages about avoiding the sun and using sunscreen every time you go out, and vitamin D deficiency is now widespread.

Not to mention, we are missing out on the other benefits of being in nature. While it’s never a good idea to burn, I think the far greater danger to most people is too little sun, not too much. Respect basic sun safety practices, but embrace the sun as the life-giving force that it is.

You’re doing all these other things to live a long, healthy life—eating right, exercising, getting plenty of sleep, nurturing your social relationships—but it can all be cut short in the blink of an eye with one silly, preventable misstep. That’s why Primal Law #9 reminds you to exercise appropriate caution when navigating this dangerous world.

In our ancestors’ time, this mostly meant avoiding trauma that could lead to immediate death or serious injury that would prevent them from being active, participating members of their hunter-gatherer clan. Today it still means essentially the same thing, except the dangers we face are a far cry from the rogue tiger or poisonous mushroom that might have taken out Grok.

This doesn’t mean wrap yourself in bubble wrap and never take any risks whatsoever. Rather, this law exhorts you to use that highly evolved human brain of yours to calculate risk and take wise precautions as needed.

Speaking of your big brain, the final Primal Law reminds you to use it. Use it to problem solve. Use it to create. Use it to imagine. Use it to dream big dreams and make them come true. Use it to reminisce about the past. Use it to learn from past mistakes. Use it to create the wild and wonderful life that is your birthright as a human.

#2938. 'Wildwood' Is a Love Letter to All Things Portland, Oregon

On Location peels back the curtain on some of your favorite films, television shows, and more. This time, we take a look at Wildwood.

There are several times when Prue, the 13-year-old protagonist of fantasy epic Wildwood, seems just as in disbelief of the magical world she inhabits as us viewers. As someone who grew up in Portland, Oregon, she thinks she's already encountered the limits of weirdness, and yet—as one of the giant animal denizens of the Impassable Wilderness (known by locals as Wildwood) is quick to remind her—there she is, speaking with an owl having tea in a waistcoat. Par for the course for the stop-motion studio, Laika's newest creation is a visual feast with soaring eagles the size of commercial airplanes, ancient trees with roots that sprawl across the whole realm, and even an all-out war between worlds reminiscent of the D-Day Landing in Saving Private Ryan. As much as you could lose yourself in these grand spectacles, the film is also emphatic about remembering the magic that imbues just a single blade of grass. As the first Laika movie explicitly set in Portland, the studio looked for inspiration in their own backyards for how to bring the universe of Colin Meloy's titular novel to life. Condé Nast Traveler spoke with Wildwood director Travis Knight on the inspiration behind this exhilarating, stop-motion saga that takes place not too far from home.

While Dripsters isn't a literal spot in Portland's St. Johns neighborhood, the storefront is a nod to Laika's own in-studio coffee shop.

This movie is such a love letter to Portland, from the Voodoo Doughnuts Prue and Mac eat to the Decembrists posters in her room. This is the first Laika film that’s been explicitly set where the studio calls home. How did that influence the filmmaking process?

It was a joy, unlike anything I've ever experienced. Filmmaking for me is such a privilege to be able to do for a living. To be able to tell a story that was set in the place that I was born, the place that I've lived almost my entire life, the place where our studio is, where so many of us live, was a very special experience because we know that place better than anybody, and we could bring that world to life with real specificity, with real personality. Wildwood has fingerprints from all over the Pacific Northwest in its set designs, from Crater Lake to Haystack Rock.

There’s a lot of well-deserved applause for the entirely hand-crafted approach to this film. I also think it is a meaningful statement that is relevant to the film and to our culture at large. There is a huge maker community in Portland. It's a group of people that actually celebrates handcrafted things, and that really is what's beating at the heart of the movie, that the film is a celebration of human creativity, of human innovation, of the human touch, and in a world that's often designed to be frictionless, easier, and automated. I think the human touch is something that's special and deserves to be celebrated and deserves to be preserved.

Stop motion is such a deliberate practice, where every wrinkle is quite literally sculpted. As a director, are there specific details about a place that you gravitate towards or like to use as launch points?

I think when you're approaching a story, you really want to listen to what the story wants to tell you. When I think about Kubo and the Two Strings, I go back to my own experiences when I was exposed to Japan for the first time. I was eight years old with my father, and it was a world unlike anything that I'd ever seen before. It left a huge impact on me, and those were the types of things that I was channeling. Ultimately, what is driving all of these things is the theme and the emotion underneath it, and that is the thing that everything else needs to spiral out of. The first movie that we came out with [Coraline] was in 2009, so it was only a year after that that I discovered Wildwood. Portland is a very different place now than it was then. Many, many things have happened since then, some very positive, some tragic. We moved the film into pre-production in 2019, seven years ago. I felt like at the time, we were ready to make Wildwood. Then the whole world turned upside down, and we were hit with a global pandemic the likes of which we had not seen in a century. It changed the whole world, and it changed Portland, and when I see the version of Portland on screen in Wildwood, it's the Portland that I know and the Portland that I love. My hope is that, in time, it can become that again.

Nothing is easy in stop-motion. Everything is difficult in stop motion. But the things that tend to be more manageable are interiors, like a child's bedroom, but even those have so many details that have to be thought of and designed and built and manipulated. What's difficult to do is nature because organic shapes have their own unique grammar. Things like a forest are really difficult to do in stop motion, and I made the decision to set a movie that was almost entirely based in a forest. It's the materials you use, how you build these things, their shape. There's so much beauty to the natural world that's so different and so strange compared to the inorganic world, the things that we humans construct, and most of the film is set in those places. Places like when Prue and Curtis enter Wildwood for the very first time took us a long time to design what that looked like, and it is just so exquisitely gorgeous. It's so beautiful. It took set designers, set dressers, painters, artists, just so many people who built this space to be a work of art. It's something that belongs in a museum, and for a lot of the movie, that moment will pass by within a matter of seconds. What you're seeing is just extraordinary human craftsmanship and artistry on a grand scale, and it's one of the things I love so much about this movie.

How did you incorporate the wildlife and ecosystems of the Pacific Northwest into this film?

As someone who grew up in the Portland area, I have a lot of knowledge of coyotes. We have a lot of knowledge of crows. We're always next to the natural world. I grew up outside of Portland, essentially on the side of a mountain. I spent most of my childhood days wandering around the forest and climbing up trees and discovering things. The film is emotionally about one primal thing, but there are a lot of layers to the story that we're telling, and part of that is that the film is an eco-fable. That was something that we treated seriously, as it’s something that so many of my collaborators care about deeply, and it’s something I care about deeply. We wanted to try to express that in an organic way without feeling like we were emblazoning it in neon that we're making a message movie. The hope is that you want to honor these things in a way that feels authentic. There's no greater teacher to you than observation, and going out in nature, and seeing how the world was formed, independent of how we’ve tried to shape it. Fortunately, in Oregon, one of the many great gifts of the state is that in any direction you go, you will discover it. We poured so much of that into this movie.

From manuscript to big screen, Wildwood took 16 years to create.

Now that you’re on the other side of creating a movie based on where you call home, do you have any words to the wise for any creatives out there who feel daunted to tell their own stories about home?

What is the piece of advice that they leave out on the old chair-side chest? It was something like writing what you know. I don't think that necessarily means write where you're from, but you have a unique way of looking at the world based on your experiences within it. We know our own lives, unlike anyone else, but that's also the thing that we have to offer that no one else can offer. That's the one thing an artist can do that nobody else can do. They know their own experiences, they own their mind, they know their own imagination, which is different than everybody else's. It's the one thing that we can really offer—how you've lived, how you grew up, the people you grew up with, the people you've interacted with. Every place has its own unique characteristics and its own unique personality. Even within that, our experiences are different from the person next to us. The daunting thing shouldn't be how to tell a story about your home, it's how to really honor the things that you're feeling, how to honor your imagination in a way that really does it justice. Those are all things that we strive to do as artists. We have big dreams, we have big feelings, and how can we get that out in a way that it makes sense to somebody, and it makes them care?

#2940. Nearly Half of Farmers Globally Poisoned by Pesticides Each Year

This article was originally published by The Guardian and is reproduced here as part of the Climate Desk collaboration.

Almost half of the world’s farmers are poisoned by pesticides every single year, a study has found.

Using publicly available data from 2006 to 2023, the study, which was commissioned by Pesticide Action Network, found there to be an estimated 402 to 433 million cases of unintentional acute pesticide poisoning of farmers and farm workers every year.

#2942. NASA’s Perseverance Mars rover just shattered the all-time off-world driving record

NASA's Perseverance rover has officially set a new driving record on another world, traveling farther across an extraterrestrial surface than any vehicle before it. On October 11, its odometer reached 28.10 miles (45.22 kilometers), surpassing the previous record of 28.06 miles (45.16 kilometers) held by NASA's Opportunity rover, which explored Mars from 2004 to 2018.

Even more impressive is how quickly Perseverance accomplished the feat. Opportunity needed nearly 15 years to cover its record distance, while Perseverance did it in just five and a half years. The milestone came on the rover's 2,006th Martian day, known as a sol.

The record was set as Perseverance made its way toward a location called "Idubi," traveling through an area beyond the western rim of Jezero Crater known as "Lac de Charmes." This region contains what may be some of the oldest terrain ever examined in the solar system.

The landscape preserves a remarkable glimpse into the distant past. Similar rocks on Earth have largely disappeared because of erosion and the continual movement of the planet's crust through plate tectonics (Mars doesn't have tectonic plates). At Idubi, scientists hope to uncover evidence of ancient hydrothermal systems, where heated water once circulated through rocks and may have created conditions suitable for life.

How NASA's Self-Driving Mars Rover Broke the Record

One of the biggest reasons Perseverance has been able to travel so far is its sophisticated autonomous driving system.

Known as AutoNav, the technology allows the rover to navigate much of the Martian landscape without waiting for detailed driving instructions from Earth. As Perseverance moves, it continuously captures images of its surroundings, constructs a three-dimensional map of potential obstacles, and selects a safe route between destinations chosen by mission controllers.

NASA engineers describe this process as "thinking while driving."

The system operates while Perseverance travels at or near its maximum speed, allowing the rover to cover as much as 1,351 feet (412 meters) in a single Martian day.

More than 90% of the distance Perseverance has traveled has involved AutoNav evaluating the terrain. By comparison, the earliest versions of autonomous navigation used by Opportunity and its twin rover, Spirit, were responsible for assessing only about 5% of their driving distances.

The technological progress is even clearer when compared with NASA's first Mars rover, Sojourner. That early robot evaluated just 20 measurement points to determine the safest route ahead. Perseverance now analyzes approximately 600,000 points each time it examines the surrounding terrain.

"It is an honor to be working on Perseverance at a moment when the technology has evolved to the point that drives like these are routine," said Steven Lee, the project manager of the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California. "The original mission called for no more than 12.4 miles of driving. We've blown well past that, and with all mobility systems fully operational and favorable extended-ground-life test results, we expect Perseverance to keep adding to this record for many years to come."

NASA has already approved Perseverance's first mission extension. Beginning in October, the extended mission will allow the rover to continue investigating the rim of Jezero Crater through approximately the end of 2028.

Nearly 30 Years of Mars Rover Exploration

Perseverance's achievement builds on almost three decades of robotic exploration on Mars. With each new generation, NASA's rovers have become more capable of traveling longer distances, navigating difficult terrain, and investigating locations that earlier vehicles could never have reached.

The journey began in 1997 with Sojourner, a small rover roughly the size of a microwave oven. During its 83-sol mission, it traveled approximately 330 feet (100 meters).

NASA took a major step forward in 2004 with the arrival of Spirit and Opportunity. These golf-cart-size rovers were originally designed to operate for just 90 Martian days, but both dramatically exceeded expectations.

Spirit covered 4.8 miles (7.7 kilometers) before its mission ended in 2010 after it failed to survive the harsh Martian winter.

Opportunity continued operating much longer, remaining active for an extraordinary 5,111 sols. In 2015, it became the first vehicle to complete a marathon distance on another world. Its total mileage eventually established the driving record that Perseverance has now broken.

Meanwhile, NASA's Curiosity rover, which landed in Gale Crater in 2012 and continues exploring today, has traveled more than 23.65 miles (38.07 kilometers).

Perseverance Is Becoming an Even Smarter Driver

Although Perseverance has already surpassed every previous extraterrestrial driving record, NASA engineers are continuing to improve its navigation capabilities.

"The rover that broke this record did so by making its own decisions about the path. Given the autonomy to just go, Perseverance is a better driver than the one that landed, and we're not done," Mark Maimone, deputy team chief for robotic operations on Perseverance, said. "Our newest software teaches Perseverance to steer less and roll more, and to check its own position against orbital maps mid-drive. The next frontier is sunlight itself. Glare in the navigation cameras is why we park by mid-afternoon if we're driving toward the Sun, and if we can teach the rover to see past those artifacts, every rover that follows gets a longer working day."

One challenge is that bright sunlight can interfere with the rover's navigation cameras, making it harder for its software to interpret the terrain. When Perseverance travels toward the Sun, mission planners currently stop driving by mid-afternoon to avoid these problems.

Finding ways to overcome that limitation could allow Perseverance and future Mars rovers to spend more time exploring each day.

The rover's record also extends beyond Mars. Earlier in its journey, Perseverance exceeded two historic driving distances achieved on the Moon.

In 1972, Apollo 17 astronauts Gene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt traveled 22.2 miles (35.7 kilometers) across the lunar surface. The following year, the Soviet Union's robotic Lunokhod 2 rover covered 24.2 miles (39 kilometers). Perseverance has now surpassed both of those distances.

Exploring 4-Billion-Year-Old Rocks for Signs of Ancient Life

For NASA, however, setting mileage records is not the primary objective. The rover's ability to travel farther and faster allows scientists to investigate more geological formations, examine a greater variety of rocks, and piece together Mars' environmental history.

Perseverance has already explored several dramatically different landscapes. Its journey began on an ancient lakebed inside Jezero Crater and continued through the remains of a river system that once carried water and sediment into the basin.

Along that route, the rover encountered a rock called "Cheyava Falls," where scientists identified a potential biosignature. A biosignature is a feature or chemical pattern that could provide evidence of past life, although nonbiological processes can sometimes produce similar clues.

The rover has since climbed toward the crater rim, where scientists are investigating a different type of ancient environment. Instead of the lake and river habitats examined earlier in the mission, researchers are now interested in the possibility that hydrothermal activity once occurred in this region.

Such systems form when water interacts with hot rocks, potentially creating chemical conditions that could support microbial life.

So far, Perseverance has gathered 29 samples of rock and regolith, the loose material that covers the Martian surface. These samples come from several distinct environments, including the crater floor, a fan-shaped deposit left by an ancient river, and the crater rim.

Some rocks in the rim region formed during major impacts approximately 4 billion years ago. They preserve evidence from an extremely early period of planetary history, much of which has been lost on Earth through geological activity.

After completing its investigation of Lac de Charmes, Perseverance is expected to continue traveling along the crater rim before heading south during its extended mission.

One future destination is Rainbow Falls, an enormous piece of ancient Martian crust measuring 328 feet (100-meter-wide). The formation was pushed upward by the powerful impact that originally created Jezero Crater.

As Perseverance continues its journey, each additional mile could bring scientists closer to understanding the ancient environments of Mars and whether the Red Planet once possessed the conditions necessary for life.

#2945. How to Move and Get Fit the Primal Way

There are certain fundamental inputs every person needs to be healthy: nutritious food, plenty of sleep, social connection, and the one we’re talking about today, movement.

Movement is an essential part of the Primal Blueprint master plan for crafting your strongest, fittest, most dynamic, most enjoyable life. That’s what I want for all my readers—a long life well lived until the end. To reach that goal, you need a strong foundation of physical health and mobility, starting as young as possible to stave off the inevitable physical decline that comes with age.

Everyone knows how to move. We’ve all heard that exercise is important, that we should be aiming for 10,000 steps a day, building core strength, and actually using our gym memberships. Yet many, nay most, people don’t do the right amount, either moving too little or too much (the former being far more common). Nor do they move in diverse enough ways to achieve their optimal fitness and longevity potential.

In short, they don't move in accordance with the Primal Blueprint.

Humans are designed to move through the world. To be active. To physically manipulate our environments.  Our ancestors, of course, had no choice in the matter. They didn’t worry about becoming strong or physically competent. Their fitness was a natural result of living in an unforgiving environment that required them to work for everything—food, shelter, water, transportation, socializing, defense, and every other aspect of survival.

But life is easy now, at least when it comes to physical demands. We modern folks do need to work at being fit.  When I was devising the Primal Blueprint, I considered how our ancestors moved, what qualities they embodied, and what, therefore, our genes need and expect for optimum health. Here’s what I came up with:

Humans are designed to move a lot—mostly slowly, sometimes very fast.

The human body is meant to move through all planes of motion: to push, pull, lift, jump, spring, sprint, throw, and balance.

Our ancestors had to be physically strong, powerful, and quick on their feet, with a good strength-to-body weight ratio and plenty of endurance.

With this in mind, I came up with the following Primal Blueprint movement laws.

Four of the ten Primal Blueprint Laws speak to how you should be moving. It’s that important.

We know that our ancestors spent, on average, several hours each day moving about at what today’s exercise physiologists might describe as a “low-level aerobic pace.” They hunted, gathered, foraged, wandered, scouted, migrated, climbed, and crawled. This low level of activity prompted their genes to build strong muscles, bones, and joints; to build a robust cardiovascular system; and to burn fat for fuel.  Today’s sedentary-dominant lifestyle is about as far from this as we could possibly get. Yet, our genes still expect us to move frequently, mostly at a slow pace. From a chronic health perspective, this is arguably one of the most detrimental discrepancies between Primal and modern life.

By the same token, until relatively recently, there was simply no way to get by without moving heavy things like wood, stone, water, animals killed many miles from home, and one’s own body weight. Moving heavy things, what we’d now call “resistance training” when done in an exercise setting, makes you strong.

Strength isn’t just about having an attractive physique or succeeding in sport, although those are perfectly good reasons to want to be strong. Muscle is metabolically active tissue and a major repository for blood glucose (glycogen). Too little muscle means a greater risk for insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction. A weak musculoskeletal system equals frailty, and frailty is strongly associated with mortality as we age.

Going all-out delivers a powerful signal to your genes, telling them, “Hey, we need to be ready to perform at maximum capacity at a moment’s notice.” For Primal humans, this would have been a matter of life and death. The resultant hormonal cascade and adaptation make the body fitter and more resilient to future challenges.

Hunter-gatherers generally worked fewer hours and had more leisure time than the average 40-hour-plus American worker. Once the day’s catch was complete, and the roots, shoots, nuts, and berries had been gathered, our ancestors spent hours involved in various forms of social interaction that we might categorize today as “play.”

The net effect was to solidify social bonds and sort out the social hierarchy, stimulate creative thinking and problem solving, practice important skills like climbing or spear throwing in a low-stakes environment, and pass on wisdom. Play was also important to prompt the release of endorphins (feel-good brain chemicals) and to mitigate any lingering stress effects of life-threatening situations. You might not need to be adept at spear throwing in your daily life, but the other benefits of play remain as relevant today as ever for kids and adults alike.

Maybe your goal is to hike the Pacific Crest Trail. Maybe it’s to be able to spend the whole day on your feet at Disney World with your family. Maybe you love CrossFit or you want to run your first 5k. Maybe your job requires that you meet certain physical fitness standards. Perhaps you just want to live a long, healthy life, but you never want to see the inside of a gym.

These are all valid. As I’ve said many times, the best exercise is the one you will do consistently.

Whatever your goals, the Primal Blueprint Movement Laws provide a springboard to build a broad base of fitness.

Crucially, the Primal Blueprint will also help you avoid the pitfalls of chronic cardio and other overly stressful exercise patterns. These movement laws provide the right formula for getting lean and functionally fit for life with minimal time commitment, pain, suffering, and sacrifice—and, more importantly, with as much fun, enjoyment and ease as possible.

It’s not the only way to get fit, but it might just be the most efficient, effective, and pleasurable way.

#2947. The Best Islands in the United States: 2026 Readers’ Choice Awards

Trends come and go, but here at Condé Nast Traveler, one thing never changes: your insatiable appetite for seeing the world. This year our readers cast an almighty 798,867 votes in our 39th Readers’ Choice Awards to tell us about the planet’s best travel experiences: You fell hard for palace hotels in Morocco, cruised down the Amazon, and got swept up in New York City’s exhilarating energy. Now, read on to find out where your favorites fall among the winners—and get inspired for where to go next, whether it's to a top-rated hotel or resort, or centered around a worthy cruise, spa, or island. These are the best islands in the US as voted by you, our readers.

View all of the the Readers’ Choice Awards winners for 2026 here.

A gated barrier island halfway between Charleston and Savannah, Fripp Island is the invitation to totally immerse yourself in the Lowcountry way of life you've been waiting for. With two 18-hole championship golf courses—Ocean Creek Golf Club and Ocean Point Golf Links—the main way of getting around these parts is by golf cart. Be sure to go beyond the manmade greens to spy the island's abundant wildlife, which ranges from loggerhead sea turtles to the over 175 species of birds who pass by.

Pro tip: While it may be missing a tropical tree or two, Fripp might strike some as familiar as it was a filming location for Forrest Gump, specifically for the scenes set in Vietnam: Take in the same stretch of the Atlantic coast Tom Hanks jogged along on morning runs while filming.

Getting there: Take I-95 to the end of local highway US-21 for the Fripp Island Bridge. Hilton Head Airport (HHH) is the closest option, but Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport (SAV) and Charleston International Airport (CHS) are also within driving distance.

Just under a 30-minute drive from Savannah, this Southern Georgia barrier island is ideal for nature and history lovers who can make a day out of visiting the Tybee Island Lighthouse (the oldest in the state) and Fort Pulaski National Monument. Don't miss feasting on massive crab legs at Sting Ray's Seafood.

Pro tip: With Little Tybee Island close by, visiting Tybee can feel like a two-for-one deal. Don't let the name fool you; this second island is actually two times bigger, giving visitors plenty of untouched nature to explore.

Getting there: Tybee is located just 15 miles from Savannah/Hilton Head International Airport—which is named one of the best airports in the US by our readers year after year. Tybee is just 20 minutes east of Savannah’s Historic District; visitors can drive here via I-95 or I-16.

A long-beloved vacation spot for Midwesterners, most of the island (80 percent, to be exact) lies within the protected Mackinac Island State Park, meaning it’s both well-preserved and filled with natural beauty. The island is perhaps most famous, however, for the iconic Grand Hotel, with its sprawling, 660-foot front porch looking out to the water—and it's indulgent five-course dinners.

Pro tip: The island is completely car-free, meaning you’ll need to rely on biking or walking to get around. But you should definitely treat yourself to a horse-drawn taxi ride from Mackinac Island Carriage Tours, the oldest continually operating horse-and-buggy service in the world.

Getting there: Two ferries service the island seasonally: the Star Line Mackinac Island’s Hydro-Jet Ferry, departing from St. Ignace, and the Shepler’s Mackinac Island Ferry, departing from St. Ignace and Mackinaw City. There’s also the on-island Mackinac Island Airport for private charter flights.

Though this resort town is small, it's also mighty. Just seven miles of sand, marshes, and moss floating off Georgia's Atlantic coast, Jekyll Island's claim to fame rests primarily on two attractions: The primordial landscape of Driftwood Beach, which is exactly what it sounds like—a stretch of sand punctuated by weathered trees that looks especially Dalí-esque at low tide—and Jekyll Island Club Resort, a storied and exclusive property that titans of industry like the Vanderbilts and Rockefellers were once known to summer at.

Pro tip: One of the best ways to see the island is by bicycle rental from a local spot like Jekyll Wheels to pedal off and explore the 20 miles of smoothly paved trails webbed around the island.

Getting there: Take the local highway US-17 South to the Jekyll Island Causeway. Daily parking passes on the island cost $10 for standard vehicles.

The lushest and greenest of the Hawaiian Islands, Kauai more than earns its nickname, “The Garden Isle.” Most spots worth visiting require a bit of effort, as you can only reach one-fifth of the island on foot or in a car, but it’s more than worth it. You’ll need a four-wheel drive to get to Polihale Beach, for example, which is accessed via a rough and rocky five-mile-long road. But it’s arguably one of the most beautiful beaches in all of Hawaii, with sweeping sand dunes, incredible sunsets, and unforgettable stargazing thanks to the lack of light pollution. Another tough-to-access-but-worth-it spot is Honopu Beach, which you can only reach by swimming from an offshore boat or from the neighboring Kalalau Beach (a quarter-mile swim).

Pro tip: It would be a sin to visit Kauai and not spend a day on the Napali Coast. Get the best vantage points on an open-door flight over the cliffs and valleys, or join a kayak tour to paddle to its isolated beaches.

Getting there: Most airlines offer non-stop flights to Lihue Airport (LIH). There is also the option of flying into Honolulu International Airport (HNL) and hopping on a half-hour flight to Kauai.

Think of the Big Island as the ultimate adventure playground. While it’s not exactly Hawaii’s best beach destination, the island stands out for its stunning national parks and volcanoes. The 505-square-mile Hawaii Volcanoes National Park is the place to experience lava flows, craters, and steam vents—it's home to the very active Kilauea volcano. Waipi‘o Valley (known as the Valley of the Kings) is another staggering display of nature, with verdant walking trails dotted with waterfalls.

Pro tip: After a busy day spent outdoors, refuel on the Aka roll with shrimp tempura and spicy ahi at Aka Sushi Bar in Waimea. You’ll thank us later.

Getting there: Most major air carriers offer daily flights to Ellison Onizuka Kona International Airport (KOA) in Kona. If you can’t find a direct flight, fly into Honolulu International Airport (HNL) on Oahu and take a 40-minute flight to the Big Island.

The 13-mile strip of land is located off Florida’s northeastern coast, and it feels far more untouched and natural than most of the state’s other beachy locales. After soaking up the sun on nearly deserted beaches, hike a trail through Fort Clinch State Park or explore the downtown area for shopping and local restaurants.

Pro tip: Elizabeth Pointe Lodge is one of the best hotels on the island, with a beachside address and delicious breakfasts. Spend one morning spent eating French toast and gazing at the ocean from the lodge’s wraparound porch, and you’ll soon declare Amelia Island your new favorite destination.

Getting there: Amelia Island is easily accessible, located only 30 minutes by car (over the Intracoastal Waterway) from Jacksonville International Airport.

From a sandcastle-sculpting class where you can pick up the tips and tricks from the pros, to adventure parks where you can zip-line through the air, to horseback riding across the beach, South Padre Island is packed with fun. But this Texas island is also dedicated to conservation, with sea turtle rehabilitation, birding, and nature centers that welcome visitors to learn more about the unique ecosystems that make up its marshes, meadows, and shores.

Pro tip: April marks the start of nesting season for sea turtles, with early-morning hatchling releases by Sea Turtle, Inc. occurring from mid-June through August. Visitors can join in to watch the tiny creatures make their way toward the waves via the National Parks Service.

Getting there: If you plan to fly in, Brownsville SPI International Airport (BRO), Valley International Airport (HRL), and McAllen International Airport (MFE) are close to the island. From there, you'll be able to take one of the shuttles or a taxi. For those arriving by car, the island is accessible by several major US highway routes.

Flying and flopping at its finest—there's not even a single stoplight on the island—Sanibel is seated a mere three miles away from Fort Myers' mainland. To sweeten things even further, this is a two-for-one deal: Just north of the island sits its sister island, Captiva. Together, the island pair lays claim to 15 miles of beach, 25 miles of biking trails, 50 types of fish, and 230 bird species. As far as variety goes, Sanibel punches well above its weight.

Pro tip: The best souvenir to commemorate your visit is one of the 250-plus shell varieties sprinkled throughout Sanibel's shores. Be sure to visit during low tide for your pick of shells.

Getting there: Sanibel is just off Florida's I-75. From there, head to Summerlin Road and onto the Sanibel Island Causeway, which has a $6 toll to cross. The nearest airports to the island are Florida Regional South West (RSW) and Punta Gorda Airport (PGD).

Private and exclusive, Lanai has drawn the likes of Oprah, Cindy Crawford, and Tom Cruise. The main draw here, though, is not the celebrity sightings but the scenery. Polihua and Kaiolohia (also known as Shipwreck) beaches offer a taste of isolation. Kanepuu Preserve, a protected dry-land forest, is home to rare plant specimens endemic to Hawaii.

Pro tip: It may not be on the beach, but the mountaintop Sensei Lanai, a Four Seasons Resort is an idyllic spot to recharge with daily exercise classes, meditations, massage treatments, and a Nobu restaurant.

Getting there: There are no direct flights to Lanai from the continental US; you’ll have to fly to Honolulu International Airport (HNL) or Kahului Airport (OGG) in Maui, where you can take a local airline to Lanai Airport (LNY).

Travelers return to this quintessential summer getaway every year to walk among the narrow rows of wood-paneled houses and bike out to the lighthouses that ring the island. Although crescent-shaped Nantucket may be small, it has a mix of terrains, from sand dunes and salt marshes to craggy bluffs, making it nearly impossible to take a bad photograph on the island.

Pro tip: If you’re in Nantucket, you’re eating seafood. For a good old-fashioned clam bake, head to Sayle’s Seafood, where $46 will get you piles of lobster, oysters, mussels, clam chowder, corn, and potatoes.

Getting there: Nantucket is easily accessible via plane from most airports in the Northeast. But we’re fans of the relaxed ferry rides from Cape Cod (one hour) and New York (six hours), where you can enjoy the coastal scenery.

Oahu is the most-visited of Hawaii’s islands—it has Honolulu, Waikiki Beach, and the North Shore, with its world-renowned surf breaks and garlic-shrimp vendors, to thank for that. Many of the tourist-heavy attractions are actually worth visiting, like Pearl Harbor and Doris Duke’s Shangri La, but you can dodge the crowds if you know where to look. The North Shore is usually your best bet, with places like Mokulei Beach Park offering excellent snorkeling, whale watching, and calm waters—and few other visitors.

Pro tip: You’d be missing out if you didn't head for a meal in Honolulu’s red-hot Chinatown. Stop by Wing for some shave ice, Char Hung Sut Restaurant for pork buns, and Livestock Tavern for the perfect bacon cheeseburger.

Getting there: Hawaii’s main airport in Honolulu is easily reachable from many US cities.

Hawaii’s second-largest island is known for its volcanic beaches, oceanfront resorts, and the towering Mount Haleakala, a traveler favorite to scale for sunrise. It’s the place to be for snorkelers, hikers, and food lovers alike. It also happens to be a great place to travel with kids, with tons of family-friendly resorts and activities for all ages: Think ziplines, turtle-spotting canoe tours, luaus, and even a pretty awesome goat farm.

Pro tip: To catch as much of Maui’s natural beauty as possible, drive the Road to Hana, a 52-mile scenic route featuring dramatic waterfalls that includes the spectacular Seven Sacred Pools.

Getting there: Many mainland airlines offer direct routes to Maui from airports on the West Coast. Otherwise, it’s about a 40-minute flight to the island from Honolulu International Airport (HNL).

Kiawah Island is seemingly custom-made for escaping the drudgery of the work week. Bring the family to the barrier island for a weekend at the luxe oceanfront Sanctuary Hotel at Kiawah Island, rated by our readers as one of the top resorts in the South, for some friendly rivalry on the golf course and tennis court (and plenty of water time in the many pools).

Pro tip: Kiawah Beachwalker Park at the southwest tip of the island is a rare public beach on Kiawah, and one of the best beaches near Charleston—enjoy sand as soft as talcum powder for only a $5 to $10 fee, in season.

Getting there: It’s about an hour’s drive from Charleston International Airport (CHS) over a causeway from the mainland.

#2949. Satellite Images of Penguin Droppings Reveal Impact of Antarctic Warming

An aerial photo of a penguin colony on an island off the coast of the Antarctic Peninsula. Penguin guano leaves a clear mark on the landscape. Thomas Sayre-McCord / WHOI / MIT

Penguin colonies are so large that their masses of excrement can be seen from space, offering scientists a useful means of studying the Antarctic birds. An analysis of 30 years of satellite images of penguin guano shows how the birds’ diets are shifting in response to warming.

Penguin droppings not only amass in huge volumes, but also stand out clearly against the white snow blanketing most of Antarctica. In 2024, scientists discovered four new colonies of emperor penguins by scanning satellite imagery for the signature brown streak left by their excrement.

For the new study, scientists analyzed subtle shifts in the color of Adélie penguin guano to track changes in their diet. First, they collected samples of guano and analyzed their color and contents, which allowed them to infer what penguins were eating by studying satellite imagery. Diets rich in krill — tiny, shrimplike crustaceans — lent guano a pink color.

Ecologist Casey Youngflesh spreads out Adélie guano samples. Casey Youngflesh

Scanning roughly 4,000 images gathered between 1984 and 2013, scientists found that when sea ice is abundant, penguins eat more fish, and when sea ice is scarce, they rely more on krill.

With warming, sea ice is declining, hitting its lowest mark ever in 2023. And that is a problem for penguin chicks, which are typically heavier when fed fish than when fed krill, and are more likely to survive until adulthood. The new study, published in Current Biology, finds that in places where penguins are eating fewer fish, their numbers are dropping.

An Adélie penguin feeds a chick. Jerzy Strzelecki via Wikipedia

“Adélie penguins are an iconic species breeding all around the continent of Antarctica,” said coauthor Michael Polito, an ocean scientist at the University of California, Santa Cruz. “They act as a ‘canary in the coal mine,’ and our study illustrates how recent warming has disrupted the Antarctic marine food web they rely on to the detriment of many of their populations.”

Warming is not the only challenge facing penguins. At the same time that the loss of sea ice is cutting off access to fish, industrial supertrawlers are vacuuming up thousands of tons of Antarctic krill each year to feed a global market for dietary supplements and animal feed.

Scientists say both the retreat of sea ice and the loss of krill are driving declines of emperor penguins, which were listed as endangered by the International Union for Conservation of Nature earlier this year. A recent study found that krill fishery managers are not accounting for the compounding effects that krill fishing and warming are having on penguins.
