#653. Ukraine: Life near the front line in Sloviansk

As Russian continues to attack Sloviansk in eastern Ukraine, thousands of residents refuse to leave or struggle to find a way out. For many, poverty, family ties and beloved pets make evacuation painfully difficult.

A few days ago, three Russian aerial bombs struck a residential neighborhood on the outskirts of Sloviansk. One hit a five-story apartment building, injuring seven people. Along with neighboring Kramatorsk, Sloviansk is one of the last major towns in the eastern Ukrainian region of Donetsk still under Ukrainian control. Since summer, the city has been a combat zone.

In a courtyard surrounded by damaged apartment blocks, a dusty carpet hangs over the metal frame of a swing set. Tetjana, a pensioner who lives in the building across the street, has brought it out of her rubble-strewn apartment to clean it. She was in her kitchen when the bombs struck.

"The bomb hit the second floor, and a woman who lived on the fourth floor was screaming," she tells DW. "They managed to rescue her before that entire section of the building collapsed," she adds.

Despite being offered the opportunity to evacuate, Tetjana has yet to leave Sloviansk. "I don't know where I would go," she says. Rent can cost up to €400 ($449) a month. Tetjana's pension is the equivalent of just €67 ($75) a month. Even with monthly relocation assistance of about €40 ($45), she could not make ends meet. "I couldn't live on that. Nowhere!"

Meanwhile, electricity and water are still available in her neighborhood, but the gas supply has been cut off. "We'll get through this," Tetjana says hopefully. "Everything will be OK. That's what I tell everyone. I want that so much."

Days after the attack, the few residents remaining in the damaged buildings were still clearing out their apartments. But another attack hit as they worked. This time, the bombs struck a building housing a state-run emergency assistance center and the Red Cross. While firefighters put out the blaze, rescue workers and volunteers retrieved belongings left inside and cleared away broken glass. Eight people were injured in the attack.

"I was on the balcony when there was a deafening bang. It felt as if someone had hit me over the head," says Natalja, describing one of the recent attacks. The pensioner comes to this part of the city to care for her twin sister. "She can no longer get around. Today, I couldn't bring her bread, only crispbread," she tells DW. Although shops and a few stalls remain at the burned-out market, bread is not available every day.

"I tried to persuade my sister to leave. I would go to a nursing home and she would go to a hospital. But her dog is sick, and I have one, too," Natalja says, noting that pets are not allowed during evacuations. "I can't leave my sister behind. She is worried and angry with me, as if everything were my fault. I have no strength left."

Natalja slowly sets off for home, "so I can get there before dark." The city center is nearly 4 kilometers (2.5 miles) away. Public transportation has been suspended since 30 people were killed in an attack on two buses in neighboring Kramatorsk.

The center of Sloviansk is heavily damaged. Few people are out on the streets, though a handful of shops remain open. Of the city's 100,000 residents, about 20,000 remain, most of them elderly people who are unable to leave.

Vasyl, a pensioner, is from the village of Mayaky, north of Sloviansk. A neighbor drove him into the city center, where he sat down for lunch in a cafe. "We're used to living under fire by now," he says. "But it's a bad habit. We should have broken it long ago."

Vasyl wants peace for his homeland. But he rejects Russian President Vladimir Putin's demand that the part of the Donetsk region under Ukrainian control be handed over to Moscow.

Two elderly women talk on a street covered with nets to protect against drones. "I can't remember the last time I slept through the night," says Iryna, a pensioner. "It's terrible, but I have nowhere to go. I'm supposed to leave my two cats behind, but I love them so much. They're all I have."

"We can only hide, trembling, and pray to God that everything will be all right," Iryna continues. "When I have to go to the market, I don't know if I'll make it there alive."

Russian drones attacked the central market several weeks ago, injuring at least three people. Since then, fewer vendors and customers have been coming to the market.

Valeriya sells women's underwear. With kindergartens closed, she has to bring her 5-year-old daughter Sofia to work. Their neighborhood in Sloviansk is not yet subject to the mandatory evacuation of families with children.

"We're used to this life by now. We were evacuated in 2022, but later returned. Now we're waiting to be evacuated again," Valeriya says.

Sofia races through the market aisles with her friend Anya, who is a year older. Anya's mother is a soldier defending her hometown. Her grandmother, Tetjana, sits nearby selling men's clothing. She, too, has to look after her granddaughter while working.

Tetjana has started shipping her belongings to the Dnipropetrovsk region, where she hopes to start a new life. She is sending them while the post office is still open. "It hurts to see our Ukraine being destroyed," she says, fighting back tears. "Who will rebuild our cities?"

She hopes to return one day. But the fate of Bakhmut weighs on her mind. "Look at Bakhmut. There are no houses left. I'm counting on our boys to hold our cities, even though it's very difficult." Bakhmut, about 50 kilometers (31 miles) from Sloviansk, fell to Russian forces in 2023 after months of heavy fighting.

The women see little hope for a ceasefire. "Putin won't want one. And our younger generation will ask us what we fought for in the first place," Tetjana says. She believes Russia must withdraw from Ukrainian territory.

Drones buzz overhead as gunfire echoes across the market. "It's hard for our boys to shoot them down," Tetjana says. "But they're doing their best."

#659. Is the Military Low-T?

Last week, a research letter published in the Journal of the American Medical Association—using data from a sample of nearly five hundred military members—reported that approximately twenty-five per cent of participants had at least one measured testosterone level that could qualify them as deficient. The letter is something of a Rorschach test, offering new information that could either bolster or undermine Pete Hegseth’s effort to establish a “high-T Department of War.” Universal screening for testosterone deficiency among men thirty and older has become one of Hegseth’s signature policies: “No fatties, no trannies, no beardos, no weirdos,” he crankily told a crowd of mid-level military members last month, during his “State of the Force” speech. “The patriotic cowboys are in—with testosterone testing on top.”

Every relevant medical society recommends against screening asymptomatic men for testosterone deficiency, because it is akin to scouring a hotel room with a black light: you’re going to find a lot of stuff of indeterminate significance that you wish you hadn’t seen. The data in the JAMA letter, collected across five U.S. military sites between August, 2024, and January, 2026, suggest that more than a hundred thousand men in current service could qualify for testosterone-replacement therapy (TRT) if their screening results are treated as diagnostic. Other military data collected between 2018 and 2025 had estimated the prevalence of diagnosed testosterone deficiency in male military members at less than one per cent.

This variation reflects that a diagnosis of testosterone deficiency can be made rigorously, with a desire to rule out, or capaciously, with a desire to rule in. Decreased testosterone is often cited as an explanation for an array of nonspecific symptoms, including depressed mood, poor libido, fatigue, and spotty memory. Serum testosterone levels vary with stress, sleep, body shape, and time of day; borderline low levels are common and are hard to interpret outside the context of a careful clinical evaluation. Different organizations even use different thresholds to determine what a “low” value is: the Centers for Disease Control uses two hundred and sixty-four nanograms per decilitre, whereas the American Urological Association and the military use three hundred.

These ambiguities make the condition a perfect container for all manner of dysphoria and shame, and set patients and doctors up for conflict. Before prescribing TRT, evidence-based doctors will require multiple confirmatory tests, disregard symptoms that are vague and easily attributed to other causes, and recommend other interventions to boost testosterone production. Patients who are intent on starting injections may then flee to wellness influencers and direct-to-consumer prescribers. “I’ve been experiencing lack of energy, motivation, and 0 sex drive,” an aspiring T user lamented on Reddit’s r/Testosterone forum, dismayed that his physician would not offer him a prescription because his blood work looked fine.

TRT might restore a man’s sex drive, build lean muscle, and boost energy, but it can also accelerate hair loss and lead to abnormal blood counts and clotting. (It also carries a risk of infertility; wanton TRT use among America’s soldiers risks doubling as an inadvertent sterilization effort.) Nonetheless, testosterone prescriptions have accelerated wildly in recent years, both from licensed sources and on the gray market. The fastest growth has been among young people, who sometimes inject doses intended to push testosterone levels far beyond the usual physiologic range.

In the MAHA/MAGA manosphere, the epithet “Low T” stands in for a failure to meet increasingly punitive standards of maleness. “It’s about restoring and optimizing your natural capabilities, protecting your longevity, and insuring you have the biological foundation required to sustain the fight,” Hegseth said when he announced the policy in July, emphasizing that America’s soldiers should remain on “the leading edge of lethality.” But optimization is a goal with no clear threshold for success: one can always look better, feel better, or, I suppose, be something closer to a killing machine. Hegseth is promoting gender-affirming care for cis men, encouraging them to better align their bodies with his idea of what they should be.

When I learned about testosterone deficiency during medical school, twenty years ago, the term referred to a circumscribed diagnosis requiring the presence of specific signs and symptoms: absent libido, infrequent erections, scant body hair, and blood work demonstrating low morning testosterone levels on multiple occasions. It was considered uncommon, especially in young men, yet under-recognized in many instances. (The most common genetic cause of primary testosterone deficiency is the presence of an extra X chromosome; it’s a disorder called Klinefelter syndrome, which goes undiagnosed in upward of fifty per cent of cases.) I’ve never diagnosed low T, although I’ve had patients request an evaluation. This means I’ve probably missed it, but not in the patients who thought they had it.

Separately, doctors have long prescribed TRT to older men for quality-of-life reasons. Men’s testosterone production decreases with age, and older men might experience its lack in much the same way that menopausal women experience decreased estrogen production: fatigue, loss of libido, increased frailty. In both of these cases, hormone prescriptions are not recreational, but they are elective; the drug is being used to supplement rather than restore, and “the real question is benefit” rather than diagnosis, Brad Anawalt, an endocrinologist and men’s-health expert at University of Washington School of Medicine, told me.

Over the past decade, however, several factors have shifted the norms around who seeks a testosterone prescription and who gets one. “Hormones have become magical,” Anawalt said, and testosterone is “the most magical of all. It’s genuinely hard to have a candid conversation about the science of testosterone with almost anybody, because there’s so much emotion in it.” In part, this is because low testosterone, which was previously treated as a source of individual suffering, has been cast as a social crisis. Testosterone levels have dropped worldwide by as much as fifty per cent since the nineteen-seventies—a decrease associated with the rising prevalence of obesity, because excess fat tissue decreases the production of testosterone and its binding proteins.

Low T due to obesity is best treated through weight loss and life-style modifications, but because testosterone supplementation can help shed pounds, many men would rather start there. Members of online testosterone forums often introduce themselves by sharing their age, their weight, and, sometimes, a photo. After a period on testosterone, they’ll post an “after” shot—washboard abs, straining traps, bulging pecs. “You look superb, man” is a typical encouraging response; “kind words, bro, thank you” a customarily humble reply.

In addition to obesity, some researchers posit that hormone-disrupting chemicals, such as the phthalates and bisphenol A (BPA) found in certain plastics, may have a role. This is a favorite preoccupation of Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., and who himself has copped to taking testosterone supplements to maintain his ropey physique, and who calls the global testosterone drop an “existential problem.”

A worldwide decrease in testosterone is notable, and might be a problem, but its public-health significance isn’t yet clear. Testosterone has been measured differently over time and across studies, often without controlling for relevant concerns such as participant B.M.I. or presence of symptoms. Kennedy told a Fox News host that “a teen-ager today, an American teen-ager, has less testosterone than a sixty-eight-year-old man,” which is a dramatic overread of current trends: with the data we have, we can say with confidence only that average testosterone levels have decreased over time, but not much about in whom, why, or what it means. Lower testosterone on average across a cohort doesn’t equate to meaningful clinical deficiency in an individual, since a fifty-per-cent drop might still land someone comfortably within the normal range. Still, some researchers—and many men seeking testosterone prescriptions—argue that “normal” should be an age-adjusted variable. “To optimize your overall physical (sexual/athletic/aesthetic) performance, a man 40s and 50s needs to be in the 700-1000 range,” one r/Testosterone regular advised his comrades.

Low testosterone is often diagnosed without any measured testosterone recorded for the patient at all. Data from 2018 suggest as many as one quarter of men are prescribed testosterone without recent blood work. That’s what we know from the doctor-to-pharmacy world; we have much less insight into the prescribing habits of direct-to-consumer (D.T.C.) platforms, and even less clarity on how much testosterone is being purchased on the gray market with no prescription at all. In one study, D.T.C. providers were happy to prescribe testosterone to a secret shopper with a level of 675 ng/dl. Whatever we might think the rate of testosterone use is, it’s almost certainly an undercount.

The Trump Administration draws a sharp distinction in legitimacy between the use of hormone therapy in the case of transgender-affirming care versus in cisgender-affirming care. I asked Anawalt to help me puzzle over why I consider these interventions discontinuous with one another, rather than variations on a theme. “The trans population I take care of—a fair number of them aren’t looking to become something different than the gender they feel they are,” Anawalt told me. “They’re not looking to become supermen or superwomen.” This was a provocative suggestion: that transgender care, by offering harmony between a body and an identity, is, in a sense, naturalizing, whereas TRT used to compensate for insecurity and enhance masculinity might be the more unnatural intervention.

Testosterone’s current cultural ubiquity “reduces masculinity to a single number,” Anawalt said, even as that number fluctuates and means little out of context. Hormones work via exquisitely calibrated feedback systems, sensitive to all sorts of stresses and rhythms, acting as messengers across our internal ecologies to keep things in balance—a balance that can easily be disrupted by circumstance. In 2020, a group of researchers led by Christopher Frueh, a clinical psychologist at the University of Hawaii at Hilo, published a description of Operator syndrome, a cluster of symptoms—including sleep disturbances, psychiatric illness, chronic pain, and hormone disruptions, and symptoms of traumatic brain injuries—observed in some special-operations personnel after prolonged periods of physical and emotional stress. Testosterone deficiency is a feature of Operator syndrome, and Hegseth has cited the condition as a rationale for his screening program.

Yet it seems reductionist to think of the individuals who fit this profile as primarily “testosterone deficient.” Rather, testosterone production is suppressed in men living under stressful conditions as “an adaptation to energy scarcity, not a disease, and it typically resolves within days of restored sleep and intake,” as the military’s own document on testosterone screening acknowledges. Evidence that TRT helps men with Operator syndrome is mostly hypothetical, and no prospective study has been conducted. In one small case series comparing a handful of special-operations personnel who were on testosterone with those who weren’t, there were no observed benefits in terms of mood, sleep, or sexual function in the men taking TRT. Instead, men on testosterone reported higher pain scores, and researchers noted anecdotal reports of increased irritability and aggression. Several study participants also apparently expressed a belief that, as special forces, they ought to have testosterone levels well above those of a civilian.

There are approximately seventy thousand troops classified as special ops in the United States military, out of a force that contains some two million people. Sarojam Radhakrishnan, a former bank employee in Kerala, was diagnosed with breast cancer in 2021. Doctors prescribed life-saving targeted treatments but the monthly cost, of more than 63,000 rupees (£500), was far beyond her family’s means.

Realising that thousands of families were being ruined by the cost of such drugs, Radhakrishnan filed a petition to the Kerala high court in 2022 demanding government intervention to lower the costs.

When the case reached the supreme court, the judges looked at hospitals’ and retailers’ mark-ups on cancer drugs and called the situation “carnage” and “extortion”.

During a hearing on 29 September, justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta noted how a life-saving cancer drug supplied to hospitals at 2,700 rupees carried a vastly marked-up price of 27,000 rupees.

The judges found that hospitals, distributors and chemists enjoyed large margins on medicines. Analysis by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority revealed a trade mark-up on some cancer drugs averaging 170%, with some extreme examples stretching to more than 700%.

A 30% trade margin cap on the price of 42 drugs has been in place for some years but many others remained off this list. Now, prodded by the supreme court’s findings, the government has imposed the cap on 137 other drugs.

“The measure is expected to reduce medicine prices by up to 70% and help cancer patients save 2,500 crore rupees [£199m] annually, significantly reducing the amount they pay from their own pockets,” a statement from the Department of Pharmaceuticals said.

People on low incomes in India dread illness, as the cost of treatment can mean financial ruin, particularly for cancer patients.

Whenever the Indian Cancer Society holds a free diagnosis camp in Gurgaon where he lives, Krishan Kalra urges local drivers, maids, cooks and tailors to turn up because tests they cannot afford are offered free.

As a former member of the council of the Rajiv Gandhi Cancer Institute and Research Centre, Kalra knows the importance of early diagnosis, but he said very few people attended. “When I ask why, they say they’re too scared – if something serious like cancer is detected, they haven’t got the money to pay for the treatment so they prefer to remain in the dark,” he said.

With the cost of cancer drugs ranging in price from 38,000 to 380,000 rupees, many families quietly walk out of the doctor’s consulting room upon hearing the estimated cost of treatment.

Studies by the Public Health Foundation of India show that spending on healthcare pushes about 55 million Indians a year below the poverty line.

Few Indians can afford the premiums for comprehensive health insurance. The government’s nationwide insurance scheme, Ayushman Bharat, covers treatment for some cancers but it provides only 500,000 rupees’ worth of annual cover for a family – the cost of just one dose of Kadcyla (ado-trastuzumab emtansine), a drug used to treat breast cancer. Patients may need up to 14 doses.

Dr Ravi Kannan is a surgical oncologist and director of Cachar cancer hospital and research centre in Silchar, Assam. He has worked in hospitals elsewhere in India and is familiar with doctors’ trauma of seeing patients walk out to go home and die.

India records about 1.56m new cancer cases annually – up from 1.39m cases in 2020 – making it a significant public health challenge.

The couple had even set up their own agency, Mark Surrogacy, to lend a patina of legitimacy to their family-making operation. They eventually ended up having twenty-seven children, most of them still infants and toddlers. Many of those children, Hochman said, had been living in “a house of horrors.”

Although the parents claimed to be unaware of these acts, videos also showed them in their home office, laughing at CCTV footage of the beatings. Prosecutors were now charging them with conspiracy to commit child abuse, and Guojun with participating in the abuse himself. During one incident, Hochman said, Guojun lifted one of his sons into the air while hitting his head so hard “that the child’s body lurched in alternating directions with the blows.”

This wasn’t the first time the couple had been arrested. In May, 2025, Silvia and Guojun were jailed after one of their children, a two-month-old, was hospitalized with bleeding inside his brain. Social workers removed the baby’s siblings from the house and placed them in foster care, where they remain. After four days in custody, the parents were released. The D.A.’s office declined to file a case, requesting more investigation from the Arcadia police. At Hochman’s press conference, I asked about the timing of the charges, given that officers had seized the home videos more than a year ago. “We do it methodically,” he replied, noting that the footage required translation, as both the parents and the nannies spoke Mandarin.

After that first arrest, Silvia and Guojun kept at it. Even while under scrutiny from police and the child-welfare system, the couple went on to have six more babies through surrogates. They launched custody battles in Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Georgia, in an attempt to bring those infants to California. And, when some of the surrogates resisted that idea, the parents sued them for breach of contract and demanded a million dollars in damages. All the while, Silvia and Guojun maintained that their arrest was simply a misunderstanding, and that their vast family would soon be reunited.

Earlier this year, I published an investigation into the couple for the magazine; last month, In the Dark released a podcast series in which we expanded on that reporting. A source with knowledge of the case told me that The New Yorker’s findings—and the public scrutiny that followed—played a role in the county’s decision to press charges. According to court records, investigators observed thousands of acts of alleged abuse in the home footage, and the extent of the violence “cannot be fully conveyed through written description alone.” If convicted as charged, both parents face over a decade in prison.

News of the arrest spread quickly. Kayla Elliott, a surrogate for the couple who’d first exposed their scheme on TikTok, immediately texted a group chat composed of some of the couple’s other surrogates. “Everyone was, like, ‘What the hell?’ ” she told me. Elliott watched a live stream of the press conference from her home, in Corpus Christi, Texas; her husband watched from work. “We talked about it so many times, like, ‘What’s taking so long?’ ” she said. “ You have to try to be patient and trust the process, but it was difficult when the lives of these kids are on the line.”

#700. Trump, Putin and Xi at one table, without Ukraine. What Moscow wants from the proposal and why Beijing might agree.

Moscow is pushing for a summit with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping to discuss ending the war in Ukraine, without Kyiv or the EU. What does Russia hope to gain, and why might China be willing to take part?

The administration of US President Donald Trump has stepped up diplomatic efforts this autumn to revive stalled negotiations aimed at ending Russia's war in Ukraine.

Washington has increased pressure on Kyiv and its European partners while seeking to draw Moscow back to the negotiating table through a series of concessions, including a deal involving Russian diesel exports to the US and the temporary easing of some sanctions.

Yet the Kremlin appears in no hurry to resume meaningful peace talks. Moscow is ready to hold negotiations with Ukraine at a technical level next month, the Kremlin said after the meeting between US special envoy Steve Witkoff and Russian President Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg on October 21.

Putin agreed to resume the peace talks mediated by Washington after November 10, his aide Yury Ushakov said after the meeting. He added that the next round of negotiations could take place in Abu Dhabi and would focus on "building the security system for Russia and Ukraine after the end of the war."

According to his aide, Putin also shared some thoughts with Witkoff on the possible trilateral summit involving US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping. Ushakov pointed out that Beijing is already preparing the meeting.

Moscow proposed to hold a three-way meeting on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, scheduled for November 18-19 in Shenzhen, China, The Wall Street Journal reported earlier this week. According to WSJ sources, Putin hopes to reach an agreement with Trump and Xi on ending the war in Ukraine on terms favorable to Russia.

Moscow's proposal for the three-way summit involving Russia, the United States and China — but excluding Ukraine and the European Union — offers a glimpse of the negotiating format the Kremlin would prefer. Russia insists that Ukraine must be prepared to make territorial concessions before negotiations can move forward. Putin has also repeatedly stressed that he is willing to negotiate only if Russian interests are taken into account.

According to the WSJ, Putin views the proposed gathering in Shenzhen as a potential new Yalta Conference, referring to the 1945 meeting of the leaders of the United States, Great Britain and the Soviet Union to discuss the defeat of Nazi Germany and the postwar order in Europe.

The newspaper reported that Chinese officials had indicated through diplomatic channels that Beijing would not object to the three-way format if Trump agreed to participate. Trump, whose relations with Kyiv have deteriorated significantly in recent weeks, has also expressed openness to such talks.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy pointed out during his visit in Oslo on October 20 that talks concerning the future of his country will be acceptable only if Ukraine gets a seat at the negotiating table.

Temur Umarov, a researcher at the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center in Berlin who specializes in China-Russia relations, is not surprised by the Kremlin's proposal. He told DW that Moscow wants to feel respected by Washington and treated as a major international power.

The previous US administration was unwilling to discuss questions affecting Ukraine's sovereignty with Russia without Kyiv's participation. Now, Umarov said, the Kremlin believes such negotiations may have become possible.

Moscow apparently hopes that any agreement reached at the proposed meeting would prompt the White House to pressure Ukraine and the EU into accepting its terms. But what role would China actually play?

Umarov believes Beijing would participate largely as a symbolic partner, rather than as an active mediator responsible for the outcome. "Beijing has no intention of taking responsibility for Ukraine's future," he said.

Simply attending the summit would not impose any additional obligations on China. "It is hardly surprising that the Kremlin insists on this format," Umarov explained. "Russia wants to demonstrate that it is on an equal footing not only with the United States, but also with China. Yet, objectively speaking, Moscow is not the senior partner in that relationship."

Umarov believes China has little direct interest in such negotiations. Even if the leaders issued a joint statement, Beijing would seek language that was as vague and noncommittal as possible, he said. That would allow China to present almost any subsequent development in the war as consistent with the understanding reached at the summit. For Umarov, such flexibility would be essential to Beijing's participation.

However, Yurii Poita, a senior associate researcher at the Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS), offers a different assessment. He believes China and Russia have overlapping strategic interests, particularly in their efforts to promote an alternative security architecture across Eurasia.

Both countries, he argues, would benefit from a reduction in Western military presence in Europe and Asia and from a weakening of NATO's influence. "If Russia and China manage to convince the United States to reduce its military presence in Europe and military assistance to Ukraine, Russia would gain additional advantages on the battlefield," Poita told DW.

He added that such a development would further undermine the EU's confidence in the United States as a NATO ally. Poita believes Moscow and Beijing would seek precisely these outcomes in any negotiations with Washington.

Michael Kimmage, director of the Kennan Institute in the United States, also doubts that Beijing is preparing to take a more active diplomatic role in ending the war.

Speaking to DW, Kimmage said China may see the conflict as destabilizing and would prefer to maintain trade relations with Ukraine and other countries in the region. But he believes another priority outweighs those concerns. "More important to China is the political survival of Vladimir Putin," he said.

According to Kimmage, Russia provides China with several geopolitical advantages, including low-cost energy and a foreign policy partner that rarely stands in Beijing's way.

He believes China will continue indirectly supporting Russia's military efforts to prevent a Russian defeat. An outright Russian victory may not necessarily be Beijing's objective, he said, but ensuring Putin does not lose is crucial. For that reason, Kimmage does not expect China to pressure the Russian president into ending the war if Putin is unwilling to do so.

Neither Ukraine nor the European Union would be part of the proposed talks in China.

Kateryna Krasina, a junior researcher at the Kyiv-based New Geopolitics Network, believes their exclusion could reveal how Beijing actually views their international influence.

In her assessment, China's approach suggests that it does not regard Ukraine as an independent player in the negotiations and considers Europe relatively weak.

Kimmage, however, argues that excluding Europe from the summit would not necessarily diminish its influence over the course of the negotiations.

He acknowledged that European governments may have little power to prevent a meeting between Trump, Putin and Xi. But he pointed to European efforts surrounding the 2025 Alaska summit between Trump and Putin, which he believes helped prevent outcomes that would have been unfavorable to Europe.

Kimmage said Europe's influence has grown through financial assistance to Ukraine and extensive defense industry cooperation, particularly involving Germany and other European countries.

He argued that European support has played an important role in Ukraine's ability to resist Russian advances. "And that support equals leverage," Kimmage said.

He believes Russia, China and the United States may all be interested in portraying Europe as a secondary power, but that such a portrayal would overlook Europe's actual influence.

"The optics and the reality are not quite the same," he said, drawing a distinction between the appearance of great-power diplomacy resembling the Yalta Conference and Europe's role in supporting Ukraine.

He believes that any diplomatic agreement between the US, Russia and China would face the reality on the ground.

"China understands that whatever the outcome of negotiations with the United States and Russia, the reality on the ground will be very different and will depend above all on Ukraine," he said.

"Whether the Kremlin likes it or not, Ukraine has agency of its own. Neither the United States nor Western countries can fully determine what happens on the battlefield through diplomatic pressure alone."
