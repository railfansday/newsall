#1351. The Boox Palma 2 Pro finally got me to stop reading on my phone

The $400 Boox Palma 2 Pro is a smartphone-sized e-reader designed for people who want to read books, articles, and documents on a small E Ink display. However, it offers more than just a dedicated reading experience because it runs Android and supports Google Play, giving you access to third-party apps. Think of it as a cross between a Kindle and a compact Android tablet.

What drew me to reviewing the Palma 2 Pro was its size. When I’m not reading a physical book, I like to lie on my side and read at night before bed. Most e-readers are squarish and hard to hold without something like a PopSocket, and they can feel too big for reading while lying down. So even though I own a Kindle, I often ended up reading on my iPhone simply because it was more comfortable in my hand. But, reading on your phone for too long can of course cause eye strain. The Palma 2 Pro ultimately solved this problem for me during the two months I spent testing it.

The device’s front-lit screen lets you choose between cool and warm lighting. This was a big plus for me because when I’m reading in bed at night, I don’t want to disturb my sleeping husband with the light. While you can lower the brightness on a smartphone, the Palma 2 Pro can be dimmed to an exceptionally low level. Even when reading in complete darkness, I could see the screen clearly without straining my eyes or disturbing my husband.

Not only was the Boox Palma 2 Pro easier on my eyes, but it was also noticeably lighter than my iPhone, weighing just 175 grams and making it even more comfortable to hold.

And since it’s pocket-sized, I ended up carrying it everywhere, from the doctor’s office to the airport, especially since it fits in my small, everyday purse that isn’t big enough for a Kindle. So, in moments when I would normally scroll on my phone, I was getting some reading in instead.

Since it’s not a locked-down e-reader, you can download apps like Kindle, Kobo, and Libby, as well as apps for audiobooks, podcasts, read-it-later services, and email. This means that you don’t have to use the built-in reader app, and can choose to read on your preferred app.

Access to apps turns the compact E Ink device into a versatile tool for reading, research, and productivity. It’s worth noting that while scrolling through these apps, animations and some interfaces may feel sluggish because not all apps are optimized for E Ink. And while the device is capable of displaying color, images will definitely look less vibrant than they would a smartphone screen.

For me, the Palma 2 Pro became a dedicated device for reading and listening to audiobooks, which allowed me to enjoy the best of both worlds without the usual distractions that come with a smartphone.

The Boox Palma 2 Pro also features a rear camera for scanning documents, a built-in speaker for listening to audio without headphones, and a hybrid SIM slot for cellular data on the go.

The device is compatible with a stylus, so you can take notes and mark up PDFs. However, I can’t speak to that experience since I didn’t receive a stylus with my review unit.

As for battery life, the device lasted about 5–7 days, which worked for me but could be a downside for people who are used to Kindles, which can last for weeks on a single charge. Additionally, if you want to get more juice out of the device, you can just turn off Wi-Fi.

One downside I found, especially for an e-reader at this price point, is that there’s quite a bit of ghosting. Text from a previous page can linger on the screen when you turn to the next page or return to the home screen. There’s a refresh button in the navigation bar that quickly clears it, but it may get annoying for some users to repeatedly have to do that.

I think the Boox Palma 2 Pro is a great device for a very specific type of reader: someone who wants a smartphone-sized device for comfort, portability, as well as access to apps. If you’re someone who loves your Kindle, I don’t think this device is for you. But if, like me, you’re tired of reading on your phone and dealing with eye strain or headaches, the Boox Palma 2 Pro is a great option.

Of course, the price is quite steep for an e-reader, so you’ll have to decide whether the added versatility and portability are worth the extra cost. It’s worth mentioning that Boox is launching a non-Pro Palma 3 soon for $340 that features a black and white display and replaces the Palma 2.

As is tradition, iFixit has torn open the latest iPhone and laid its parts bare on a table. It found some interesting details about how Apple might be hiding the crease on the Duo’s folding screen and gave it a 5/10 repairability score. That might not seem particularly high, but it’s the best score the site has given to a foldable yet.

The internal screen’s matte nano-texture cover doesn’t just make it more readable in bright sunlight; it also helps mask the crease by scattering reflections. When looking at the display under a microscope, iFixit also found oval-shaped subpixels arranged at various angles, which the site believes are designed to reduce artifacts and further mask the crease down the middle of the display.

The nano-texture outer layer is relatively easy to replace without swapping out the OLED underneath. If you do need to repair the display itself, however, good luck. As for reports of damaged screens and cracking hinges, iFixit has some theories. The hinge on the iPhone Duo is incredibly complex, and they suspect part of it sometimes isn’t descending properly. There is also an accordion cable that passes over that exact spot, and it’s near the edge of the battery. It’s possible this is concentrating pressure and leading to the “audible crack” reported by some and the broken screens. Apple did not immediately return a request for comment.

#1355. The Terrifying Future of States’ Criminalizing Pregnant People Is Already Here

The idea of charging people who have abortions with murder is gaining traction on the right. But a report finds that people are already being prosecuted for their pregnancy outcomes.

Anti-abortion rights demonstrators march to the Supreme Court for the 52nd annual March For Life in Washington, DC, on January 24, 2025.

Since the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade in 2022, the idea of charging people who have abortions with murder has gained terrifying new traction. During her brief stint as White House press secretary, Katie Zacharia became just the latest prominent conservative to draw attention for her ties to a radical wing of the anti-abortion movement that wants to charge people who have abortions with murder. This year alone, state lawmakers floated 14 bills to charge people who have abortions with homicide. (The idea is still so unpopular across the political spectrum that none of those bills passed.) Yet this whole debate is, in a sense, a red herring, because hundreds of people a year are already being prosecuted for their pregnancy outcomes. The vast majority of those cases involved a live birth where the mother was suspected of using drugs, but in a rising minority of cases since Dobbs, people are also being criminalized for pregnancy loss.

I spoke with Dana Sussman, senior vice president at Pregnancy Justice, about a report the organization published today finding that in the first three years after the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade, prosecutors have initiated at least 902 cases across the country charging individuals with crimes related to pregnancy, pregnancy loss, or birth.

I’ve edited our conversation for brevity and clarity.

Amy Littlefield: What’s new in this report, which looks at pregnancy criminalization in the first three years after Dobbs?

Dana Sussman: When Roe was overturned, we suspected that pregnancy loss would become an increasingly criminally suspect event, and we unfortunately are starting to see that play out in the data. This report finds a significant acceleration in pregnancy loss prosecutions. We now have documented 70 pregnancy loss prosecutions in the first three years post-Dobbs.

AL: I was struck by the breakdown of allegations prosecutors made in the cases you found. There were 871 allegations of substance use during pregnancy, 77 allegations of a lack of prenatal care, and just 13 related to obtaining, attempting or researching an abortion. What does this tell us about who is being criminalized for pregnancy post-Dobbs?

DS: This is the continuation of a trend that we’ve been seeing for decades. The way that pregnancy is policed and prosecuted in the United States is through the lens of substance use. The foundation for prosecuting pregnancy is based in the war on drugs from the 1980s, and the way in which Black women, in particular, were targeted for charges of child abuse when they were deemed substance users, primarily with crack cocaine and this obsession over a “crack baby epidemic” that has been thoroughly debunked. Three state supreme court decisions used pregnancy and substance use as the way to redefine who a person is [thereby extending child abuse protections to fetuses] under their state laws: Alabama, South Carolina, and Oklahoma, which consistently rank among the top three states that criminalize pregnancy.

AL: What did you find about the race and socioeconomic background of the folks who were criminalized for their pregnancy outcomes in this report?

DS: In the ’80s, ’90s, and early 2000s, we were seeing disproportionately Black women being charged with crimes in connection with pregnancy, and that has shifted since the mid-2000s until the present. The majority are white women at this point, but poverty is a very big indicator, as it is across the criminal justice system. So we’re looking at poor white women as the majority in the data set at this point. There are a few different theories from scholars about why this is. [Legal scholar] Michele Goodwin has said that Black women were the canary in the coal mine; cruelty by the state is tried out on Black women, and then it spreads more broadly. There’s also a lot of scholarship showing that when a victim is white, the perpetrator of the crime is treated more harshly, and so we are talking about the assumption that these are white babies, and so they are held in higher esteem, frankly, than non-white babies, and so their mothers are treated more harshly, potentially. There’s also shifting patterns in policing around nonviolent drug crimes; we see less of it in city centers and more of it in rural areas, and also the patterns of drug use, as well.

AL: So it’s mostly low-income white women in a small number of states. I’m looking at the map: 381 of the cases in South Carolina, 222 in Oklahoma, 204 in Alabama, and then there’s states like my home state of Massachusetts where there’s zero.

DS: Each one of those three states has proclaimed by state supreme court decision that fetuses are children under the child abuse laws. In South Carolina, it was in the late ’90s; in Alabama, it was 2013; and in Oklahoma, it was 2020. So that kind of unleashed permission for prosecutors across the state to apply laws in this way. In other states, we see prosecutors doing this, but those cases have not yet gone to the state supreme court. In Mississippi, for example, the state has no fetal personhood law or judicial decision, and yet we see 27 cases in that state of prosecutors just choosing to reinterpret laws that were never intended to be applied in this way.

AL: So what impact did Dobbs actually have on these types of prosecutions, and is Dobbs really the culprit here, or are there other, more long-standing forces at play?

DS: Two things are true here. One is that the foundation for these prosecutions was laid way before Dobbs, and it’s a continuation of a decades-long trend. Fetal personhood is at the root of these cases, and fetal personhood has existed in state law or in state judicial decisions since the 1990s. That is, ultimately, the anti-abortion movement’s goal. It wasn’t the overturning of Roe. It wasn’t sending issues back to the states. It is the federalization of fetal and embryonic personhood, because that would ban abortion without question, without exception, and it would allow for increased criminalization of people who are not performing pregnancy in the way that the powers that be might want them to. At the same time, we suspect that we are seeing more pregnancy loss cases in our data year upon year post-Dobbs, and I don’t think that should be a surprise, either. If we ban abortion and we make pregnancy loss suspicious because in some places provision of abortion has been made illegal, then we are creating an environment in which we are inviting law enforcement in to investigate how some of these pregnancies ended. That criminal response varies depending on one’s identity, one’s behavior, and one’s access to healthcare.

AL: After the Supreme Court decision in Dobbs in 2022, there were predictions that we would see a mass number of criminal cases related to abortion, because all of a sudden providing abortion was a crime in about a dozen states. How does the reality compare to those expectations?

DS: We are seeing what we expected: that this was going to fall most harshly on people who use substances during pregnancy and were going to be charged with child abuse or neglect, and that pregnancy loss would be an increasingly suspect event. I remember getting lots of questions about period-tracking apps and wiping that from your phone, and I was like, if they want to criminalize pregnancy, they have other ways to do it, and they’ve been doing it. This is not going to be some new dragnet, it’s going to be the same old ways that they’ve been doing it for a very long time, and that has played out. And the circumstances are often through healthcare, that is, the people who are reporting to law enforcement or child welfare [are often healthcare providers]. That was the case before Dobbs, and it remains the case now.

AL: Katie Zacharia recently made headlines for the fact that she was on the board of White Rose Resistance, which is part of an extreme wing of the anti-abortion movement that has called for “equal protection” laws that would treat embryos as people and abortions as murder. I’m wondering if you could reflect on the fears generated by these efforts versus what the data already show about criminalization?

DS: There are 17 states that redefine who a person is to include an embryo or a fetus right now. So this movement to provide “equal protection” to embryos and fetuses already exists in a slightly different form under many state laws.

AL: What would you tell someone who lives in a state where abortion is banned, and is ordering abortion pills to self-manage an abortion?

DS: These are an incredibly small number of cases [criminalizing self-managed abortion in a ban state] when it comes to how many abortions have happened post-Dobbs. You can still access abortion safely and confidentially, and if you are experiencing medical concerns around abortion care, you can call the Miscarriage and Abortion Hotline. You can get care that you need and you do not need to say anything about abortion. People should keep their community of people who know as small and as trusted as possible, because the most common scenario [triggering criminal prosecution] is getting reported by other people.

Amy Littlefield is The Nation’s abortion access correspondent and a journalist who focuses on reproductive rights, healthcare, and religion. Her latest book is Killers of Roe: My Investigation Into the Mysterious Death of Abortion Rights (Hachette).

#1356. Art for freedom: Maria Kalesnikava in Bonn

The Belarusian musician and civil rights activist Maria Kalesnikava held the keynote speech on the importance of art at the 2026 Beethovenfest in Bonn. She also visited DW.

Maria Kalesnikava knows all too well how authoritarian regimes repress art: The Belarusian flautist and activist spent five years in detention as a political prisoner, three of which were in solitary confinement. Speaking at this year's Beethovenfest, she said: "A person's external freedom can be taken away, sometimes almost entirely. Their freedom of movement, contacts with other people, and access to information can be limited." She pointed out that it is, however, much harder to control what has already become part of one's memory, imagination and inner experience. "And it is precisely in this that I find the explanation for why culture is so important for a free society and why authoritarian systems so often view it with distrust" she concluded during the keynote speech.

A key figure of the Belarusian opposition, the artist and political activist was arrested by the regime of Alexander Lukashenko in the summer of 2020, after the regime had violently suppressed the peaceful revolution for democracy and freedom. Across the country, mass protests and a democratic civil rights movement took place after the controversial presidential election on August 9, 2020.

Kalesnikava recalled how, during her imprisonment, she had carried her world and her experiences, with her all along. This enabled her to remain free, and not to feel like a victim. To Kalesnikava, this is precisely the power of art and also the reason why all dictators — whether Stalin, Hitler or Putin and Lukashenko — hate independent arts and culture, even if it's not overtly political.

During a panel discussion in Bonn, Kalesnikava stressed this point directly:

"The question often arises: what are we defending in Ukraine? Why do we need so much money and new weapons? For me, it is completely clear: the war Russia started is not just against Ukraine. It's a war against European values and European culture. It is a war against our civilization." Kalesnikava realized this immediately when she heard about the Russian war against Ukraine in February 2022, while she was imprisoned.

Kalesnikava was released and has been living in freedom again for ten months now — a miracle that she didn't think was possible and an achievement of international diplomacy. The fact that she emerged and returned to her life full of resilience is thanks in part to Beethoven and Bach, as Kalesnikava, who studied the flute and earned a degree in early and new music in Minsk and Stuttgart, recalled. "I spent a lot of time in complete silence. But this silence was almost never empty, because music constantly played in my head," Kalesnikava said in Bonn. "We musicians know this state very well — sometimes a melody or a musical phrase gets so stuck in your head that it's simply impossible to get rid of it. In normal life, this can even be annoying. In prison, I realized for the first time that this could bring great happiness."

During her visit to Bonn, the Belarusian activist also visited DW and appeared as a guest on the DW Russian language show "DW Novosti." Kalesnikava currently lives in Berlin following an invitation by the German government, where she is also recovering from the horrors she experienced. She sees it as her responsibility to remind the world about what is happening to more than 800 political prisoners in Belarus, as well as the importance and fragility of free artistic expression. On October 2, 2026, she held her keynote speech at the Beethovenfest in Bonn as part of a conference titled "Cultural Events as Political Spaces?," organized with the support of the German Federal Cultural Foundation.

More than 200 representatives from concert institutions and music festivals from across Germany came to the newly renovated Beethovenhalle in Bonn for the final weekend of this year's Beethovenfest. Together, they debated innovative forms and formats for music, as well as issues that stretch far beyond the music industry and urge on to think outside the box.

"Arts and culture are not responsible for taking on specific opinions from political parties, or for making explicitly political statements," Steven Walter, artistic director of the Beethovenfest and host of the conference, told DW. "What art always creates, however, is spaces for encounter, and places where people can tolerate ambivalence. And that is such an important part of our society, that we're able to handle making collective experiences." To Walter, this is precisely where arts and culture come in, as artists create spaces for independent thought, subjective interpretations, as well as doubts. "Perhaps it's true that there is something like a DNA of freedom in the nature of art," Walter explained in an interview with DW.

The festive closing of the 2026 Beethovenfest took place on 3 October, which is also German Unity Day. The BBC Philharmonic Orchestra, under the baton of conductor Anja Bihlmaier, performed Johannes Brahms' "A German Requiem," an iconic piece that evokes reconciliation and trust.

After four weeks of Beethovenfest 2026, the festival recorded the highest number of visitors in a decade. Around 35,000 people came to experience, celebrate and discuss art together at more than 100 concerts and events.

#1358. Here are the top AI agents that can live in your text messages

Rather than downloading another app, a growing number of agents can simply be texted like an ordinary person.

You text it what you need, and it can remember context, connect to the apps and services you already use, and complete tasks on your behalf. That can mean scheduling an appointment, organizing a calendar, researching a trip, sending an email, making a reservation, shopping online, or reminding you about something days later.

While Instinct is one of the buzziest AI agents at present, thanks to its $10 billion valuation after its latest funding round of $1 billion, there are many others also making a play for this space.

Below are the most notable options so far, from general-purpose personal assistants to agents designed for families, travel, and work.

Ask Proxy’s biggest differentiator is that it “thinks like a woman,” meaning it aims to anticipate your needs before you even ask, the company explains on its website.

It works like a chatbot you can text like a friend, whether you need gift ideas, help planning an event, or recommendations for things to do that weekend. You can ask it to add important dates to your calendar, set reminders, or help with everyday decisions. You can also send it photos, like an empty table, to figure out how to decorate the table for a dinner party.

Unlike a typical chatbot, Ask Proxy also takes a proactive approach. It can reach out when it notices something you might forget or when it comes across a new activity, restaurant, or experience you might enjoy.

There’s also a work-focused version, which the company explains “gets employees back to work faster, while keeping personal data off company systems.”

The personal version costs $1 per day. For businesses, pricing starts at $15 per employee per month for large companies and $23 per employee per month for small teams with fewer than 49 employees.

Ask Proxy works over SMS. You can text “start” to 820-732-2625.

Caddy is an AI assistant that turns the information scattered across your phone into things you can actually act on. It lives in iMessage for iPhone users and RCS for Android users, so there’s no separate app or inbox to constantly check.

For instance, an email contains an appointment that needs to be added to your calendar or a friend sends a list of things to pick up. Instead of worrying about all the details getting buried, Caddy connects to your calendars and conversations, then identifies things that may require action. It can add events to your calendar, set reminders, keep track of follow-ups, and even do research for you.

Caddy has been available in public beta since April 2026.

Comma is designed to manage tasks across both work and everyday life. Comma carries tasks through to completion, checking its own work and determining when a task is finished.

Completed work is surfaced for review when a decision or approval is needed. It also proactively notifies users and recommends the next steps.

It can work across a user’s browser, computer, and files, while remaining accessible remotely through messaging apps such as Signal, Telegram, and WeChat. Comma maintains context and memory across devices and conversations, and can deliver daily briefings, flag urgent matters, and summarize recorded calls.

Fambot is an AI-powered “chief of staff” for families, designed to bring together the many moving parts of family life, including school communications, sports activities, meal planning, calendars, and other day-to-day responsibilities, and turn them into an organized plan.

The service is designed to work across both an app and text messages. Fambot is available on iOS devices, Android devices, and the web, and families can also interact with it directly over SMS. Every night, Fambot automatically sends a summary of the following day, including upcoming events, to-dos, and details such as school uniform or packing requirements. Parents can reply to those messages to ask questions, check information, or make changes to their calendar.

Fambot currently connects to Gmail, Google Calendar, Outlook, and WhatsApp, with support for Apple Calendar planned for the future.

The company launched in beta in early September 2026 and has raised $3.5 million in pre-seed funding. The service is currently free during the beta period, but Fambot expects to eventually charge roughly the equivalent of a Netflix subscription.

Folk is designed to be an AI assistant you can reach through iMessage, WhatsApp, and Telegram. It can remember personal context, manage reminders, research topics, track flights, handle email, and perform real-world tasks such as restaurant reservations.

One distinction is that Folk runs on its own private cloud computer. It can also run code and execute multi-step tasks rather than merely responding conversationally.

Folk launched its beta in May 2026, and it’s available for free but also offers a Pro subscription for $8.33/month if you want unlimited tasks running in the background.

Instinct first made headlines after raising $350 million at a $2.5 billion valuation. In September 2026, it raised $1 billion, valuing it at $10 billion.

The always-available agent can take actions on your behalf, rather than simply answer questions. Users can communicate with Instinct by text or voice and connect it to the apps and services they already use, such as email, calendar, Google Workspace, and more. The company says early users have used Instinct for tasks ranging from planning trips and buying groceries to booking tickets and canceling subscriptions.

In September, Instinct began giving users dedicated email addresses for their assistants. The idea is to let Instinct create and manage accounts on a user’s behalf without filling the user’s personal inbox. The agent can use its address to sign up for services, contact businesses, follow up on requests, and handle tasks that require an email account. (However, this level of autonomy has also raised concerns about privacy and security.) It’s also begun rolling out support for placing calls.

Powered by Hermes Agent, Iris is designed to let users delegate tasks to an AI assistant directly through iMessage. Users can connect the apps they use and hand over a task with a simple message. The agent then works through the required steps, while retaining relevant context from previous interactions. It’s also designed to follow up with users proactively rather than waiting for another request.

Martin is a general-purpose personal assistant reachable through SMS as well as phone, WhatsApp, email, Slack, and its own iOS app.

It can help manage calendars, email, reminders, tasks, notes, and communications. It can also act on behalf of users by sending messages or making phone calls, while providing daily summaries of what needs attention.

It’s a bit on the pricier side, with subscriptions starting at $21 per month.

Maverick describes itself as an “AI companion who lives in your texts,” acting as a personal assistant that helps manage everyday tasks directly from your messages.

It can fix typos, cancel subscriptions, schedule meetings from voice notes, and help group chats settle on a date for an event. One notable feature is that Maverick asks for your permission before taking action.

The platform also offers hundreds of integrations, including Discord, Gmail, and Slack, allowing it to connect with the apps you already use.

It’s currently in beta and free to use on iMessage. There’s also a Pro version for €27 per month (or approximately $30.25 USD), which includes 500 tasks a month,  20 connected apps, scheduled tasks, and memory.

Miso takes the personal-assistant concept and applies it specifically to travel.

It operates through iMessage and combines AI trip planning with support from a dedicated travel team. Users can arrange flights through messaging while Miso takes into account their travel preferences, loyalty points, and other priorities. The specter of a more intensive backlash does nothing to address the issues that sent Albanian students into the streets: increased education fees, poor school conditions, and a lengthy list of government corruption scandals, including the possibly illegal sale of land to Jared Kushner for luxury property development. In lieu of endorsing reforms on all these issues, Rama chose violence.

The latest turn in Albanian protest politics will likely just ratchet up the mood of growing frustration among dissenters. For 130 days this summer, Tirana saw a wave of peaceful protests against Rama’s approval of the Kushner-backed resort project—a boondoggle that detractors have argued only benefits powerful oligarchs close to Rama’s government.

But what happened last week took everyone by surprise. The Albanian student union called for school strikes and planned a series of demonstrations at the Education Ministry in central Tirana. The protests kept growing, and eventually a handful of black-masked provocateurs began tearing out CCTV cameras and stoplights in the city center. The confrontation escalated from there: Restive protesters threw rocks, exploded fireworks, and burned dumpsters. The cops responded with tear gas, water cannons, and baton beatings. Not surprisingly, the response pushed more demonstrators into anger.

Toward the end of the week, things got ugly. Demonstrations and vandalism paralyzed the city; banks, government ministries, police stations, and Rama’s political party headquarters were battered and burned by bricks and Molotov cocktails. A pattern emerged: Daytime protests mixed peaceful protesters with teenage Albanians throwing and breaking things; and nightfall brought pitched street battles between cops and more aggressive, older, and angrier crowds. “Dialogue at any hour, on any day, anywhere, with anyone, except the political forces behind the violence that refuse to distance themselves from it,” he said. Then came his bid to keep the security forces in crackdown mode: a pledge of across-the-board bumps in salary and a one-time bonus of about $2,800 to cops clashing with rioters. “Rama doesn’t have the money to educate students because politicians steal public funds and he sells our land to his friends and foreigners without debate,” said Agim, a 22-year-old barista and student who comes out to protest when his schedule permits. “But somehow he’s got millions of euros to bribe the police to protect him.”

In the 1990s, after Albania broke free from a brutal communist regime that ruled for 45 years, crushing dissent and immiserating the populace, the country fell into chaotic violence. Local gangs, former security commanders, and an influential cohort of organized crime bosses effectively lorded over the streets until peacekeepers from nearby Italy helped restore government control in 1999.

From that low point, Albania was able to transition into a fairly calm, peaceful, and democratic state, even as its economy floundered. As the government managed to deliver basic services, and secure basic freedom of movement and speech, street crime also abated, and a small but growing tourist economy took hold, seeking to exploit the country’s pristine beaches and stunning mountain ranges.

After Rama came to power in 2013, he was able to leverage Albania’s more stable footing into an investment-friendly economic agenda. He also embarked on the long path to Albania’s admission to the EU in 2030—a plan that most citizens of this former pariah state eagerly support.

Yet Rama’s reputation as a competent technocratic reformer was short-lived. Despite Albania’s progress toward a more open society, the country has never managed to break the feudal control of its economy by powerful families and clans. Corruption moved behind closed doors as officials and oligarchs divided public funds among them, and employed bribes to secure overpriced government contracts. The economy, particularly for young people, completely stagnated, leading to an exodus over the last decade of at least a half a million Albanians out of a total population of just under 3 million.

Albanian crime lords, meanwhile, acquired a major interest in Europe’s lucrative cocaine trade. Albanians played an outsize role in transporting cocaine from South America to ports in Europe, which they then leveraged into control of much of the wholesale trade across Europe. Albanian gangsters ran much of the traffic in cocaine and heroin in the United Kingdom after waging a bloody gang war across London to drive out rival English and Kurdish gangs.

The proceeds and criminal fallout of this drug coup haven’t directly affected life in the Albanian homeland—most Albanians can’t afford cocaine, and the local marijuana trade is peaceful enough. But the country is plagued with rampant money laundering, as Albanian drug lords seek to transform billions in cocaine-related revenue into bank accounts and legal assets.

This means that there’s been a paper boom in the construction of restaurants, business, and apartments and resorts across the country. Tirana’s under a massive construction boom, with cocaine money springing for an estimated 12,000 new luxury apartment units that few Albanians can afford.

If Tirana were able to harness all the incoming drug money into something like a viable development plan, Rama might have been able to purchase a measure of social peace and stability. But this spending and development hasn’t led to better jobs or increased public funding of basic services. Instead, Albanians are facing the major signs of oligarch-bred inequality: soaring housing prices, expensive bars and restaurants that clearly lose money, and the surreal sight of twentysomething guys with Manchester and London accents driving Bentleys around Western Europe’s poorest capital—all in the shadow of luxury apartment towers that no reasonable person expects to be inhabited.

Albanian students aren’t dumb; the corruption and predation of Rama’s regime have greatly disillusioned the rising generation of Albanians. The luckiest among them have emigrated to other European countries, settling in the UK, Belgium, Netherlands, Germany, Greece, and Switzerland to work ordinary jobs that offer a future; the country’s émigré population is well into the hundreds of thousands. A 24-year-old truck driver named Luisi who’s turned out for the protests says he’s also thinking of leaving the country for Malmo, Sweden, where a cousin of his lives—and where Balkan organized crime has established a major hub. Luisi now supplements his modest $600-a-month salary by selling small amounts of marijuana and cocaine in clubs that he can’t afford to visit unless he’s working. His cousin assures him that “there’s work and I can make a lot of money.”

When I ask him what his cousin does, Luisi laughs. “He’s a gangster bro, he wants me to come help him. I don’t know if I should. But I’ll never get a good job or buy a house in Albania.”

Luisi then starts to interview me: “How much does a commercial truck driver get paid in America? I don’t want to be a gangster with my cousin, but I can’t stay here.”

#1378. Finland, the world's happiest nation, faces a glum winter

Finns have many reasons to feel content, but the country's pile of debt is not one of them. Russia's war in Ukraine has added to the fiscal burden. Can the Nordic nation absorb another shock?

There's an uneasy mood in Finland, the country that's ranked the world's happiest for nine consecutive years. A colder-than-usual winter is on the way and so is the toughest austerity budget in years.

With an election due in April, the argument is no longer how much to cut, but which services, benefits and pensions will face the biggest cuts.

Finland's debt and deficit levels are in their worst state since the 1990s, when a banking crash and the collapse of the Soviet Union — a key export partner — pushed the Nordic nation into its own Great Depression.

Last month, Finland's national statistical institution confirmed that the national debt had reached 90.3% of gross domestic product (GDP) in the second quarter. Before the pandemic, that figure was around 65%.

Trouble to the east is, again, part of the reason. Finland ramped up defense spending after joining NATO a year after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Helsinki, which used to rely on Moscow for about a third of its energy, also faced much higher costs as it transitioned to other suppliers.

The Finnish government has spent more than it earns since the 2008/9 global financial crisis, partly due to an aging population and sluggish economic growth. Last month, the State Treasury projected the fiscal deficit would reach 4.2% of GDP in 2026.

Brussels has piled pressure on Helsinki to bring down the borrowing gap, as European Union rules require member states to stay below 3% of GDP. In January, the European Council opened an excessive deficit procedure and gave Finland until the end of 2028 to reach the desired target.

Prime Minister Petteri Orpo's government, which took office in June 2023, said it would aim to save about €9 billion ($10.1 billion) during this parliamentary term. But whoever wins the next election will have to cut much deeper, economists warn.

"The amounts currently estimated range from €8 to €11 billion," Jarkko Kivisto, an advisor to the Bank of Finland's monetary policy and research department, told DW.

Earlier this year, all but one political party agreed to support a debt brake, where the next government commits to tightening the country's deficit to about 2% of GDP by 2031.

Remote ocean depths and habitats miles below the surface may seem buffered from the impacts of human activity, the authors wrote, but their findings suggest an industrial disaster pushed part of it into a diminished state with “limited resilience to facilitate recovery.” They said their findings also show that proposed industrial deep-sea mining could damage the ecosystem before baseline conditions are assessed.

A transoceanic drilling rig, operating for BP about 50 miles southeast of the Mississippi Delta, exploded on April 20, 2010. The explosion and fire killed 11 people and triggered the largest accidental marine oil spill in history.

The spill also released about 7.7 billion cubic feet of fossil gas into the deep sea at a rate of 88 million cubic feet per day, enough to supply about 820,000 US homes. State and federal agencies, including the Environmental Protection Agency, also authorized BP to apply 1.84 million gallons of chemical dispersants to try to reduce the impacts.

Some of the oil and gas dissipated and was transported out of the deep Gulf of Mexico region. Some coated barrier islands and devastated coastal wetlands and shellfish habitat, and some settled onto the ocean floor, where currents can stir it back into the water. But enough hydrocarbon and chemical-dispersant remnants stayed suspended long enough to do almost immeasurable harm.

A series of studies since the spill have shown a wide range of toxic effects on marine organisms, from impaired development and reproduction to increased mortality and lasting health problems in dolphins and whales. The new paper suggests that lingering pollutants may interfere with some species’ ability to find mates in the dark by disrupting their highly developed sense of smell.

The impacts continue to ripple through the ecosystem, said lead author Tracey Sutton, a professor of marine and environmental sciences at Nova Southeastern University in Florida. A 2024 study found an 80 percent decline in deep-diving toothed whales, he said.

“We used to have a big resident population of sperm whales right off the Mississippi River, but we haven’t seen a sperm whale in 10 years,” he said. Large predators need big, occasional dense patches of prey, and those have disappeared, he added.

Scientists have developed accurate ways to count fish. For this study, they started with a baseline from nine months of sampling in 2011 and compared it with nine subsequent surveys between 2015 and 2023. The researchers sampled deep-pelagic habitat along a large grid around the spill site from about 650 feet below the surface to about a mile down. The deep-pelagic zone accounts for about 90 percent of the Gulf’s total volume.

Across a grid of stations, scientists on slippery decks lowered and hoisted nets that open and close at different depths, with experts from different species groups to identify the catch. Ship-mounted scientific echosounders provided an independent check, measuring sound reflected by marine life in the water column.

“Through logical elimination,” he said, “and without actually pointing a finger, they leave no doubt that the oil spill is the cause of the decline.”

There’s no agent in a sidebar hounding me to let it handle tasks. I’m not worried about Zen training an LLM on my browsing activity. It’s just a web browser.

Zen also just kind of lets me focus on the task at hand. Links open in “glance” view, which is a temporary pop-over tab, which is really great when you’re doing some window shopping or light research. Zen isn’t perfect. I love its clean UI and organizational features, but it can’t play DRM-protected video content right now. That means Netflix, Spotify, HBO, Disney+, and more do not work in Zen. Android Auto and CarPlay have transformed the way people drive.

Both the Google and Apple solutions for this are really simple to use too. Most of the time they'll connect up automatically once you've done the initial setup. What you might not realize, though, are the demands that Android Auto and CarPlay put on your phone. Your handset is essentially doing all the regular phone stuff and then powering a large tablet-like display at the same time. That typically includes constantly updating a map in real time.

With that in mind, your Android phone or iPhone can quickly start to lose battery life and heat up when it's connected to your vehicle, which you've perhaps already noticed. To keep your handset cool and your battery in good shape, here's what you need to do.

Wireless Android Auto and CarPlay are convenient, just like wireless charging is convenient, but it puts more stress on your phone. If you've got the option of a wired connection, that's preferable—but that introduces a new potential heat problem, which is that your phone will always be charging up.

To counter that, you can set charge limits (Battery and charging from Android Settings, Battery > Charging from iOS Settings), or simply make sure your phone is kept in a cool, dark spot of the car.

This wired option has the added bonus of letting you switch to airplane mode on your phone, which will go a long way to limiting heat. Again there's a trade-off though: No one will be able to reach you, and you'll need to have your maps, music, podcasts, and whatever else you need downloaded to your device in advance.

If you do stick with wireless Android Auto or CarPlay, try to avoid charging your phone at the same time, especially on the wireless pad built into the car’s dash (which generates more heat). You don't want to end up with a dead phone half way through the journey, so you may have to compromise at certain times.

As mentioned above, where your phone is in the car makes a real difference to how hot it's going to be. If you have your phone's interface up on your car dashboard, then you don't necessarily need to have it fixed to the dashboard, too, possibly under direct sunlight. The glove compartment will do just fine.

If you have a mount for your phone, think about attaching it in front of one of your car's air vents. Blasts of air conditioned air will significantly reduce how hot your handset gets, working like a DIY cooling system.

You might also want to think about taking the case off of your phone for the length of the trip, if your phone is fitted with one. Your handset is less likely to be dropped or knocked while you're driving anyway, and a case can trap the heat in.

The rules aren't too different to keeping your phone cool generally, though with the added wrinkle of needing to keep it connected to your car somehow. Make sure the cabin in general is at a comfortable temperature, though you're probably already doing that for your own sake. The bigger thing to remember is to avoid leaving your phone behind in a vehicle that's going to heat up in the sun if you're stopping off somewhere.

If your phone is hooked up to Android Auto or CarPlay, then it's probably got some work to do—whether it's getting you to a destination or streaming music. However, you can still reduce heat by keeping phone tasks down to a minimum. If your calls and (voice-activated) messages can wait, leave them until the end of the journey.

We mentioned offline syncing above, but it doesn't have to go along with airplane mode. If you sync your tunes, podcasts, and maps to your phone ahead of time, your handset has less work to do while you’re driving since it doesn’t need to pull data from the cloud while also constantly searching for a wireless signal as you travel. Less stress on the modem means less heat overall.

There may be times when your phone has to be charging, wirelessly connected, making calls, in direct sunlight, and streaming music over a cellular connection, but try to keep these times to a minimum. "Almost all her literary production has centered upon the intersection between the philologist's obsession with words and the poet's desire for new meaning," noted Anders Olsson, chair of the Nobel Committee, in his description of the Nobel laureate on October 8.

Born in the Canadian city of Toronto, Carson first developed her fascination for ancient Greek myths as a teenager. She told literary journal The Brick that she spotted a bilingual edition of "Sappho" by Willis Barnstone in the display of a bookstore of a shopping mall in Ontario, and that had led her to ask a high school Latin teacher to teach her ancient Greek.

She went on to publish her doctoral thesis in Classics, entitled "Odi et Amo Ergo Sum" (I hate and I love, therefore I am).

Carson's first book of criticism, "Eros the Bittersweet," was published in 1986. It lays the foundation of her approach, as she draws on ancient Greek writers such as Sappho and Plato to examine how desire — and the obstacles between the lover and the beloved — creates emotional tension.

The book suggests that the ache of wanting is not separate from love; it is one of the things that makes desire so powerful.

Because "Eros the Bittersweet" moves between Greek poetry, philosophy and literary criticism and personal interpretations, it is not considered a conventionally easy read. A more immediately engaging work and good starting point to discover Carson is the verse novel "Autobiography of Red," published in 1998.

"Autobiography of Red" was inspired by the ancient Greek myth of Herakles (or Hercules) and the red-winged monster Geryon, which the hero kills as his 10th task in his series of 12 monumental labors.

An otherwise minor figure from mythology, Geryon is turned into Herakles' lover in Carson's book. She reimagines the myth as a modern, queer coming-of-age story, with the monster experiencing love, jealousy, sexuality and heartbreak.

"We all feel we're monsters most of the time," explained Carson.

The work is accessible, funny and moving, and no deep knowledge of Greek mythology is required to enjoy it.

The key to reading Carson is not to expect every connection to be immediately clear. Her books reward curiosity rather than certainty, and her sprawling, non-linear web of knowledge can be compared to going down a hyperlinked rabbit hole of narrative ideas.

"Men in the Off Hours" (2000) is another intellectually playful collection of poems. Here Carson collides the modern with the ancient, with references to figures as diverse as Beethoven, Virginia Woolf, Emily Dickinson, Jean-Luc Godard, Sigmund Freud, Homer and Hokusai.

The poems bring together fragments of history, mythology and ordinary life, allowing unexpected emotional and intellectual connections to emerge.

In the spoken and sung performance piece "Norma Jeane Baker of Troy" (2019), Carson similarly brings together classical and pop culture, reimagining the story of Helen of Troy alongside the life and cultural image of Marilyn Monroe.

How do people become the victims of desires they cannot control? This is a core theme Carson explores in "The Beauty of the Husband: A Fictional Essay in 29 Tangos," published in 2001.

It features the Canadian author's wry humor in a powerful and moving way, with erotic, painful and heartbreaking scenes from a doomed marriage: a 30-year obsession with a pathological liar whose singular redeeming quality is his sheer physical radiance.

The collection of poetry, which won her the T.S. Eliot Prize, is dedicated to the English Romantic poet John Keats, challenging his famous aphorism, "Beauty is truth."

#1392. Mamdani Is Facing Heat Over the NYPD. Here’s What He Should Do.

The mayor is being given a series of false choices over his public-safety agenda. But there is another way forward.

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani speaks during an unveiling ceremony for a new 9/11 Memorial Wall at One Police Plaza on September 9, 2026, in New York City.

In recent weeks, New Yorkers have been presented with a series of false choices about how Mayor Zohran Mamdani should address public safety and the power of the NYPD.

Some have said that we can either support the police wholeheartedly or see crime and disorder rise. Others have warned that the city must implement conventional procedural police reforms or the NYPD will abuse its power. Others have suggested that the mayor must fire Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch or surrender all hope of creating a more just city.

There is perhaps no more powerful or politically sensitive entity for a New York City mayor to handle than the NYPD. Moving in any direction on policing invites anger from some portion of the mayor’s constituents. But there is a way for Mamdani to fulfill the public-safety policy pledges he campaigned on without derailing his administration. (Full disclosure: I served on the mayor’s transition team after his election, though I have no ongoing role in his government.)

Ten months into his tenure, Mamdani has avoided making an enemy of the NYPD—an accomplishment for any mayor, let alone a democratic socialist. But that peace has come at a cost: letting a variety of problems at the agency fester, or even worsen.

Since Mamdani took office, the NYPD has significantly increased low-level arrests, leading to higher headcounts at Rikers Island and backlogs in the court system. NYPD officers have repeatedly performed crowd-control duties that have facilitated ICE raids throughout the city. The Strategic Response Group unit continues to take an aggressive and at times violent approach to protests. The city’s independent Stop and Frisk Monitor has accused the NYPD of continuing to engage in unconstitutional stop-and-frisk activity and systematically underreporting the number of stops. NYPD officers accused of misconduct continue to face little or no consequences for their actions. And the NYPD continues to put the names of people accused of no crime into the racially discriminatory “Gang Database.”

Mamdani’s failure to act on these issues contradicts several of his campaign pledges. He said he would dismantle the Strategic Response Group, end the Gang Database, close down Rikers Island—which requires reducing low-level arrests—and invest heavily in a new Department of Community Safety to enhance the city’s capacity to prevent crime before it happens. The good news is that the mayor can still follow through on every one of these pledges—while avoiding some of the false choices currently on offer.

One thing Mamdani definitely does not need to do is reach for ultimately hollow versions of “police reform” that we already know won’t work. During the campaign, he rightly avoided talk of changes to training, symbolic policy adjustments, or other forms of proceduralism that have consistently failed to produce results over the last 10 years. These kinds of reforms produce the worst of all worlds: They don’t meaningfully change anything, but they still antagonize police unions and other entrenched forces.

Mamdani has mostly avoided these traps in office. (He did pledge more support for the Civilian Complaints Review Board, and has followed through on that to some degree, but this is a bit of an empty gesture since the police commissioner routinely obstructs the board’s investigations and overrules its findings.) Unfortunately, in response to the anger about the NYPD’s conduct in recent weeks, the mayor has suggested a pointless review of “use of force” policies that is destined to produce nothing of substance.

Police accountability organizers have also begun calling for the firing of Commissioner Jessica Tisch following the ICE shooting in Marble Hill and the subsequent violent NYPD response to protests there. While I counseled against retaining Tisch as early as the summer of 2025, firing her, without significant changes to public-safety policy, would be a hollow victory. If the mayor is serious about a new path for creating safety, he should focus on the substance of his agenda, not just the symbolism of who is in charge. The police commissioner works for him and should be instructed to make some major changes or, yes, step aside—the stance taken by New York City DSA. But the emphasis should be on the changes, which should be implemented in a responsible, transparent, and comprehensive manner.

As Mamdani made clear on the campaign trail, police are not the only tool for producing safety. The safest communities have low crime not because of intensive policing but because people’s everyday needs are being met. Delivering on the mayor’s affordability agenda and improving government services are important long-term strategies for enhancing public safety and community well-being.

But we also need immediate strategies to directly address crime and disorder conditions. That means following through on the mayor’s pledges to robustly fund the Department, now Office, of Community Safety, by expanding community-centered strategies to address violence, mental health crises, gender-based and domestic violence, hate crimes, and subway safety. The leadership of the new office is working hard to do this, but results have been slow to manifest, and the mayor has undercut their work with messaging that centers police as the primary tool for producing safety. Instead of leaning into this narrative, Mamdani can both dial back his material and rhetorical support for police and improve public safety through these essential evidence-based interventions.

Here are some of the changes that the mayor could make to enact his campaign pledges, respond to real community concerns about NYPD abuse, and maintain and possibly improve public safety.

The NYPD must be instructed to dial back low-level “broken windows” arrests. These are placing a tremendous burden on already poor and vulnerable communities. They are used to target almost exclusively poor people of color, many of whom are experiencing homelessness, untreated mental health and substance use disorders, and lack basic incomes.

The mayor must immediately enhance services for these populations so that everyday conditions improve for them and the communities they live in. That means adopting a robust Housing First approach that puts people in permanent housing with support services rather than endlessly cycling them through shelters, jails, hospitals, and transitional programs that fail to take into account their need for long-term stability and cost more than permanent housing. New York City currently has units designated for people in crisis, but there are often too many restrictions on accessing them and a lack of appropriate services to help people maintain stable housing.

The city also needs to expand community-based mental health and substance use facilities, including Clubhouses, Sobering Centers, Mental Health Crisis Respite Centers, and medically based drug treatment. And the mayor should look at a minimum basic income policy to create a floor for people who have been left out of the job market.

The mayor should create a plan to phase out the use of the gang database and gang suppression policing more broadly. Most of the people on the database are there simply because the police suspect they may be connected to illegal activity and violence, not because they have committed any concrete offense. There is no independent evidence that these practices reduce crime—but plenty of evidence that the harassment and criminalization of young people can actually make violence more likely.

The mayor should follow through on his prevention pledge and enlist intensive neighborhood violence reduction programs to work with these young people in real time—both to diffuse disputes and connect them with stable housing, help with mental health and substance use, and income supports. This means expanding and improving the current Crisis Management System by opening new sites and increasing their access to essential crisis stabilization services. This system has already been shown to drive down gun violence. Manhattan District Attorney Alvin Bragg is already doing something along these lines, referring young people on the margins of violence to well-established programs to help steer them in a positive direction rather than surveilling and harassing them until they can be arrested and incarcerated.

The mayor does need to fulfill his promise to eliminate the current Strategic Response Group, but just as importantly, he needs to address the policies that guide the unit’s activities. Ever since it was created under Mayor de Blasio, the SRG has seen its mission primarily as one of suppressing any dissent that it perceives to be either too disruptive to daily life or too politically radical. Former NYPD chief of department John Chell recently posted that the SRG is hated because it has successfully “dominated” protesters in the service of “protecting and serving the true community members.”

The NYPD must be forced to accept that disruptive protest is a necessary and even desirable aspect of democracy and that people who engage in First Amendment activity are also legitimate community members. This means that the goal of protest policing must not be to suppress disorder at all costs.

Instead, the mayor should pursue a policy of “negotiated management” that emphasizes good communication with demonstrators before and during protests and tolerance of some disorder to help maintain an overall peaceful protest. This can be achieved in part through civilianizing the city’s approach as much as possible by creating a civilian office of community engagement that works with groups before demonstrations, issues permits, and has a visible presence during demonstrations to help keep protests on track. This can be used for the vast majority of protests that the SRG currently manages through intimidation and suppression.

That doesn’t mean that the NYPD will have no role to play in the immediate future. There will still need to be some kind of capacity to manage the possibility of violent protest and to make, when necessary, mass arrests in a responsible manner, but the SRG and its anti-terrorism focus are the wrong unit with the wrong approach.

Not all of this can be done overnight. It requires new investments, program development, and community consultation. But the mayor should begin publicly committing to move forward with these changes and implementing them as they are ready, rather than continuing to lean into a police-centered narrative about public safety and superficial calls for procedural reform that amount to little more than box-checking.

Product-market fit — when a startup’s product sells well — has become even harder to find in this AI era. Despite startups achieving record-fast growth, not all customers are sticky and products can be replicated more easily than ever.

Anu Bharadwaj, a partner at Iconiq; Rajeev Dham, a partner at Sapphire Ventures; and Rahul Vohra, the founder of Superhuman Mail, discussed the new rules for product-market fit last week at TechCrunch Disrupt.

“It’s never been easier to gain a bunch of pilots because lots of companies across the board are open to trying new things,” Bharadwaj said to the audience. “But going from pilot to production is really the hard thing.”

Right now, she said, a lot of founders pitch revenue metrics to investors without specifying which revenue they are talking about. “Is it true ARR or is it run rate?” she said. “There’s many ways to obfuscate the whole thing.”

Lately, much chatter on X has focused on AI founders inflating ARR numbers (which could mean “annualized run rate” or the more solid “annual recurring revenue”) to make it seem like their companies are doing better than they are. A common gimmick is counting pilot customers, who might only use the product for a couple of months, in the annualized run rate revenue number, implying that income is expected to continue for the entire year.

It should be obvious, but Bharadwaj pointed out, “It’s definitely in your long-term interest that you be very honest and upfront about what quality of revenue this is.”

She said companies need to separate hype from reality when looking at the quality of their revenue. It’s a red flag, for example, if only a few customers make up much of a company’s revenue or if the customers can easily go to another vendor for a better price. “Churn rate and retention [are] really dependent on that,” she continued.

That’s why Vohra advises startups to track customer engagement and dollar retention, especially for consumer products. “Figure out what benchmark [is] good, great, and excellent for your space,” he said.

For example, at Superhuman, he said that the company tracked ARR but also had their own internal metric called “delighted ARR,” which, he said, “was the ARR only from customers who are super engaged with the product.” He said that metric helped them see how sticky the customers were and how long they were likely to stay around.

“Those users are now still users, those companies are now still customers,” he continued. “We obsessed over serving that particular category of our user base and our customer base.”

The panelists also discussed green flags that indicate a startup has indeed found product-market fit. For Vohra, he said talk to customers to understand what value they find in the product and how they would feel if they could no longer use it. Then be honest with yourself when measuring those responses.

“If you’re 15%, 14%, 13%, or below, consider a complete pivot. Take the codebase, take the team, build something entirely new. “But if you’re 20%, you can totally optimize your way from 20% to 40%,” Vohra said.

Actually, Dham said, it’s important for a founder not to confuse having a vision with having a good business.

At the seed level especially, Bharadwaj said that it’s fine to pivot. She doesn’t even look for product-market fit at that stage, but “does the founder have a thesis that seems plausible and do they have the agility to duck and wave as the situation changes?” she said.

Dham added that he values founders even more these days who have made numerous pivots because it shows they’ve had to make very hard decisions in this ever-shifting moment.

Product-market fit, therefore, is something founders will always have to redefine and re-earn. “Even at a billion in revenue, these companies are re-earning their product-market fit,” he said. “You constantly have to be innovating on products.”

#1397. Hyve Smart Package Locker Review: Protecting Your Holiday Deliveries

The onslaught of tech gear being delivered for WIRED Reviews, combined with the samples I receive constantly for my side hustle at Drinkhacker, means that delivery trucks are a constant occurrence on my suburban street. UPS and FedEx both make drops at my house nearly every day, and that’s not to mention occasional deliveries from the likes of DHL and, of course, Amazon and its ilk. But it’s usually not a problem. I work from home, so picking up packages left on the doorstep is just part of the scenery.

What is a problem is that I can’t be home 24 hours a day, and even though I have a very kind neighbor across the way who keeps an eye out for boxes on my front steps when I’m traveling, he isn’t always at home either. Package hold requests aren’t consistently honored by either of the major carriers—and earlier this year, FedEx seemingly discontinued its vacation hold option without bothering to tell anyone, the modern epidemic of porch piracy be damned. (A rep has since confirmed this with me, though no formal announcement has ever been made.)

Having packages held at shipping centers or access points usually costs money, typically about $5 per package and often more depending on the length of the hold, and that involves a trip after I get home to collect what’s arrived. If I’m gone for a week, that can mean fetching a dozen boxes or more.

All of that is a time-consuming pain, for which a startup called Hyve has come along to offer an assuagement: A smart lockbox that lets delivery drivers drop off packages in a secure location, notifying the homeowner whenever it’s been accessed. While the growing pains were significant, after weeks of testing, I found it a useful device that—just maybe—may earn a permanent home on my front porch.

Parcel lockboxes aren’t exactly a new concept. They operate basically like a standard post office mailbox, with a cantilevered hatch up top that allows deliveries to drop into a secure storage area below, where they can only be accessed through a keyed door. Delivery folks can put packages in through the hatch, but only you can get them out.

The Hyve Smart Package Locker is a play on this idea, forgoing the two-chamber design in favor of an electronic hatch accessed via 4-digit PINs. The box itself measures 32 inches high, 20 inches wide, and 20 inches deep, with interior dimensions about 4 inches shorter in each direction. That makes for a total of 7.4 cubic feet, which Hyve says will fit 80 percent of Amazon package sizes (though obviously this depends on the specifics of your ordering habits). For me, that math worked out: Deliveries of three-pack bottles of wine fit fine, but anything larger was typically left out by the courier.

It’s built from a combination of plastic and steel, and while the side panels seem a little flimsy, there’s enough reinforcement behind them that I never worried that the unit would be easily damaged by a would-be package thief without power tools. Units are available in gray or white; I requested the latter to match my home’s color, but alas received a gray one.

Assembly is required—and be warned, it’s no cakewalk. The locker comes packed flat, notably with no printed instructions on how to put all the pieces together. A QR code in the box takes you to a web page featuring a walkthrough video and a PDF guide, which are my two least favorite ways to be guided through an installation process on a cell phone. The PDF is very difficult to read on a small screen, and this contributed to making what was already a cumbersome construction process even more difficult. I consider myself quite handy, but I ultimately spent nearly an hour putting the locker together due to a couple of easy-to-overlook goofs. Real, printed instructions—especially considering they’re all of five pages long—would go a long way toward streamlining the process. Or, better yet, why not just ship the thing preassembled?

A security bracket and cable tether are included so you can attach the Hyve to an immovable object on your property to prevent pirates from simply running off with the entire thing. (Unloaded, the device weighs 40 pounds. While awkward, it would be fairly easy to abscond with if it isn’t physically locked down.) Though oddly, the installation of these components is covered only in the video, not in the written instructions.

The unit is powered by four AA batteries and features a solar panel on top for trickle charging a backup battery. There’s also a USB-C port that can be used to supply emergency power if all else fails. Hyve says a fresh set of cells should last about five or six months, though strangely, my first quartet of AAs died inside of three weeks and without warning. There’s fortunately a USB-C port that can be used to supply emergency power if all else fails (which I needed when my cells died).

The Hyve Locker requires its own Wi-Fi bridge, which you must plug into an inside outlet near where you’ll be placing the locker. I found this to be a bit of a tall order, as I don’t have any power outlets in my entry hall, and an outlet only 15 feet away from the locker turned out to be out of range. (Exterior walls will do that to a wireless signal.) I ended up finding another outlet, forgotten in the guest room, that was able to reach the Hyve, but if that hadn’t worked, I would have been forced to relocate the locker elsewhere. Fortunately, the signal has held strong over several weeks of testing.

Hyve’s app is deceptively simple. At its most basic, the homeowner sets a master PIN, which everyone uses for access to the locker to both make deliveries and to retrieve them. It’s a simple idea, but it obviously presents a bit of a security risk, since anyone with the four-digit code can pop the lid open and rifle through what’s inside. Whether this is a legitimate concern or not is perhaps a matter of personal risk tolerance. Due to the physical design of the device, there’s nothing preventing one courier from removing a package that another has left inside the Hyve, but then again, there’s nothing preventing them from taking an unattended package off your doorstep, either. Presumably for Hyve, and I would agree, random passers-by are the bigger concern for most homeowners, so the monolithic PIN mode is probably good enough for many users.

If you want more fine-grained security than the mono-PIN, you’ll need a subscription plan. Hyve’s Essentials subscription plan runs $5 per month or $50 per year, the main feature being that you can set separate PINs for each delivery service or carrier (including major shippers as well as services including DoorDash, Instacart, Best Buy, and more). With this setup, the app notifies you each time the lid is opened, and it tells you who opened it (or at least who employs them), logging it in the system. One month of Essentials is included with purchase.

What do you do with all these PINs? Alas, you’ll have to go it alone, though Hyve provides detailed advice on how to enter the PIN and information about the lockbox into each carrier’s delivery instructions page on their website. You’ll need an account with each carrier, tied to your address, and I found that some of these instructions are more useful than others. Both FedEx and UPS, for example, provide fields for entering gate codes, but neither lets you specify specifically that packages should be left in a lockbox. (Both used to offer free-form delivery instruction fields, but these have recently changed into more simplified drop-down systems, and “lockbox” is not a choice for a delivery location.)

If you don’t have the Hyve Returns subscription but do have Essentials, you can still use the Hyve for on-demand returns by scheduling a pickup through Uber Courier, with no additional charges due to Hyve. The trick with this scheme is that while you avoid the $15 subscription fee, you have to pay Uber’s service charge for the pickup. Those fees vary based on the distance to your nearest UPS, FedEx, or Amazon drop-off location. For me, the Uber Courier charge for dropping off an Amazon return typically ranged from $8–18, making the $15 twice-monthly return plan far more enticing than the ad hoc option. (But alas, unavailable to me.)

I did test the alternate method by using Uber Courier to create a return, and found that Hyve didn’t add much to the equation. The only difference with Hyve is that instead of grabbing the item from the doorstep, the driver fetched it from the bin with a PIN. More secure? Yes, but given that I only had to wait 20 minutes for the driver to show up, the risk of an exposed package was minimal compared to receiving a delivery when I wasn’t home for days.

While the lockbox is rather obtrusive on my front porch, the hardware works well. The weatherproofing is fine, the door opens and closes with a satisfying, locking click, and the box sounds an alarm if it’s left open for too long. Push notifications from the Hyve mobile app come quickly and accurately, letting you know whether it was FedEx or Amazon—or you—that opened the hatch.

There is, unfortunately, a catch, and at first I found it to be an awfully big one. The problem was that no delivery driver seemed to want to use the Hyve locker, no matter how much I tried to get them to.

After getting everything set up on day one, I waited with anticipation when FedEx arrived with a box, and my phone lit up with a text from the company that the delivery had been completed. Alas, it was not in the Hyve locker but sitting next to it, nestled against the exterior. I was crestfallen, yet undeterred, and kept trying to get someone to open that hatch.

The next day, I encountered the FedEx driver in person, only because he rang the bell because he had a delivery with a signature required. I asked if he had seen the delivery instructions on my FedEx account and, with a puzzled look, said that he “thought he heard something” when pulling up to my house. It turns out that FedEx’s driver devices provide verbal instructions on where to leave packages (in addition to rather garbled written ones, which he showed me), but that he had the volume on his device turned down. Clad in my bathrobe, I demonstrated the Hyve to him personally and got his buy-in.

The following day, I was thrilled to get a push notification from the Hyve app that FedEx had successfully left a box for me inside. The driver even rang the bell again, only to show me his success with a smile. I tell you, opening that hatch and seeing my package inside was like Christmas morning for both of us.

Alas, on the third day, a fresh FedEx delivery appeared on the porch again. My driver had moved to another route.

I was about to give up on the whole shebang when, all of a sudden, something clicked. FedEx, UPS, and Amazon all started using the Hyve locker, and regularly. I soon became accustomed to getting notifications multiple times a day that the box had received a delivery, with my estimation of its usage rate approaching 75 percent. No longer was my doorstep darkened with cardboard, as trips to empty out the Hyve became an undertaking necessary to complete several times a day.

In fact, on some days, the usage level revealed an entirely new problem: A single UPS delivery of a few boxes could fill the locker, leaving no room for anything else. My hopes of leaving for a week and not having to worry about packages were dashed; there’s just not enough space for more than a day or two at most in the bin, given the pace of deliveries at my house.

Early on in my testing, I spoke to Hyve about the usage issues I was experiencing at the time, and its CEO freely admitted that this is, in no uncertain terms, a problem for many homeowners. To combat it, the company provides an adhesive sign that you can use to instruct carriers to use the box, and while this was likely quite helpful at improving usage rates, it’s not a panacea (nor is it particularly attractive).

And over a Zoom call, the CEO showed me another innovation that she said was having even better results: Attaching a placard that reads “Snack Stop” to the top of the lockbox, and stocking a collection of treats like chips and meat sticks inside for hungry drivers, encouraging them to open it up to grab a bite when making their rounds. What delivery driver could possibly say no to a free Slim Jim?

#1399. Early-onset cancer rising worldwide: Causes, risk, prevention

Across Latin America, early-onset cancer rates have risen, especially of bowel, breast and cervical cancers. But it is a worldwide trend.

The numbers are striking. Some cancer rates have doubled in the past 20 years. It's just not yet clear whether we're seeing a rise in actual cases or whether we're seeing more early-stage cases because of better awareness, diagnosis and treatment in hospitals.

Take, for example, colorectal (or bowel) cancer: In 2001, about one in every 26,000 people under 50 in England were diagnosed with it each year. By 2023, the rate of diagnoses was about one in 12,000. Twice what it was at the turn of the century.

That's according to the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF), an international network of charities, which analyzed registry data from England, the United States and the Netherlands. It found the number of new cancer cases had risen for seven cancers in England alone.

So, rates have doubled, but the numbers overall are still small, said Amy Berrington de Gonzalez, a professor of clinical epidemiology at the Institute of Cancer Research in London.

"Cancer is still very uncommon among young people," Berrington de Gonzalez told DW. "Three times a small number is still a small number."

But she and other scientists still want to know why this is happening, because it is a worldwide trend. Latin America is a case in point.

A 2025 study analyzed registry data from 50 countries and territories up to 2017. It found early-onset colorectal cancer rising in 27 of those regions.

In Latin America, rates rose in Chile, Argentina, Ecuador, Costa Rica and Puerto Rico. Chile's rise — at almost 4% per year — was among the highest.

In Brazil, the evidence comes directly from hospitals. At the A.C. Camargo Cancer Center in Sao Paulo, the number of colorectal cancer patients under 50 grew by 7.6% per year between 2000 and 2023. That's a five-fold increase.

The number of patients over 50 grew even faster — at 8.1% — or about six-fold.

But since these figures came from a single hospital, they do not show whether cases are rising across Brazil. They may only reflect that this one hospital is treating more patients.

"We need better cancer registration systems," said Berrington de Gonzalez, "especially in countries like Brazil and Mexico, where obesity rates have also been increasing."

Marc Gunter, professor of cancer epidemiology at Imperial College London, said he expected Latin America to follow richer countries as diets there become more Westernized and lifestyles more sedentary. "I think it's already happening," he said.

In another study in 2025, involving 42 countries, Berrington de Gonzalez compared data from two age groups — 20 to 49 years and 50 years and older — between 2003 and 2017.

Six cancers — thyroid, breast, colorectal, kidney, uterine and leukemia — had risen among the younger groups in most countries. All but colorectal cancer were seen to rise in older adults, too.

In fact, colorectal cancer stood out. In about a third of the countries, it rose faster in younger adults than in older ones. "It is the one cancer that seems to be increasing in younger people whilst decreasing in older people," said Berrington de Gonzalez.

This has consequences for research. If cancers are rising in all age groups, the causes are likely to be widespread, so studies looking only at young people risk chasing complicated explanations with too little data to test them, she said.

A US study of pancreatic cancer in adults aged 15 to 39 years found the rise was almost entirely made up of early-stage tumors.

The authors said this suggested that instead of the number of cancer cases going up, it was the number of early diagnoses that had gone up. And that can be explained by better awareness in the community, with people getting checked more often.

But the data for colorectal cancer looks different: Some US studies show it rising in advanced stages of the disease as well, which suggests an increase in the number of cases, not just the number of diagnoses.

Obesity is an established cause of at least 13 cancers, according to the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).

According to a 2021 review of existing studies, young adults with obesity were almost twice as likely to develop colorectal cancer before the age of 50.

Obesity has also spread fastest among the young. The WHO reports that obesity rates have doubled among adults since the 1990s and quadrupled among adolescents.

Some also say ultra-processed foods and microplastics cause cancer. But Gunter said the evidence was inconclusive.

"There's a lot of hype," Gunter said, adding that more research was needed. Berrington de Gonzalez agreed: "Diets have changed, but we don't yet know the role they might play."

Gunter said individuals should not be blamed for living in societies built around convenience foods and desk jobs, and that lasting change needed public health policy.

But he also said that young people could lower their cancer risk by maintaining a healthy weight, moving more, eating more fiber and cutting back on processed meat.

"Going for a walk, being more physically active, eating more fruits and vegetables," he said. "Those things are maybe not so trendy, but they are really important."

For a 30-year-old reading this, Berrington de Gonzalez's message was one of reassurance: Cancer at a young age remains relatively rare.
